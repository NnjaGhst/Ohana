### Previously on Elsewhere

A woman called Ninja arrived in the mountain village of Bellwether, searching for the one thing her old home could no longer offer: anonymity. Five years earlier, her brother Cassian had died in what authorities described as a robbery gone wrong. In the Crooked Lantern, she ordered a whiskey. Then a stranger in the corner sent over another glass.

This is the story of the man who sent it.

### The Boy Who Noticed

Before Hex learned how to make himself unreadable, he was the child who noticed everything.

He noticed the moment a teacher's cheerful smile became something forced. He noticed when a classmate came back to school hungry and pretended he wasn't. He noticed the hesitation in his mother's voice when she said that everything would be fine.

He didn't have a word for any of that. He only knew that when somebody hurt, the feeling settled under his skin as though it belonged there.

It was an inconvenient quality in the neighborhood where he grew up.

People who cared too openly could be used. People who asked questions became involved. People who stepped between a frightened person and an angry one sometimes ended up with bruises of their own.

Hex knew all three facts before he was old enough to shave.

He stepped in anyway.

There was a winter when an elderly man in their building went without heating for days because he couldn't afford a repair. Hex was fourteen. He borrowed tools from a neighbor, spent an entire Saturday trying to fix something well beyond his understanding, and succeeded only in making the radiator hiss like an offended animal.

The man didn't get warm that day.

But the next morning, Hex appeared again with a friend who knew somebody whose uncle could fix it. The job took twenty minutes. The old man pressed a handful of coins into his palm.

Hex refused them.

His friend did not.

"You're supposed to take money when people offer you money," Roan explained afterward, counting the coins as they walked home.

"He's got nothing."

"Now he's got heating. We have money. Beautiful system."

"We shouldn't have taken it."

"Correction: you shouldn't have taken it. I have excellent moral flexibility."

Hex gave him a look.

Roan grinned, tossed the coins into the air, and nearly lost them down a drain.

They spent all of it on groceries and left the bag outside the old man's door.

Roan claimed the choice was practical.

Hex knew better.

The difference between them was never that one cared and the other didn't.

Roan cared in explosions. Hex cared in quiet, sustained acts that made him useful long after the moment of crisis had passed.

One day that difference would save Roan's life.

It would also change both of them forever.

### Roan and the Trouble They Shared

Roan lived four doors down. He was the sort of boy who collected scrapes, debts, broken bicycles, and friends at approximately the same rate.

He had an enormous talent for turning a reasonable plan into an incident that required explanations.

When they were fifteen, Roan insisted he knew a shortcut over the roofs behind the shops. By the time they arrived at the next street, he had torn his jacket, lost a shoe, and accidentally upset a flock of pigeons that apparently considered the entire roof their ancestral territory.

Hex climbed down by the actual stairs.

Roan appeared at the bottom a minute later with one sock and a very offended expression.

"You abandoned me."

"I told you the stairs existed."

"You could have sounded more supportive."

"I was supportive. I shouted 'use the stairs' six times."

Roan stared at the missing shoe.

"Do you think the pigeons stole it?"

"I think it made a decision to leave."

They laughed so hard they had to sit down.

On the hard days, they didn't always laugh.

Once, when they were seventeen, Roan arrived at Hex's door after midnight. His lip was split and his knuckles were swollen, and the normally endless stream of talking had stopped.

Hex let him inside.

He found ice in the freezer and a clean cloth in the kitchen.

"What happened?"

"Nothing."

"That's a remarkably violent-looking nothing."

Roan stared at the floor.

Hex waited.

Finally Roan said that somebody he cared about had been threatened, and he'd made the mistake of believing he could frighten the threat away.

"Did it work?"

"No."

"Did you make it worse?"

"Probably."

Hex leaned back in his chair. His own first instinct was always to fix things. But fixing required understanding, and Roan's face said he was ashamed enough already.

"Okay," Hex said. "Then we find a better way tomorrow."

"We?"

Hex looked at him.

"Do you know anybody else who's going to tolerate you?"

Roan managed half a smile.

"No."

"Thought so."

They grew up imagining that adulthood would solve the things childhood couldn't. They pictured jobs, homes with working locks, money left over after rent, weekends that belonged to them.

They didn't understand that adulthood sometimes just meant becoming legally responsible for the same problems.

In their early twenties, Hex was holding down regular work and helping people where he could. Roan had started doing favors for anyone whose situation looked worse than his own. Most ended with somebody grateful. One didn't.

The first warning was that Roan stopped turning up.

The second was that he started insisting everything was fine.

Hex had heard that sentence used as a warning long enough to recognize it.

### The Debt

It took three weeks to get the truth out of him.

They were in a café near the bus depot, sitting beside a window fogged from the inside. Roan kept moving his coffee cup without drinking from it.

"How much?" Hex asked.

Roan laughed once.

"Hello to you too."

"How much do you owe?"

"I've got it handled."

"Then say the number."

The silence lasted long enough for Hex to begin calculating what he could raise himself.

Roan finally told him.

Hex stared at the table.

"That's not a debt. That's a hostile takeover."

"Yeah, well. They were very persuasive on the subject of interest."

"What happened?"

Roan looked toward the street.

There had been someone who needed help. Someone Roan couldn't stand to watch suffer, and he had believed that borrowing money would get them through the worst of it. A few small payments later, the amount owed had multiplied. The men collecting didn't care who had originally needed the money or whether anybody could repay it.

They'd begun visiting people Roan loved.

"You should've told me."

"And what were you going to do? Pay it with your charming personality?"

"I've been told it's valuable."

"By who? People with head injuries?"

Hex almost smiled.

Then Roan pushed up his sleeve.

The bruises beneath it were fresh.

The humor left the table.

Hex understood something Roan hadn't said. The lenders didn't merely want the money back. They wanted a person who would do what he was told.

"Are they threatening anybody else?"

Roan didn't answer.

"Roan."

"Don't."

"Who?"

"I said don't. I didn't tell you because I didn't want them coming after you too."

Hex looked at his oldest friend, at the swollen skin around his wrist, at the man still trying to protect other people while his own world closed around him.

And he made a promise without knowing what it would cost.

"I'll find a way."

"You can't fix everything."

"No," Hex said. "But I can try this."

Hex knew it sounded wrong.

He also knew Roan's time was running out.

### The Offer

The woman who met him wore a dark coat and an expensive watch. They sat in a hotel lounge where the music was soft enough to be ignored and the staff were discreet enough not to remember them.

She did not introduce herself.

"I understand you have a friend in difficulty."

"You understand correctly."

"And you want his account cleared."

"That's the idea."

She studied him for a moment.

"You've been asking the right questions."

"That's usually a polite way of saying I've become inconvenient."

She smiled without warmth.

"We value people who think before they act."

Hex glanced at the exits without meaning to. He was nervous, but he knew how to sit still when it mattered.

"Then you've probably noticed I don't have the money."

"Money isn't the only thing people can offer."

She opened a thin folder.

Inside were a photograph, a name, a handful of dates, and details about a man who had agreed to move information for the wrong people and was now trying to disappear.

Hex read it twice.

"What do you want?"

"To ensure he no longer interferes with certain arrangements."

Hex closed the folder.

"That could mean a dozen different things."

"Here, it means one."

For a long moment, neither spoke.

"No," Hex said.

The woman nodded as if she'd expected that.

"Then I hope your friend finds another arrangement. Our offer expires tomorrow."

He left.

He got halfway down the street before his phone rang.

Roan's name flashed on the screen.

His voice was shaky. Somebody had been waiting outside his building. They'd told him the next visit would not be a conversation.

Hex stood beside a shop window, watching his own reflection in the glass.

He thought about the man in the folder. A stranger with a name. Somebody frightened enough to run from the people who had once paid him.

He thought about Roan.

And he did a thing he would spend years trying not to name.

He called the woman back.

### The First Line

The job itself was frighteningly ordinary until the final minutes.

Hex was given instructions, a contact number, and one small window of opportunity. He learned more than he had ever wanted to know about the man whose photograph had been in the folder.

The man was not a monster.

He had made compromises and broken promises. He had helped people with money do things that damaged people without it. But he had also tried, too late, to leave. There were signs of a life outside the work: a daughter who called every Sunday, a half-finished repair in his apartment, a photograph of a dog on the refrigerator.

Hex noticed those things.

That was part of the problem.

He was supposed to see the target as a problem to be solved. Instead he kept seeing a person who was afraid.

The final meeting happened in a shabby apartment that smelled of old tobacco and paint.

The man knew what was coming when Hex stepped through the door.

"Please," he said.

It was not dramatic. Not a speech. Just one word from a person who understood he was out of time.

Hex remembered that word afterward more clearly than the rest of the encounter.

He did what the network had asked him to do.

And then it was over.

The room was still.

He looked at the man's hand on the floor. There was a pale ring where a wedding band had once been. Hex couldn't stop looking at it.

He had expected horror to arrive like a thunderclap.

Instead there was a terrible, practical silence. A clock ticked. Something dripped in the next room. Somewhere outside a delivery truck reversed with a shrill warning sound, entirely indifferent to the fact that a person had just died.

The woman from the hotel called that evening.

"The account has been settled."

Hex said nothing.

"Your friend is no longer in danger from that debt."

He closed his eyes.

"You said it would be contained."

"It was."

He wanted to shout. To tell her that she'd made him kill someone who had been trying to escape. To demand the man's name be said aloud, properly, by somebody who regarded it as more than a line of work.

But he hadn't been tricked about the essential fact.

He knew what the job was.

He had chosen to do it.

"Because I couldn't bear to lose you."

For the first time in their friendship, neither knew what to say next.

### The Hook

Hex tried to return to ordinary work.

For six weeks, nobody contacted him. The world continued with an obscenity he would never quite get used to: buses ran, rent came due, strangers argued over parking spaces, and the man from the apartment remained dead.

Hex found himself listening for footsteps and checking reflections in shop windows.

Then the woman called again.

"A small assignment," she said.

"No."

"Your friend is doing well, I understand."

Hex gripped the phone.

"Leave him out of it."

"We'd prefer to."

He didn't answer.

That was the second job.

The third came a month later. Each was described as necessary, limited, a way of preventing some larger harm. Occasionally, there really was somebody dangerous at the other end of the assignment. Occasionally, he believed that removing them protected other people.

But the network made no distinction between protecting the innocent and protecting its own interests.

It was simply good at making the two things sound alike.

As the years passed, Hex became trusted because he was observant, calm when others panicked, and capable of taking responsibility for choices everyone else preferred not to acknowledge. He could enter a room and see who was afraid, who was lying, who was close to breaking.

People in the network praised his composure.

They did not see what it cost him.

He learned how to keep his expression still while somebody described a human life as a complication. He learned how to answer with sarcasm before an uncomfortable question got close to anything true. He learned the particular usefulness of being the man who seemed in control.

One colleague called him the responsible one.

Hex laughed in the man's face.

### The Man Who Rose

It was years before he understood what the first job had helped create.

A name began appearing in files, at first as a peripheral contact and then as a point through which different kinds of power seemed to flow.

Elias Voss.

He didn't control a territory or command men on street corners. He connected people who needed information hidden, money moved, witnesses frightened, or introductions made discreetly.

Everybody owed him something. If they didn't owe him money, they owed him silence.

Once, reviewing an old internal report, Hex recognized the name of the man from his first assignment. The file described his death as the removal of an obstacle in an acquisition of information channels.

Further down, buried among dry, impersonal language, was a note.

*Voss expanded operations following disruption of competing intermediary.*

Hex read it again.

A man had been killed. Roan's debt had been cleared. Elias Voss had become more powerful.

The chain was obvious when placed in front of him. It had simply never been shown to him before.

He took the report to his handler.

"You knew."

She glanced at the document.

"About?"

"Who benefited from the first job."

"It was an effective assignment."

"You used me to make somebody like Voss stronger."

"We didn't ask you to approve the long-term consequences."

"That's convenient."

She folded her hands.

"You've benefited from our arrangements, too."

Hex stood very still.

He wanted to deny it. Instead he thought about the years of work, the money that had kept Roan safe, the doors that had opened and the people who had died because somebody had decided they were inconvenient.

The handler watched his face.

"You're exceptionally good at your work," she said. "Don't confuse being good at something with being entitled to like it."

He left without answering.

For a while, he hoped Voss might eventually become the network's problem instead of its favored middleman.

That wish came true.

It did not make anything better.

### The File

Five years before Bellwether, Hex received a new assignment.

The folder was heavier than the others.

Elias Voss had started keeping copies of things he was only supposed to move: offshore routes, political favors, names belonging to people who publicly denied knowing one another, recordings of conversations that could ruin entire careers.

He had made himself too useful to ignore and too dangerous to tolerate.

The order was unambiguous.

Eliminate Voss. Recover what could be recovered. Leave no chain of exposure.

The intelligence described the location as controlled, with few people expected inside.

Hex read it in silence.

Then he reached the final section.

Associated persons.

There were names, phone numbers, traces of addresses, and photographs attached to potential connections. Most were listed as couriers, financial associates, or individuals to be monitored.

One line identified Cassian as a lower-level contact, possibly under pressure. A note suggested he had personal ties outside the network's immediate interests.

Beneath it was a photograph.

A young woman with pink hair, dark clothing, and the kind of expression that suggested she had little patience for anybody wasting her time. There was something stubborn in the angle of her chin, something alive in the way she looked at the camera.

Hex paused.

He couldn't have explained why.

It wasn't the prettiness of the picture alone. He'd seen countless faces. This one gave him an uncomfortable sense that he was seeing a whole person rather than an entry on a page. Someone who would laugh at the wrong moment and argue on principle and have no idea how close the machinery of his work had come to her life.

The note beneath the photograph read:

*Associated risk. Possible witness or emotional connection. Monitor if necessary. No direct action authorized.*

Her name was Ninja.

He read that name twice.

Then he turned the page.

A minute later, he turned back.

### The Night He Couldn't Bury

The operation began with the certainty that everything had been accounted for.

That certainty lasted less than a minute.

There were people inside who weren't in the brief. Voices where there should have been silence. Doors opening in the wrong sequence. A shouted warning.

The team was trained to continue through confusion.

Hex had been good at that for years.

But there came a moment when he saw a man who clearly had no idea how to survive what was happening around him. The man wasn't one of the designated targets. He was simply there, trying to move away from danger.

Then the gunfire changed.

The man fell.

Hex remembered his own body moving forward before thought could catch up. He remembered someone ordering him back. He remembered looking at the floor and realizing the person was no longer moving.

What happened in the next moments would stay in him as sharp, unfinished fragments.

A hand reaching for a door.

Someone calling out a name.

The terrible silence after a room full of shouting.

And later, in the report, the word *incidental*.

The team completed the assignment.

The network had used that word, too.

Completed.

Hex stood in a corridor long after the others had moved on, looking at the blood that had no place in the briefing and thinking of the photograph that had no place in his mind.

He didn't understand the connection yet in the way he eventually would. He only knew that the dead man on the floor had been real, that the woman in the file was real, and that the job had swallowed something it had no right to take.

He hadn't pulled the trigger that killed the man.

It wasn't enough to make him innocent.

### The Purge

After the mission, the people who used to speak confidently about discretion began using different words.

Exposure.

Containment.

Loose ends.

A colleague stopped answering messages. Another was reported to have died in an accident no one would discuss. Hex noticed that files he'd once been permitted to see now disappeared before he could open them.

He confronted his handler over the telephone.

"Are you cleaning up the team?"

"We're reviewing the operation."

"That's not what I asked."

"Then you shouldn't have asked."

A pause.

"You're a valuable asset."

"I've heard that before."

"Make sure you remain one."

She hung up.

Hex sat for several seconds, staring at the dark phone screen.

Then he called Roan.

His oldest friend answered on the fourth ring.

"Well, look who's decided I still exist."

"Listen carefully."

The humor died immediately.

"Hex?"

"You need to stay away from anyone who asks about me. Don't come to my place. Don't call this number again."

"What's happened?"

"I can't explain."

"You're going to have to do better than that."

Hex wanted to. He wanted to sit opposite Roan in the café and explain everything, and let Roan be furious with him, and have somebody know what it had been like.

But he knew the network could follow such conversations.

"Please," he said. "Trust me on this."

Roan was quiet for a long time.

Then he said, "You don't have to carry this alone."

Hex closed his eyes.

"That's the trouble. I do."

He ended the call before either of them could say goodbye.

That night, he removed every predictable habit from his life.

He left behind the apartment, the car, the job title he had never spoken aloud. He abandoned the people who might be used to find him and the routines that made him recognizable.

It wasn't a clever escape or a beautiful one.

It was a man with one bag walking away from the last pieces of a life he had already made unrecognizable.

### Five Years of Weather

Bellwether was not the first place he hid.

There were rented rooms above garages, cheap motels with unreliable locks, bus stations where he sat until morning because moving felt safer than sleeping. He changed routes for no good reason and sometimes left an entire town because he recognized a car he'd seen twice.

Eventually the running became quieter.

He reached a mountain village where nobody asked for an account of the previous decade, provided he turned up when he said he would.

He repaired engines and stubborn household machinery for local people. He helped an elderly neighbor get her heater working during a cold spell. He carried groceries for someone recovering from surgery and pretended it was because the bags had looked personally offensive.

He made acquaintances.

Friendship was harder.

The villagers knew him as Hex. Most assumed it was a nickname he'd earned somewhere interesting. He let them assume whatever they liked.

The local repair shop owner, Tomas, had a habit of asking questions disguised as observations.

"You know, a man your age could probably find something better to do than argue with broken kettles."

"Have you met the kettles? They deserve it."

"You never talk about where you came from."

"Terrible weather."

"Worse than here?"

"Emotionally."

Tomas laughed, then passed him another screwdriver.

Nobody in Bellwether had reason to connect Hex to the contract network. Every new day without a sign of pursuit was a day he tried not to ruin with fear.

But he still chose seats near doors.

He still woke at sounds other people slept through.

And on certain nights, when the rain was heavy and the streets were empty, he remembered the man falling in the building.

The face from the file followed.

Not because he knew Ninja. He didn't.

He didn't know how she laughed, what music she liked, or whether she'd hate everything about him if they ever met.

He could only imagine what it would be to have a brother whose name appeared in a hidden folder, and then to lose him without ever learning why.

Sometimes he wondered if the woman had been told the truth.

He doubted it.

Sometimes he imagined finding her, explaining what had happened, and accepting whatever she might do with the knowledge.

He never tried.

The network had already shown what it did to people associated with its operations. Finding her might be the same as handing over her location.

And he had no right to turn his guilt into her danger simply because the truth was eating him alive.

### The Crooked Lantern, Before the Door Opens

On the evening she arrived, Hex spent forty minutes trying to persuade an ancient motor to stop making a noise like a bucket full of cutlery.

By the time he gave up, the workshop was nearly dark.

Tomas appeared in the doorway.

"Come to the Lantern. You look like something crawled into your skull and started redecorating."

"That's just my personality."

"Well, take it out for a drink."

Hex considered refusing.

Then he washed the grease from his hands, pulled on a dark coat, and followed him into the rain.

The Crooked Lantern was half full. The bartender nodded toward Hex's usual place in the far corner.

He had become predictable in small, almost embarrassing ways.

A certain table. A certain whiskey. A repair job to complain about and somebody willing to listen without expecting the full story.

He ordered his drink and sat down.

Tomas left after a while to meet a friend. Hex stayed. Rain clattered softly against the windows, and somewhere by the fireplace two people argued over the correct way to cook potatoes.

It was ordinary.

For once, he wanted ordinary.

Then the door opened.

### The Photograph Becomes a Person

She came through the door like she belonged in a different life.

For one absurd, stretched-out second, Hex thought he was seeing something his mind had invented to punish him.

Pink hair damp from the rain. Dark jacket. A face he'd looked at in a file half a decade earlier and never quite managed to forget.

The actual person stood just inside the Crooked Lantern, taking in the room with the tired irritation of somebody who had already had enough of the day.

The photograph had not prepared him for movement.

For the way she shook rain from her sleeve. For the small, impatient adjustment of her bag. For the fact that a stranger could be the center of his worst memory and still be doing something as ordinary as choosing a place to sit.

His heart hit against his ribs.

He knew her name.

He knew who her brother was.

He knew what the network had done and what he'd helped it do.

And he knew she had absolutely no reason to know him.

"No."
