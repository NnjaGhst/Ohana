# Elsewhere — Episode 16: The Last Crossing

### Previously on Elsewhere

Ninja opened the room above the print shop for two hours. Iona came with a tea towel that Elise had borrowed. Leonard brought his old staff pass, which might help the officer find the filing cards kept by the Directorate. Just before closing, a former ferry worker called Jo brought an envelope marked with an L. Her colleague Sal had kept a second envelope inside it. He wanted to be there if it was opened. They agreed to meet on Saturday by the ferry landing.

### Chapter One — First Ride

Ninja set both feet on the pavement and lifted one pedal about as high as it could go before it came round and hit the toe of her boot. It had been years since she’d done this from the saddle. The shop-front to her left reflected two dark wheels and Hex, who had walked several feet uphill to pick up her coat when she put it over a railing.

He brought the coat back over his left arm. He had taken the keys out of the pocket because they kept hitting the railing. She asked if he had put them in the wrong pocket in his own jacket. He said there was only one kind of pocket she would dislike him for choosing, and he was pretty sure he knew which it was.

“That’s a very long way of asking me to go ahead while I still can.”

Hex backed up a pace. The road was clear to the corner. She started with one foot down, put pressure on the other pedal, and found it was easier to get upright when she let the front wheel move a little. In a few seconds she’d made it through the reflection in the shop window. She looked back only after she was sure both wheels were going the way she wanted.

The wind was coming down the hill. It only took a minute of it to bring the blood back to her hands. She found herself on the gentle slope by the post box, trying to remember if she’d ever asked Elise to ride the same way she did. She pictured a shoe planted hard on the road as Elise pushed off in front of her. If she had ever ridden beside her sister without looking over to see who knew what they were doing, the moment was lost in all the others. She wished it could still be an ordinary thing she didn’t remember.

At the top of the hill she squeezed the brake a little too hard. The front tyre came almost to a stop. She heard two steps from behind, then none. Hex had been keeping up. He let her get the other foot down before he asked what she thought of it.

“It stops. I might need to practise making it stop without announcing it to everyone in my spine.”

Hex gave her the coat. She sat on the saddle with both feet down while he helped her get her left sleeve started. Then he handed her the keys. He had put them on the outside of the badge in his pocket so he could get them with one hand. When she put them back in her own coat they made a familiar sound against the small coin she kept there.

They could go down the long way towards the river. There was a place at the square where she would have to get off because a work van was blocking the end. Hex said the choice was to ride down to the van, or walk to the better road where she could ride almost all the way.

She got off and walked with him. He didn’t try to take the bicycle. At the end of the path she stopped under an awning to move a lock out of the way of her pedal. There she found a tiny break in the clouds through which sunlight reached two empty chairs set outside a café. She said if he wanted to have breakfast there on the way back he should remind her. By the time the two of them reached the corner she was already planning which chair she wanted. She got on the bicycle again before she could be tempted to leave it by the awning and walk down all the way.

### Chapter Two — Low Water

At the ferry landing the river was low enough to reveal old lines of green on the stone. A man at the top of the ramp had tipped a little sand into a bucket and was putting a second layer beside the first. He said part of the lower landing was slick in this light. Ninja got off early and wheeled the bicycle past him.

The officer waited in the small booth where the tickets were kept. She had asked to come after Ninja rang. She’d told Ninja she only needed to check whether the papers were relevant before she could decide what to ask Sal. She’d wait on the near bank unless Jo asked her to cross. For something that could take a while there was a table in the ferry office, where everyone would be able to sit down.

The new timetable had more hours on it than the sign could fit. A strip of white tape held the Sunday times at the bottom. Ninja could see why someone might have missed a notice that only covered one day. The officer told her there was a separate line on the watch list for safety checks later in the day. It was a record someone at either end of the crossing had to sign. She wanted to see whether both lists had changed together.

Hex found a rail where Ninja could tie up the bicycle. He clipped the frame to it and left room for somebody with wet hands to handle a combination lock on the next rail. The ferry attendant told them not to leave it too near the wall because it was where a goose came to sit. Hex looked at the amount of sand on the ramp and asked whether the goose had asked for that too.

“It looked at me,” said the attendant. “You can work here twelve years and never need a further instruction.”

While they were waiting for the boat to arrive, Ninja climbed up a little farther on the bank to see how much of the town she’d passed through. Looking back from there she could pick out the print shop by the color of the roof, which was much lighter than the one beside it. She thought anybody else who looked would see an ordinary building in a place where you might go for a newspaper. Even Rose had worked there a long time before she’d started leaving cups upstairs.

“Do you think there’ll be a minute after we come back when I can take the long road by myself?”

“There’s a whole road. I’ll meet you wherever you want.”

She hadn’t been asking if she should be able to do it. She’d been wondering whether there would be energy left to want to. The boat was moving away from the far slip. In the gray morning it looked colder on the other side of the water. A person in a bright jacket was at the end of the quay. Ninja held up her hand when the person lifted theirs. When she put her hand back in her pocket, she found that the keys had moved around the coin and she had to turn them once to make room.

The first passenger down the ramp had a dog that wanted to go on without her. The woman let the lead run through one hand for three feet and then said its name. The dog stopped one paw short of the paint. Ninja waited for them both to finish. In the white booth, the officer stayed where she had said she would. The attendant came up to collect the tickets, and Hex showed him two on the same screen. The boat’s small engine trembled under the walkway as they stepped on together.

### Chapter Three — From the Far Bank

Jo waited on the far bank with a man in a brown canvas jacket. He was standing close enough to the shelter that a line of water hit the ground just ahead of his shoes. Jo moved to the other side of him and said the first thing he ought to know was that the two people coming off were the two people she’d told him about. He didn’t have to spend the whole morning wondering who might come up behind them.

They walked to the bench under the shelter. Sal went to the end that had a view back over the water. He had a small blue folder with the elastic wrapped twice round it because someone had put a knot at the end of the original length. The elastic fell to the floor when he opened it. Hex picked it up and left it on the bench between his own legs so it couldn’t blow away into the river.

Sal said he was trying to remember the part before he saw Elise. He had come down from the little room where they heated the meals when somebody asked whether he could help a man on the road with a bicycle. The front wheel had got caught by the bottom of the steps. By the time he got round the bollard, a woman had already got the wheel loose and told the man he’d want one foot behind it when he stopped again.

Ninja could picture the step by the bike shop. Sal said he used to walk past it just before coming on shift if he’d set out before lunch. He’d thanked the woman at the shelter, two streets farther down. She was waiting for a different person. He knew her as Elise by then, from the times she came over with papers for the upstairs office.

She had a receipt folded over the corner of an envelope and wanted to know if he’d been told to keep the last boat ready. He showed her the revised timetable. She went back up the path to see if the person she was expecting was coming. It was a woman who came for him instead. She had a bag of food, and then a tin with a spoon in it, which had already spilled something sticky against the flap.

“Was the woman Jo?”

“My sister. Jo had her turn down on the other bank by then. My sister couldn’t stay either.” He stopped for a few seconds. “Elise came back and gave me the envelope while I was holding the bag. I can still see myself trying to take both of them with one hand.”

He handed Ninja a cream stub torn from the ferry block. The number on it was the one he’d used to mark the envelope. Later he’d put it in the blue folder because the office was locked, and he'd used a stub as a marker when he couldn’t find the proper forms. His father taught him to do that when the men were shutting the ticket desk. A ticket had a number people could use if they came back for the thing.

The man had started to look at his boots. Ninja waited. Above them a drip hit the railing and the sound was almost like the end of a sentence. She told him Margaret remembered Elise at the shop, and the officer had a copy of the signed time. Sal wanted to know if the officer needed to hear the part about the bag and the spoon. He’d worried it would take too long to get to the time.

“I think that’s a good place to start. It’s how you remember having only one hand free.”

He looked back at her and nodded. Jo reached for her phone to ring the officer. Sal kept the folder open while they waited. The elastic lay exactly where Hex had put it.

### Chapter Four — The Blue Folder

There was good light over the wide counter in the ferry office. They moved the tea to the narrow table by the door. Sal had turned the cream stub the same way up as the boat number every time he looked at it because he couldn’t see both upside down at once. The officer noted which way he set it the first time and asked if he could show her how he used to match a stub to a lost-property entry.

He laid a sheet from the blue folder beside the envelope. The part that matched was a handwritten number. Once he’d pointed to it, Ninja could see how somebody in an office could have taken the hand of the man who wrote that number for the hand of a man who’d done nothing but write numbers for years. Here he sat over it with two rough fingers drawn down the edge so the paper didn’t slip off the counter.

He said the earlier crossing had left before dusk. He’d rung the station and asked if the spare staff notice had been carried downstairs. It was the kind of question that could send someone to the wrong room if they hadn’t put the phone down before walking. Evelyn had asked what the time by the boat was. He’d said eighteen forty. That was when she wrote down he'd called. It was also the time he wrote on the sheet when he put it in the folder. He had an empty line beside it for the boat number, which never got filled.

The officer lifted the copy of what Pike had offered. On it, a time field said nineteen twenty beside a typed line about Elise being notified. The time on Sal’s slip mentioned a phone call to the station. Nobody had written that Elise was at the ferry at nineteen twenty. Sal had already been halfway up the roadway home by then.

“Can that time be in the new account without my sister’s name attached to it?”

The officer said they could show exactly what Sal had reported and check it against Evelyn’s record. She made sure he could see the titles on the forms before she asked him to turn his piece of paper over. There was nothing on the back. She wrote that down as carefully as the mark on the front. Some things only didn’t exist until someone was tempted to invent them.

Sal drew another envelope out from under the sheet. It was addressed to Iona, care of the upstairs room. He said if it had had a street number he would have walked it there the following day. He had asked the station if somebody had reported it missing. When he finally heard that Elise had died, he had been too late for her to tell him where the upstairs room was. He told himself he’d need to find Iona. There were so many rooms near the station that the words had never meant very much to anybody he asked.

The officer asked to photograph where he’d kept the envelope and how it had been folded. Ninja said she would take it to Iona if she wanted. Jo had come to the other end of the counter. Ninja nearly put a finger on the envelope but stopped when she saw how tightly Jo had closed her hands around her empty cup.

“She ought to be able to choose who’s there.”

The officer said she would ring Iona and ask. It was easier than Ninja thought it would be to wait for her to reach for the telephone. She was only waiting to hear what Iona wanted to do, while somebody who knew why was sitting by the window with her feet on the floor. Nothing had ever been as simple as getting a piece of paper back. For a minute it was as simple as that.

### Chapter Five — What the Letter Said

Iona called Ninja back while they were out under the canopy, waiting for the office to make the necessary copies. She was in the car park at her supermarket. A trolley with one bad wheel kept turning across the space where she wanted to stand. Ninja could hear her put the phone down on the boot of the car so she could use two hands to get it out of the way.

After a while she came back to the phone. She said she’d expected one of these things to turn up in the end and she’d imagined saying she wanted it on a day when she could sit very still as it arrived. If nobody needed to take the original away, would Ninja be able to bring it to the print shop? She could meet them upstairs when she was back in town.

The officer spoke to her briefly. Iona decided it could be opened in the ferry office with the officer there. She asked Ninja to stay, then read the note on the way home if she wanted. Her voice went all the way quiet after she said that. Ninja stayed on the line until Iona said she was getting into the car.

The letter was one piece of thin paper torn down one side. It had already been folded to fit, and there was a smudge on the top corner where it had got damp once. It asked Iona to come upstairs on Friday even if they missed each other that evening. It said she had made a list of everyone who had asked for their own statements back, and that she’d put the list somewhere safe at the office on Market Street. Iona was on the list. The last line of the letter said Elise had found a new chair, and could she please bring two mugs.

Hex came outside with Ninja’s clean copy in the same hand as two cups, pinned against one cup by his thumb. He’d let the officer put the original away for Iona. The two of them sat with a view of the river, from where they could watch the ferry only after it came around the bend.

“I think she meant to tell Iona about the room sooner. She wanted to show her a whole thing instead of trying to explain what she was making.”

Hex said it sounded familiar. Ninja turned to him and waited for the rest of it. He said he’d learned from the last three days that some people needed to see the floor after the extra chair went in. It was harder to imagine where two chairs would fit while you only ever saw the one.

She put her hand on his knee. He stayed close. She leaned her shoulder into the thick part of his coat and watched him see which bit of the letter she was still holding with her fingers. The light under the shelter was coming from the river, pale and moving. They kept very still while the boat went past. It left a low brown wake, with two birds settling on it a second after it had passed.

She folded the copy and drew back just far enough to find his mouth. It was a small kiss and he didn’t ask it to hit any particular mark. When she put her forehead against him he held both the cups farther away.

“I’d like to take her letter home now.”

Hex finished enough of the tea to stand up with the cups in one hand. She left her place on the bench warm, which was an almost impossible thing to carry away with her. She tried as far as the first railing.

### Chapter Six — The Bell on the Way Home

They took the next boat across. It ran on time. The ride had that kind of quiet you can only get near something making a regular sound. Hex pointed to the bicycle by the rail when he could see it. Ninja had already seen the red coastguard sign, and the bucket of sand, and the dry strip half way up the ramp. It felt a little like coming home to a place she was only just starting to know.

Jo and Sal stayed on the far landing while the boat pulled out. Ninja lifted a hand to them. Sal raised the old blue folder instead of his hand, then looked down at it with surprise. It had come back to him almost empty. She thought she might remember that part later and like the way both his shoulders came down all at once.

Hex unlocked the bicycle and gave her the key. The road she wanted to ride went around the low slope, between a high wall and a path that led almost straight to the print shop. He told her he’d meet her at the gate. When she got onto the saddle he waited until she was moving to turn himself toward the path. She heard one of his steps quite far to the left as the roads began to separate.

The first stretch was colder than the morning. After a few minutes it was possible to move one of her fingers off the handlebars without noticing the wind. She stopped at the crossing by the house where people liked to hang out wet tea towels. A boy went past with a bicycle the same size as his father’s, trying as hard as he could to ring his bell without lifting his hand. Ninja tried hers. The first time the lever came back without making a sound. The second time it gave a clear note right across the street.

The boy looked up. She rang once more as she went by. He rang back.

Outside the print shop Evelyn had met Rose with the lost scarf under one arm, by mistake, because she’d meant to put it back with the water bottle. The two women stood under the shop sign as Ninja wheeled the bicycle to the gate. Hex was there wearing an expression of exaggerated patience. He asked if she’d put the coat to any use. She told him he could put a hand in the pocket if he wanted to find out.

He did, and she put her hand over his for a minute. She could have gone straight upstairs to see whether Iona was there. She was glad to have just this one stop before she did. He had put a thumb through the hole in the handle of her bicycle basket and she knew it was him by the way he checked the whole thing would take his weight before he rested any on it.

“I’d like to take the long road again when it’s light.”

The inside door was open, and Iona stood on the top stair. Rose called up to ask whether they had enough mugs. Hex looked at Ninja, and Ninja called up that they had four.

On the little table there was room for a person to put something down in front of them. Iona and Ninja sat on the same side so they could look at the letter together if Iona liked. Hex gave them the warmer cups. He stayed by the window long enough to see the last of the sunlight reach the wall opposite. Then he took the chair by the door and left it open a little, the way Ninja liked it. He could still hear a bicycle going past long after everybody on the street had stopped ringing bells.
