# Elsewhere: The Hidden Valley
## Episode 1 — The Light in the Hall

The day somebody took her photograph, Ninja was wearing one wet sock.

The other one was dry. She knew because she'd been shifting her weight between them for the last six minutes, waiting under the narrow awning of a corner shop for Elise to come back with an umbrella. It was a good umbrella with a solid wooden handle. Ninja had lent it to her that morning.

Her phone buzzed. Elise had sent a picture of a front door with a brass lion on it.

Found out where Mrs. Vale lives.

Ninja typed back, I thought the plan was to WALK HER TO THE DOCTOR.

It was. Then she needed walking home.

Ninja put the phone away and took a chip out of the paper bag under her arm. By the time Elise appeared on the other side of the road, Ninja had eaten enough to affect the spirit of sharing. Elise saw the bag and hurried the last few steps with her head down.

“You bought me chips.”

“I bought me chips. Your application is under review.”

Elise reached in anyway. A bus went through a puddle behind her. Someone on the far side of the shelter shouted something the sisters couldn't hear. Ninja turned just in time to see the man's trousers, which were wet from the knee up and dry from the knee down.

“How?” she said, and laughed so hard Elise had to take the bag from her.

For a second, while the bus was moving away, she saw a man across the street with a phone up near his face. She thought he might be taking a picture of the puddle. Then Elise nudged her under the umbrella and Ninja went home thinking about how good it would feel to take off her socks.

She forgot about him before she'd reached the crossing.

* * *

Ninja's flat was warmest in the hallway. A pipe ran through the wall just inside the door, which meant every winter she started dropping the post there and by February had invented a whole second desk on the floor.

Elise picked up a letter with her shoe and pushed it out of the way. Ninja turned the hall light on. From the kitchen she called to ask if Elise wanted a plate.

“Are you getting yourself one?”

“No.”

“I'll get them both.”

Ninja took off her shoes and left them at an angle where the heat could get in. The flat was on the third floor. From the living room window, if you stood close enough, you could see a building opposite with a star in the upstairs window. The family there left it up until at least February every year. She liked them for it, without knowing anything else about them.

Elise brought in the plates. She found the salt in the kitchen, came back for the vinegar and then asked Ninja how her shift had gone.

“Fine,” Ninja said.

“Did they fix the lift?”

“They said they'd look at it.”

“That's a lovely way of saying they used the stairs once.”

Ninja smiled. She ate for a little while. Elise told her how the neighbour had turned back from the doctor's halfway down the road because there was a lamp on at home. They'd got all the way there to find the neighbour's grandson studying at the kitchen table, perfectly happy in the light.

“Did she make you turn it off?”

“She made me stay for a cup of tea. I got the impression that had been the plan all along.”

“You walked that woman halfway across town so she could show you her grandson.”

“It was a very good cup of tea.”

Ninja got through another couple of chips. Then she said, “A patient's son came in today.”

Elise waited for more.

“He asked me what he was supposed to do when he got home. I thought he meant paperwork. I started telling him where to find a number. Then I realised he meant what was he supposed to *do*. Like, what was he supposed to have for dinner? What would he put on television? That sort of thing.”

“What did you tell him?”

“I told him there was a chair by the window if he wanted to sit down for a minute. I went back two minutes later and he'd already gone.”

They sat with that. Elise put the side of her foot against Ninja's dry sock. A little later she asked if Ninja wanted her to stay and watch something.

“Only if I get to pick.”

“How long does it take you to pick?”

“Forty to fifty business days.”

“Start now, then.”

They watched an episode of a detective show in which the detective spent an hour overlooking the one person who had a key to a boat. By the end they were both shouting at him. Elise laughed with her entire chest and sometimes knocked her head lightly against Ninja's shoulder. Nina from work could usually tell when Ninja had spent a night with her sister. She came in the following morning a little less wound up.

Before she left, Elise borrowed the umbrella again. She stood in the hallway untangling its strap.

“Thursday,” she said. “Are you late again?”

“Until eight. Why?”

“I thought I might come by afterward.”

“Bring the umbrella. That'll give us something to talk about.”

Elise made a tiny bow and went down the stairs.

Ninja shut the door, saw the two empty plates and decided she would wash them in the morning. She was careful about a lot of things. With Elise, she had always had the luxury of leaving something for tomorrow.

* * *

Three months before the photograph, Elise took Ninja to the sea for one day.

They left from different ends of town and met at the station. Ninja had put a clean shirt in her bag because she didn't trust the weather. Elise had put in a whole lemon and a knife wrapped in a tea towel. When Ninja asked what she planned to do with them, Elise said it was against her principles to find herself by the sea without any way of cutting a lemon.

The first train took them as far as a town with a green station roof. The second ran beside an embankment of yellow flowers. They sat facing forward for the first part, then swapped because Elise wanted to see how the same country looked when it was going away. Ninja said it looked exactly the same. Elise watched a field disappear behind them and said it seemed bigger this way. After a while Ninja leaned across to look too. She thought Elise might have been right.

The sea came into view without warning. It was low and grey, with several men in bright orange coats carrying long poles along the water. Ninja had been expecting to catch up on all the messages she hadn't answered. She opened her phone, found there was barely a signal and put it back in her bag. They walked over a bridge above a river that had very nearly reached the sea itself. Somebody had fastened a child's yellow boot to the railing so its owner could find it. Elise stopped to make sure it couldn't fall through.

At the far end of the bridge they passed a yard full of boats standing up out of the water on wooden supports. Ninja liked boats better when she could walk around them. She could look behind one and see where somebody had sanded a groove smooth enough to run a finger across. Elise hung back now and then to see which direction Ninja chose. That was one of the reasons the two of them travelled well together. Her sister could make friends with anyone in the village in three minutes, but when she and Ninja were walking, she didn't have to decide where they were going just because there was a road.

At lunch, they bought paper cups of tea and fish from a little window in the wall. Elise unwrapped the knife. The man at the window wanted to know where she'd got a lemon that size. She told him she had found it at the bottom of a bag marked three for a pound. He said he would have paid a pound for that one alone. She gave him half, in exchange for his drawing them a map on the back of the receipt.

The map had a square labelled pub, a line labelled steep, and a very small oval that he said was the place you could see a seal if you went quietly. Ninja spent an hour on a bench above the oval. A dark shape came up and went down twice. The first time they both saw it. The second time Elise was reading a message and missed it. Ninja described it afterward with increasing confidence until she had given the seal an expression and a personal grievance against a gull.

Elise's message was from work. Ninja could tell because Elise read it twice and turned her phone over in a particular way, putting it carefully on the middle of the bench. Ninja had always thought her sister would end up in an office with too many plants and a noticeboard nobody else was allowed to move. That was how Elise had looked as a child when she'd set up a shop in the sitting room. She'd insisted on wrapping every purchase and once issued a written receipt for a conker.

The work she did now didn't have much in common with that picture. There were things in envelopes she was asked to add up, and calls from men who preferred evenings. When Elise talked about it she usually talked about the arithmetic. She could spend ten minutes explaining why two people had filed the same amount under different dates, and Ninja would listen until she could see the arithmetic had got her sister's mind away from whatever had actually upset her.

They split another cup of tea on the walk back. Elise waited until they were off the bridge to say she thought she might leave the job. She said she used to come home cross about things that were fixable. Someone made a mistake. She could show them where it was, and then they could fix it. Recently she'd been finding mistakes that people already knew about. She could hear it in the silence before they thanked her for pointing one out.

Ninja wanted to know when she'd leave. Elise said it would take her a little time to get everything in order. She had more than one reason to want that, and money was the one she could explain over a cup of tea. They found a seat on a low wall and worked out on the back of the map what she could get by on for a couple of months. The wind tried to take the map twice. They weighed it down with the lemon.

On the train home, Elise fell asleep with her cheek against the collar of her coat. Ninja stayed awake to make sure they got off at the right station. She had told someone she could pick up a shift in the morning. She took the invitation to cancel it out of her phone, looked at it and thought about how good it had felt to sit on the bench and have nowhere else to be. Then she put the phone away without sending anything.

Elise woke up as they came into town. She looked out at the same string of houses going past and wanted to know if she'd snored. Ninja said she'd conducted an entire meeting. Elise took a minute to remember what station they were at. Just before the doors opened, she found the crumpled map in her pocket and pressed it into Ninja's hand.

They could go back in summer, she said. This time they could stay a whole night and see where the little river went out after the tide had come in.

Ninja put the map in her coat pocket. For the next few months, every time she reached in for her keys, she could feel it growing softer around the edges.

* * *

On Wednesday, Elise came into the bookshop at the end of Ninja's road with six envelopes in her handbag. They'd agreed to meet there after work. The owner was making tea and allowed them to stand by the radiator as long as they kept the books out of the way of the kettle.

“Bills?” Ninja asked when Elise sat down and took out an envelope.

“Things I'm supposed to post by Friday.”

“Is one of them my birthday card?”

“Your birthday was four months ago.”

“That's why I'm asking.”

Elise took a photograph from the front of a postcard rack and held it up. It showed a stretch of white beach under a blue sky nobody in their town had ever seen. She said they could go somewhere next year. Somewhere a train ran all the way to the sea. Ninja said there was no point choosing a beach before they'd checked whether it had somewhere to buy chips. Elise picked up a second card. This one had an illustrated fish holding a cone of chips in one fin. She handed it to Ninja.

“Are you asking me to go on holiday with that fish?”

“He looks like he's got his own life. I think it's just us.”

Ninja bought the card. For months afterward she would come across it between the pages of the book she'd bought that day. At first she'd close the book every time. Then one evening she set the card on the mantel, where the fish could get on with his life.

On the way out of the shop, Elise went to collect her bag and came back without it. She was looking at Ninja with a question on her face.

“Everything all right?” Ninja asked.

Elise looked past her toward the street. “Would you tell me if somebody came round asking for me?”

“Of course. Who's going to come round?”

“Probably no one. I think I've offended a man by being better at his job than he is. He'll get over it.”

Elise picked up the bag. The story made sense to Ninja. She could think of a man at work who was still taking the long way round a corridor because she'd fixed a filing mistake he'd made in March. On the pavement she put her arm through Elise's, and they walked to the bus stop discussing whether it would be a kindness to buy that man a fish postcard too.

* * *

On Thursday, Ninja's bus got stuck one stop from home behind a van with a flat tyre. She got off and walked. The air felt cooler than the calendar suggested it should, which reminded her there was a hole in one of her gloves. She made a mental list of everything she'd meant to buy before she got too tired to go back out. A loaf of bread. Washing powder. A bulb for the little lamp in the kitchen.

There was a voicemail from Elise. It had arrived while Ninja was helping someone look for a lost pair of glasses. Elise's voice was quiet on the recording. She said she'd been held up and would try her again later. In the background there was a sound like wind passing very close to the phone.

Ninja sent a text instead of ringing. I'll be up. You don't have to hurry.

At home she found a heel of bread in the freezer and ate it toasted with butter. She put on the detective show again and waited until half past ten, then eleven. She knew Elise did odd bits of work for people round town and sometimes got held up. She wondered if Elise had left her charger somewhere, as she often did.

By midnight Ninja had put a folded blanket on the sofa in case Elise came and wanted to stay. When she climbed into bed she left the light in the hallway on.

In the morning a police officer knocked. Ninja was halfway through looking for a clean shirt. She opened the door with one sleeve twisted around her forearm and spent the first few seconds wondering if the officer had the wrong flat.

He asked whether she was Elise's sister.

“Yes,” she said. It was still the easiest question in the world.

He asked to come in.

There was a chair nearest the kitchen window. Ninja kept gesturing at it, although the officer had already sat down. He told her Elise had died the night before at a social club down by the river. There had been other people there. He asked if there was somebody she could call to sit with her.

“What time?” Ninja asked.

He told her the time they had been called out. It was before her text. She folded the corner of the sleeve over in her hand and asked him to say the time again.

She remembered a patient asking her the same question twice at work and her saying “I've just told you,” in the briskest part of her voice. She would think about that for years. Sitting there, she could hold about four words at a time. The rest had to go somewhere and come back when they were ready.

The officer left a number on the table. She thanked him on the way out. It came out politely and sounded very far away.

Then she made tea. The kettle clicked off. She poured it into two mugs, one of which had a handle large enough for Elise to put four fingers through. Ninja left that one on the counter. At four in the afternoon she tipped it away, cold, along with the cup she had made for herself.

Her phone still showed the little arrow beside the voicemail. She looked at it a long time before pressing play. She'd listen to that recording so often over the following months that she could have told you exactly where the wind came in. That first time, all she heard was Elise saying she'd try her again later.

Ninja sat in the chair by the window for a while.

* * *

The first person she called was their aunt. Their aunt asked her three questions in quick succession, then said, “Give me your address again.” Half an hour later she knocked with a bag of bread rolls in one hand. Ninja had never particularly wanted a bread roll before eleven in the morning, and the sight of them made her cry harder than she'd managed while she was alone.

Over the following days there were names to spell, documents to find, a funeral to arrange. Ninja could do all of those things. She could ask for a different day when an appointment clashed. She knew which cousin should hear the news from her and which one would feel better hearing it first from his mother. In the evening she sometimes found the pillowcase half pulled off her pillow because she'd begun to change it that morning and forgotten what she was doing.

One afternoon a woman from the funeral home asked her to bring an outfit for Elise. Ninja opened her sister's wardrobe and took down a dark jacket. They had once shared it on a trip where they'd packed for temperatures about ten degrees warmer than the ones they got. Elise had gone out to buy coffee in it, leaving Ninja in a blanket at the little hotel window. They had traded turns all weekend. Ninja put the jacket in the bag and then took it out. She wanted it hanging on that door for a while longer. She picked another one.

At the funeral people told stories. Mrs. Vale said Elise always slowed down when they crossed a road together, to walk at the pace Mrs. Vale could actually manage. A man Ninja had never seen said Elise's paperwork was always in order. That one made her laugh, because she knew Elise's idea of putting things in order was to put the most important one on top of whatever pile she happened to be looking at.

Afterward Ninja ate sandwiches she couldn't remember choosing. When almost everyone had gone, she sat with Mrs. Vale, who was struggling to open a packet of biscuits. Ninja took the packet and pulled it open, and Mrs. Vale broke one biscuit in half and gave part to her. They ate them quietly.

The police were able to tell Ninja a little more in the weeks that followed. Three people had died that evening. Elise had gone to the club by arrangement. The officer could see that much from the calls they had traced. When Ninja asked what Elise had been supposed to do there, he said he couldn't tell her. She asked if that meant he didn't know. He looked at his notes for a few seconds and said he'd call if he could tell her more.

She went to the building once. It sat back from the pavement, beside a muddy strip of river. Through the downstairs windows she could see chairs pushed against one wall. The door was shut and somebody had put a notice on it about where to direct enquiries.

She stood across the road a while, waiting for herself to have a feeling she knew what to do with. A delivery driver walked past in a bright jacket. He knocked on the next door along, was let in, and ran back to the van with his arms pulled into his sleeves because it was cold.

The normality of it went through her. She wanted to step into the van and have his ordinary afternoon instead. In the end she turned and walked home. It was almost dark when she got there. The light in the hallway was on.

* * *

In the week after the funeral, a man Ninja didn't know brought Elise's bicycle to her flat.

He had pushed it up from where she'd left it outside his office. The back tyre was flat. He apologised for that twice, while Ninja held the stairwell door open and tried to see if she remembered him from the sandwiches after the funeral. He gave her a key on a ring and mentioned Elise's first name in a tone that suggested he had used it every day. She took the key and asked him which office it was.

It was above an appliance shop a few streets from the river. He said he used to work at the next desk. Ninja asked what Elise had been doing on Thursday evening. The man looked down at the front wheel. He said he hadn't seen her since Wednesday, and that one day when it was quiet in the office she could come and collect a few things she'd left behind.

Ninja wrote his first name on a letter that had been lying by the door. He left. Afterwards she realised she didn't have a number for him and that the name of the appliance shop was something she had only assumed she would remember. She could picture its red lettering with complete clarity. She couldn't tell you the word it made.

The bike stayed in the stairwell for nine days. On the tenth, Mrs. Vale came over with a bicycle pump. She had borrowed it from the grandson who'd left the lamp on in the kitchen, and he had asked her to make sure she brought back the little attachment in the clear bag. They took the bike down to the pavement and found where the valve was. Air went in. Air came back out.

Mrs. Vale, who walked slowly and with a stick on wet days, had once delivered post up three hills on a bicycle very much like Elise's. She asked Ninja to find an old spoon and a shallow dish of water. They turned the wheel over by the front step. It took them until dusk to fix it. Ninja's hands were filthy by the time the tyre went back on, and they had both forgotten where they'd put the attachment for the pump. It was in Mrs. Vale's coat pocket.

Ninja had no plan to ride it home, because home was already just behind them. When she reached the end of the street she went round the corner anyway. There was a little public garden behind an old school, with two sloping paths and a cherry tree. She hadn't been there in years. She got off the bike and stood in the gate until she could see which bench had been replaced. The new one was made of metal. Her sister would have said a metal bench proved that the person choosing benches had never had to sit on one.

On the way back, Ninja rode up the hill slowly. She expected it to be easier when she stood up. It was easier to stay where she was and change down a gear. She went past one lit house after another until she came to her own. Mrs. Vale was still waiting by the door. She wanted to know if the patch had held.

The appliance shop didn't take long to find, once Ninja began looking. A bakery manager gave her very clear directions. She visited on a Saturday. The woman behind the counter had opened the shop a few years earlier and knew the owner of every business on the same side of the road. She said the office upstairs was empty. A cleaner had been up once to give it a going over, and the chairs had gone out that morning.

She let Ninja up anyway. There was blue carpet halfway up the stairs and grey carpet the rest of the way. Beyond the landing was a long room with broad windows, yellow plastic blinds, and dusty squares where desks had stood. Somebody had left a pencil on the windowsill. Ninja opened the cupboards, starting at one end and going separately through each, the way she searched the linen room at work when people insisted there was another box.

There was a torn pad, a metal spoon and three blue drawing pins. The pencil was sharpened, which made her think of Elise. Elise loved the neatness of a sharp pencil and despised having to find a sharpener. Ninja kept the pencil. The woman downstairs wrote the landlord's number on the back of her business card. He said later that the tenancy had ended and he hadn't been given a forwarding address.

For the next week Ninja thought about making another call to the police. She had the account of what happened in a little notebook with a red cover. Someone had collected a bicycle. Someone had paid to clear the office. There were a lot of sentences like that. Each had an ordinary thing in it. Put together, they still looked as if she were walking around the outside of a building and trying to guess what it was like to be inside.

At two in the morning she took out the voicemail again. She listened to it once, then looked at the number for the station. Finally she turned the phone over and opened the drawer where she had put the map from the fish shop.

The lemon had left a little pale circle in the corner. She could see the oval where the seal had come up. There was a part of that day they'd never talked about afterwards. On the way home, when Elise was nearly asleep, she'd opened one eye as if she knew Ninja might need to see her awake. It was an ordinary look, easy to miss. Ninja didn't want to forget it. She'd spent so much time asking about the night at the club that she had begun to think of her sister as somebody waiting in a room by the river. She'd had twenty-nine other years, all full of things just as small as that look on the train.

In the morning Ninja slid the map into one envelope and the notebook into another. She kept them in the same drawer. She could open one without having to open the other.

* * *

That first summer, Ninja took the bicycle to see her aunt. She hadn't phoned ahead. She meant to drop off a tin she'd borrowed and then go straight home. Her aunt took the tin and asked if she could use an extra pair of hands in the garden. Ninja started to turn up her sleeves. Her aunt put a chair in the shade and asked her to hold the bowl while she picked beans.

A radio somewhere next door was playing the afternoon traffic report. Ninja moved the chair closer so her aunt didn't have to reach as far. Then her aunt handed her half a handful of beans and told her to keep those separate.

They were for next year. Her aunt had been saving seeds this way since before the two girls were born. Elise had helped her once and wanted to know why you didn't eat the nicest ones when they were right there in front of you. The next year she'd come back and asked to see which row had been hers.

Ninja had no memory of this. She had been too young to be there, or somewhere else altogether. She felt a little sharp twist when she pictured it. There were whole afternoons her sister had lived that Ninja knew nothing about. Then she found herself asking whether Elise had brought anything with her the second time.

A notebook, her aunt said. In case there was something worth writing down. She had drawn the bean plants in the margin and got into an argument about how many leaves they had. Her aunt could remember which school notebook it was because there was a timetable on the cover.

They sat there until the radio was turned off next door. Ninja carried the bowl inside and found three more chairs stacked beneath the back window. For the first time that afternoon she thought of her cousin, who would be coming in with the children shortly and would want somewhere to put the little one down. She unstacked a chair while her aunt made tea.

Before she left, her aunt put three beans into the empty biscuit tin. She told Ninja when to plant them. Ninja lived on the third floor. Her aunt had already considered that and said it was a climbing plant, and it could climb in a pot just as well.

She carried the tin home on the bike with one hand clamped over the lid whenever she went downhill. In spring she planted all three on the windowsill. Two came up. She phoned her aunt on a Sunday morning to tell her. When her aunt asked what had happened to the third, Ninja said it had been saving its energy and would be going on holiday by itself next year. She could almost hear the joke the way Elise would have told it. She finished it in her own voice.

* * *

At work, Ninja said yes to anything that had an ending. Covering an hour for somebody had an ending. Finding a blanket, getting an answer about lunch, walking someone to a lift that now worked. The whole day could be made of small things she knew how to do. Sometimes she reached home still carrying the pen from her pocket in her hand.

She wasn't miserable every minute. She discovered an excellent secondhand bookshop down by the bus stop and bought a crime novel purely because the detective on the cover looked like a man who should never be allowed a boat. She spent the better part of a Sunday trying to hang a shelf, then set her books in a stack on the floor and left it. She laughed her first really helpless laugh after the funeral when Nina from work tried to tell her a joke she'd heard in the queue at a bakery and ruined the ending four times running.

One winter she bought a new lamp for the hall. It had a shade that threw a warm oval up the wall. She started turning it on before she went out for a late shift. Every evening she came back to the same little patch of light, regardless of how her day went.

Year by year people asked about Elise less often. Ninja began to think the polite thing to do was mention her whenever she had a story she wanted to tell. She managed it some evenings. On others a group would be talking over dinner and by the time she'd decided where to put the sentence, someone had started looking for the bill.

On those nights she got off the bus one stop early. She knew which windows on the long road home would be lit. A retired man did a crossword at a table near the glass. Two students had stuck a paper skeleton on their door and given it different hats throughout the winter. When a family she had never spoken to brought home a baby, she could see the pattern of their lighting change. For weeks the little upstairs room was bright at all hours. There was something comforting about walking past and knowing somebody else was awake.

By the fifth year, Nina could tell when one of Ninja's days off had filled up with favours before it began. She caught her at the coat hooks one evening and asked what she'd planned for her upcoming leave.

“Couple of things,” Ninja said.

“What did *you* plan?”

Ninja pulled one end of her scarf through the knot twice by mistake. “I'd like to sit somewhere and have a cup of tea while it's still hot.”

“That seems doable.”

It did. And then two more people asked if she could help out that weekend. She gave them answers on the way home, opened the fridge in her work shoes and stood there until the fridge started beeping.

She closed it. She took her phone out and sent two texts with a version of the same sentence. I can't do it that day.

She sat on the kitchen floor for a few minutes after they went through, waiting for someone to argue. One person wrote back, No worries. The other sent a thumbs-up.

She put the kettle on.

* * *

About a year before Ninja went to Bellwether, a woman at work brought in a bag full of worn paperbacks and left it by the coat hooks. Everyone was told to help themselves. By lunchtime somebody had made off with the detective stories and somebody else had refused to take a book without paying, so there was an uneven collection of small change beside the bag.

Ninja picked up a book she hadn't heard of, about a woman who inherited a small hotel on the wrong side of a mountain. For the first few chapters it was unclear what the woman was meant to do with the hotel. Mostly she kept fixing a dripping tap. Ninja found that unexpectedly restful. She read it on the bus and started leaving it open at home so she could read a page while the kettle ran.

The woman who had left the bag was Nina, the same one who sometimes told stories and put such work into getting the beginning right that she'd forget the end. Ninja asked her what had got her to give away that particular book. Nina said she'd been keeping it for a rainy day and eventually realised she had been keeping it through all of them.

Ninja thought that was funny enough to repeat. She nearly texted Elise. She had stopped checking their thread every evening a long time before, but sometimes the idea of telling her something still arrived first. That evening she tried telling Nina herself. She said she'd nearly messaged her sister a thing Nina had said. Before Nina could reply, Ninja added that the tap in the book was still dripping, at page eighty.

Nina asked her to tell her about Elise over a cup of tea sometime. She pronounced the name without the careful quiet other people gave it. Ninja went home thinking over several stories she might like to tell. She found she had another five, each time she chose one.

That winter, an elderly man at work refused to put on the second slipper his daughter had brought him, because the right one was perfectly good and he was saving the left for when he needed it. Ninja sat with him while he told her that slow people were allowed to make quick decisions. She laughed and told him his daughter was going to come back and find him dressed like a man who lost an argument with a shoe shop. He smiled and let her help him with the other slipper.

There were plenty of moments like that on a shift. Getting caught laughing at the desk because Nina had suddenly remembered the end of her bakery joke. Eating a sandwich with another person who had exactly ten minutes and never minded if both of them looked out of the window the whole time. The busy work was one of the places Ninja could still feel most like herself.

It also made it easy to say yes before she checked what she had left. Once Nina found her in the supply room looking for a sheet that had been on somebody's desk all afternoon. It was nearly time to go home. Ninja had already agreed to come back earlier the next day. She stood there with the door half closed and admitted she hadn't had a quiet evening all week.

Nina offered to start an hour earlier herself. Ninja walked out into the corridor and started explaining why she could manage. She got to the part where she'd come in through the back door before anybody had started getting things ready. Then she stopped. She could hear the sentence as she was saying it and see what it was doing to the evening she'd been given. She agreed to let Nina take the hour.

At home she made tea and sat by the window with the paperback. The woman at the hotel had finally turned off the water to the tap. The next bit was about her walking round to look at the mountain for the first time in daylight. Ninja stayed in the chair for longer than she'd meant to. Outside, the star across the road came on. It was the fourth of February. She silently congratulated the family on their usual good judgement.

Over the following months she got better at leaving an evening empty once in a while. It was less easy to leave a whole day empty. She'd be midway through breakfast, feeling slightly adrift, and open her messages. Somebody would have a shelf to put up, or some shopping to do, or a train they needed meeting. She knew how to do all of those things. Sometimes she was truly glad to have been asked. What she missed, when the day was over, was being able to say what she'd wanted to do before she found out what everybody else needed.

She found Elise's old jacket hanging behind a summer coat when she cleaned out the hall cupboard. The pockets held two till receipts and a folded piece of paper with a number on it. She tried the jacket on. It still fit, although it sat differently on her shoulders than it had on Elise's. She put it on to go to the shop for milk. She was halfway up the road before she noticed her shoulders had loosened.

By the time she took the three days off, she'd been thinking in little bits about what to do with them for months. At first she thought she might ride out to the sea. She looked up the station with the green roof and discovered the line beyond it was closed all week. Once she'd stopped searching for that one journey, she found several other places she could go. Some were conveniently close to friends who would want to fit in lunch. One was near a walking path she had promised somebody she'd try.

Then there was a place she'd never heard of, down a small railway line the map barely thought important enough to show. It had a station, a market and a hotel room with its own chair. She left it open on her phone for a day. By the evening she found herself opening it without having to remember what she'd meant to look for.

The next morning, she got out her one small bag and checked that the zip still worked.

* * *

The train to Bellwether was two hours and a change at a junction where the station wall had been painted the colour of custard. Ninja chose it because the hotel had a chair by the window and a review that complained there was very little to do after six. The review was so annoyed about it that she booked the room immediately.

She took one bag. At the junction she bought a coffee and a packet of crisps, got on the second train and fell asleep with her forehead against the window. She woke up on the other side of a hill while water was running in long straight lines down the glass. The fields went right up to the railway. There were lights in the distance wherever a road came out between two hills.

The conductor told her she had one stop to go. When the train reached it, she was the only person who got off.

The hotel was five minutes from the station. It smelled faintly of wet coats and furniture polish. On the wall behind reception there were six keys, each on a painted wooden fish. The one for her room had a single eye in the middle of its head. Ninja picked it up and turned it over. The back was blank. Somebody had painted exactly as much of a fish as they felt like painting.

She wanted to send a photo to Elise. That happened sometimes. Her hand would move toward her phone before the rest of her caught up. She took the picture anyway. She was glad she'd seen the fish.

Upstairs, the strange little chair in the hotel photographs turned out to be wide enough to put her feet up on if she sat sideways. There was a clock on the wall that had lost its second hand. She discovered that she liked sitting there with her muddy shoes off and nobody expecting a reply. She did it until the light went.

At about six she found the crisps again and ate them by the window. A shopkeeper across the market took a crate of apples inside. She could see light from a bar farther down, where the road narrowed between the buildings. She decided one drink would be nice. She could bring her book, which meant if she felt strange sitting by herself she'd have somewhere to put her eyes.

Downstairs she stopped to inspect the duck-shaped umbrella stand. It had eyelashes, presumably added by the same person who'd economised on the fish's eyes. She took a photo of it too. Then she went out, turned up her collar and followed the light down the road.

The sign over the door said *The Crooked Lantern*. Through the glass she could see a woman behind the bar and two or three tables she could have to herself. Ninja stuck her book a little deeper into her bag, then put her shoulder against the door.

It didn't move.

She took a step back and tried again.

“Oh, come on.”

This time, it opened.
