# Elsewhere: The Hidden Valley
## Episode 1 — The Light in the Hall

The phone on Ninja’s desk rang twice before she picked it up.

“Front desk, this is Ninja.”

"Ninja, it's Priya. Listen, I know this is last minute—"

“You're calling in sick.”

"I'm not sick. Well, I am, but not the kind that—" Priya's breath caught. "My mother fell. She's fine, mostly, but she can't be alone tonight and my brother's stuck in traffic and I just—"

“Which shift?”

"Four to ten. I know you’ve got an early one tomorrow, but I’ll owe you. I'll take your Sunday double next week, I swear."

Ninja looked at the schedule pinned above her monitor. Her rent was due Friday. The numbers didn't care about Priya's mother or her brother's traffic. They didn't care about anything.

“Give me a minute.”

She put the phone down and pulled up the shift swap screen. Four to ten was the quiet shift, the one where she answered maybe six calls and spent the rest reading. It was also four hours of pay she'd already counted on. She'd counted on it the way she counted on everything, in a careful column that never quite added up.

Her phone buzzed again. A text from Elise: You free Saturday?

Ninja typed back: Maybe. Working something out.

Always working something out, Elise sent. That's the problem.

The desk phone rang. Ninja picked it up.

“Priya, I'll do it.”

"Really? Oh my god, Ninja, you're—"

“Don't thank me yet. I'm taking your Sunday double and your Thursday close.”

"Done. Both. Thank you. I mean it."

Ninja hung up and stared at the schedule. Now she had Priya's Sunday double and Priya's Thursday close, which meant she'd work six days straight, which meant she'd sleep through Saturday and Elise would call her a martyr and they'd have the same argument they always had. She picked up her phone.

Saturday's fine, she sent. What do you want to do?

Elise's reply came fast. Something ridiculous. I'll think of it.

They met at a diner near Elise's office, the kind with vinyl booths and a menu laminated so many times the pictures had gone soft. Elise was already there, her coat folded on the seat beside her, a cup of coffee in front of her that she hadn't touched. She looked like she'd been sitting there for a while.

“You're early.”

"You're late."

“By four minutes.”

"Four minutes is late." Elise pushed the coffee toward her. "I ordered you a tea. It's probably cold now."

Ninja wrapped her hands around the cup. It was cold. She drank it anyway.

“So what's the crisis? You've got your work face on.”

"I don't have a work face."

“You have a face you make when you're about to tell me something I won't like. It's very specific. It's in the eyebrows.”

Elise's eyebrows did something complicated. "I don't—" She stopped. "Okay, maybe. But it's not a crisis. It's just—" She glanced at her phone, face down on the table, and turned it over. The screen lit up. She pressed a button and set it back down.

Ninja watched her.

“You just declined a call.”

"I did."

“From work.”

"From work."

“On a Thursday night.”

"On a Thursday night." Elise picked up her menu and opened it, which meant she was done with the conversation. Ninja knew this because Elise had done the same thing since they were kids, hiding behind whatever was closest when she didn't want to talk. When they were twelve it had been a cereal box. Now it was a laminated menu with a picture of a club sandwich on the front.

“Elise.”

"I'm reading."

“You've read that menu a hundred times. You know the club sandwich is a lie.”

"It's not a lie. It's a suggestion."

“Elise.”

Elise put the menu down. "It's nothing. It's just—there's some stuff at work. Reorganization stuff. It's boring and I don't want to talk about it."

“Reorganization stuff that makes you decline calls at dinner.”

"Can we talk about something else? Please?" Elise's voice had gone quiet, the way it did when she was asking for something real. "I wanted to see you. I feel like I haven't seen you in weeks."

Ninja wanted to push. She always wanted to push, to take Elise's problems and carry them the way she carried everyone's problems, because carrying them was easier than sitting still. But Elise was looking at her with that quiet, steady expression, and Ninja knew what it meant. It meant stop. It meant I'll tell you when I'm ready. It meant this is my thing, not yours.

“Fine.”

“We'll talk about something else. But you're going to tell me eventually.”

"Probably."

“Definitely.”

Elise smiled, small and real. "Definitely. Now. Saturday. I have a plan."

Ninja groaned.

“Your plans involve museums.”

"This one doesn't."

“Your plans always involve museums. Or historical houses. Or that one time you made me walk a heritage trail in the rain.”

"You said you liked the heritage trail."

“I said I liked the part where we got pie after.”

Elise laughed, quick and bright, the sound Ninja had been chasing since they were kids. It was the same laugh, the one that made Ninja laugh too, the one that made everything feel possible. They'd made each other laugh fast, from the very beginning, from the first time their parents put them in the same room and Elise had said something so stupid and so funny that Ninja had snorted milk out of her nose. She'd been seven. Elise had been nine. They'd been inseparable ever since.

"This one's different," Elise said. "I promise. It's not educational at all."

“Now I'm suspicious.”

"You should be. Wear comfortable shoes."

“That's what you say before a heritage trail.”

"It's what I say before anything. Comfortable shoes are universal." Elise leaned forward. "I need you to do one thing for me this weekend."

“Besides the comfortable shoes?”

"Besides the comfortable shoes. I need you to not cancel."

Ninja opened her mouth. Elise held up a hand.

"I know you. I know you're already thinking about who's going to need you and what shift you're going to cover and how you're going to make it work. I know you took Priya's shift tonight because that's what you do. But Saturday is mine. I've had it on the calendar for three weeks and I'm not letting you give it away."

“I wasn't going to—”

"You were."

Ninja closed her mouth. She had been. She'd already been running through the schedule in her head, figuring out how to squeeze in a few extra hours before Saturday, how to make up the money she'd lost. It was automatic, the way breathing was automatic. She didn't know how to stop.

"One thing," Elise said. "That's all I'm asking. One Saturday. No work, no rescuing, no covering for anyone. Just us."

Ninja looked at her sister across the table, at the cold tea and the flipped-over phone and the menu she'd been hiding behind. Elise was holding something back. Ninja could feel it the way she could feel a storm coming, in the pressure behind her eyes. But Elise had asked for one thing, and Ninja could give her one thing. She could give her this.

“Okay.”

“Saturday's yours.”

Elise's whole face changed. The tension went out of her shoulders, the worry smoothed out of her forehead. She looked younger, suddenly, like the girl who'd taught Ninja how to ride a bike and then immediately crashed it into a hedge.

"Good," Elise said. "Because I already bought tickets."

“Tickets to what?”

"That's a surprise."

“Elise.”

"It's not a museum. I promise. It's not a heritage trail. It's not educational. It's just—" She paused, and for a second something flickered across her face, something Ninja couldn't read. "It's just something I want to do. With you."

Ninja wanted to ask. She wanted to push, to dig, to find out what Elise was hiding and fix it before it became a problem. But Elise was smiling, really smiling, and Ninja hadn't seen that smile in weeks. Months, maybe.

“Fine.”

“Saturday. Comfortable shoes. No cancelling.”

"No cancelling."

Ninja raised her cold tea. Elise raised her coffee. They clinked them together, and Elise laughed again, and Ninja laughed too, and for a moment the diner felt warm and bright and full of possibility.

Later, walking home, Ninja would think about the phone call Elise had declined, and the way her sister's face had flickered when she'd said tickets. She'd think about it the way she thought about everything, in a careful column that never quite added up. But for now she just walked, and the city hummed around her, and Saturday felt like something to hold onto.

* * *

The rain had settled into a steady, spiteful drizzle by the time they reached the corner shop bus stop. Ninja stood under the narrow plastic shelter with her hood up, watching water sheet off the roof in a thin curtain. Elise arrived a minute later, damp and annoyed, her coat dark at the shoulders.

“You brought an umbrella,” Elise said, eyeing the neat black stick in Ninja’s hand. “And you’re not even using it.”

“I like the rain.”

“You like looking like a drowned cat.”

“It’s a specific aesthetic.”

Elise laughed, and Ninja opened the umbrella, holding it over both of them. The shelter was cramped and smelled faintly of wet cardboard. A bus sighed past without stopping, tires hissing on the asphalt.

“Give me that,” Elise said, and took the umbrella. She tilted it back, spinning it slightly so the rain flicked off in a spiral. “I’m taller. I should be the one holding it.”

“That’s not how umbrellas work.”

“It’s how this umbrella works.”

Ninja laughed then, a real one, surprised out of her. She was still laughing when she noticed the man across the street, standing under the awning of a shuttered pharmacy with his phone raised. The screen was bright in the grey light. He was angled toward them.

She stopped laughing. The man lowered the phone, looked at it, then back at them. He was in his fifties, maybe, in a rain jacket that was too new. He didn’t look like a threat. He looked like someone who took photos of strangers because the world had taught him it was allowed.

“What?” Elise said, following her gaze.

“Nothing. Guy taking pictures.”

“Of us?”

“Of the bus stop, probably. Or the rain. People do that.”

Elise glanced over. The man was already walking away, phone in his pocket. “Weird.”

“It’s a weird city.”

Elise didn’t answer. She was looking at her own phone now, her thumb hovering over the screen. The umbrella tilted slightly, and rain caught Ninja’s shoulder.

“Hey. You’re leaking.”

“Sorry.” Elise straightened the umbrella, but her attention was elsewhere. She had that look, the one she got when she was chewing on something she didn’t want to spit out.

“What’s wrong?”

“Nothing. Work thing.”

“You’ve been doing the work thing face since we left the cafe.”

Elise sighed. She put her phone away and looked at Ninja properly. “I have to sign something tomorrow. A document. It’s not a big deal, it’s just—it’s a lie. A small one. My boss asked me to backdate a form. Say I reviewed something I didn’t.”

“Why?”

“Because he didn’t review it. He was on holiday. And now something’s come up and he needs it to look like it was checked.”

“What came up?”

“A family. The Hendersons. Their case got flagged. It’s fine, it’s just paperwork, but if the date’s wrong it looks bad for him.”

“And for you.”

“For me it’s nothing. I sign, it goes away.”

“You shouldn’t sign it.”

“It’s not your decision.”

The words landed hard. Ninja felt them in her chest, a small cold spot that spread. She stepped back, out from under the umbrella, into the rain.

“Right. Not my decision. Not my problem.”

“That’s not what I meant.”

“It’s what you said.”

“Ninja—”

“I’m going to walk.”

“The bus is coming.”

“I know.”

She walked. The rain was colder without the umbrella, but she didn’t care. She heard Elise call her name once, then stop. The bus arrived behind her, brakes squealing, and she kept walking.

Two hours later, sitting on the floor of her apartment with her back against the radiator, she called Elise.

It rang four times. Then, “Hello?”

“I’m sorry.”

A pause. “Me too.”

“You’re right. It’s your job. Your call.”

“I shouldn’t have said it like that.”

“You shouldn’t have. But I shouldn’t have walked off.”

“You were trying to help.”

“I was trying to decide for you. That’s not the same thing.”

Elise was quiet for a moment. “I signed it.”

“Okay.”

“It felt worse than I thought it would.”

“You can still tell someone.”

“I can’t. It’s done. It’s a small lie.”

“Small lies are still lies.”

“I know.”

They sat in silence, the phone warm against Ninja’s ear. Outside, the rain had stopped. The streetlights were coming on, one by one, orange and soft.

“I have to go,” Elise said. “I’m meeting someone.”

“Okay.”

“I’ll call you later.”

“Okay.”

She didn’t call later. Ninja didn’t expect her to. She went to work at the reception desk of the hotel on the corner, the one with the fake marble and the real plants that died every three weeks. The night shift was quiet. She checked in two guests, answered the phone four times, and restocked the minibar key cards.

At eleven, her phone buzzed. A voicemail from Elise. She listened to it in the back office, the door propped open so she could hear the bell.

“Hey, it’s me. I know it’s late. I just—I wanted to say I’m sorry again. About earlier. And I’m fine, everything’s fine, I just had a weird day. Call me when you’re safe. Okay. Bye.”

The message was ordinary. Almost too ordinary. Elise was not a person who said “call me when you’re safe” unless she meant it. Ninja listened to it twice. Then she called. It rang until voicemail picked up.

She tried again an hour later. Same thing.

She tried at two in the morning, when the lobby was empty and the night manager was asleep in his chair. Same thing.

She told herself it was nothing. Elise was a heavy sleeper. Her phone was on silent. She’d left it in the other room. There were a dozen reasons a person didn’t answer at two in the morning.

At four, the night manager woke up and told her to go home. She walked the six blocks to her apartment in the dark, the streets wet and empty. The building was quiet. She climbed the stairs to the third floor, unlocked her door, and stood in the hallway for a moment, listening to nothing.

She sat on the bottom stair with her coat still on. She had keys to her own flat and Elise’s on the same ring. Elise's was marked with a little dot of nail polish. It had started to come off under Ninja’s thumb. She thought about going over there. She made it halfway back to the pavement before she remembered Elise had said she was meeting someone. Walking into a dark flat would only tell her that a woman who had gone out hadn't come home.

Upstairs she put her phone on charge and set the volume as high as it would go. She had texted Elise twice to say she was safe. She sent one more.

I'm home. Ring whenever. I mean it.

She took her shoes off and left them side by side where she could find them if she needed to put them back on. In five minutes she was going to make tea. After that she would probably put the television on. She was making very small plans because she could still believe in each one for as long as it took to think of the next.

She didn't manage either of those things. Just before dawn, somebody knocked at the door.

* * *

Ninja went to the door in her coat. She opened it with the chain still on.

The man on the step was maybe fifty, in a raincoat too heavy for the weather, holding a leather folder against his chest like a shield. He had the look of someone who had practiced a sentence in the car and was now trying not to lose it.

“Ninja? I’m sorry to come so early. My name is Corrigan. I worked with Elise. On the Saturday project.”

Ninja took the chain off.

“Come in.”

He stood in the middle of her front room and didn’t sit. She didn’t offer twice. She leaned against the kitchen counter with her arms folded and waited.

“There was a fire,” he said. “Last night. A property down by the river. Elise was inside.”

The words landed in her chest like a dropped weight. She made herself breathe once, evenly, before she spoke.

“Where exactly?”

“The old boatyard end of the river road. There’s a shuttered club out front, the Riverside something. The building behind it. It’s been empty for years.”

“Who found her?”

“A patrol car saw smoke around eleven. The fire crew got in but the roof was already gone. She was in the back room.”

“Was anyone else there?”

Corrigan’s eyes shifted to the window. “Not that they’ve told me.”

“What have they told you?”

“that it looks accidental. That’s all they’ll tell me. They’re treating it as an accident pending the report.”

Ninja let the silence sit until he looked at her again.

“You came here at six in the morning to tell me it looks accidental. What does your gut tell you?”

“My gut isn’t evidence.” He said it too fast, like he’d been rehearsing that too. “I’m sorry. I didn’t know who else to tell. She talked about you. She said if anything ever went sideways, you were the one who’d ask the right questions.”

“Did she say why anything would go sideways?”

“No.”

“Did she seem frightened? In the last week?”

He hesitated. “She seemed careful. More careful than usual.”

Ninja uncrossed her arms.

“What was the project? The thing you worked on together.”

“Housing advocacy. Tenants’ rights. She was documenting a few buildings down there. It’s dull work, mostly.”

“Mostly.”

Corrigan closed the folder and held it against his chest again. “I have to go. I’m sorry. Truly.”

She walked him to the door and watched him down the path to a grey car with a dented rear panel. He didn’t look back.

Her aunt was in the kitchen with the radio on low, doing the crossword. Ninja stood in the doorway and watched her fill in a word before she spoke.

“Aunt Mari. Put the pen down.”

The old woman looked up, read her face, and put the pen down.

Ninja told her plainly. Elise, a fire, the building by the river. She didn’t soften it and she didn’t dress it up. Her aunt listened with her hands flat on the table, and when Ninja finished, she sat very still and then said, “She was here on Thursday. She ate my soup and she told me about a man who wouldn’t fix a boiler.” Her voice was steady in a way that cost her something. “She was going to come back Sunday for the market.”

“I know.”

“Who does this?”

“I don’t know yet.”

Her aunt picked up the pen and set it down again without writing anything. “You find out.”

“I’m going to.”

The river road in daylight was a strip of shuttered units and cracked tarmac, gulls on the mud below the wall. The club front was exactly as Corrigan had described, a wide blank face with a name board stripped down to its bolts and a metal shutter pulled across the door. Behind it, the building was a black skeleton, the roof collapsed inward, the windows blown out. Tape ran around the perimeter. A fire investigation van sat idle, and two men in hi-vis were folding a ladder.

Ninja stood at the tape and looked. The smell was still there, wet charcoal and something sweeter underneath. She counted the windows, the doors, the alley that ran along the side toward the water. She took photos on her phone, slow and methodical, the way she’d been taught to look at a room.

On the far fence, a sheet of paper flapped under a plastic sleeve. She walked the long way around to it.

MISSING. A photo of a man in his forties, a name, two phone numbers. Someone had written across the bottom in marker, in a different hand, a date three weeks old.

A security man came around the corner of the fence, unhurried, and stopped beside her. He was young and polite and he did not look at her face.

“Sorry, that’s not meant to be up.” He reached past her and worked the paper out of the sleeve. “Old notices. We’re supposed to clear them.”

“Who’s he?”

“No idea. Before my time.” He folded the paper and put it in his jacket pocket, and then he did look at her, briefly, and his politeness had a lid on it. “You shouldn’t be past the tape.”

“I’m not past it.”

“Best move along anyway.” He waited until she did.

The funeral was on a Thursday at a crematorium on the north side, a low brick building with a car park that smelled of cut grass. Ninja stood at the back with her aunt’s hand tucked under her arm. The room held maybe forty people. Corrigan was there in the same raincoat. A few faces she recognised from Elise’s building, neighbours, a woman from the tenants’ group.

She watched the door.

Halfway through, a man slipped in and stood against the wall with his hands behind his back. He was thin, grey at the temples, in a good coat that didn’t fit the room. He didn’t look at the coffin. He looked at the people.

When the service ended and the room began to move, he was already at the door. Ninja let go of her aunt’s arm and went after him. She caught him on the path by the flower beds.

“You knew her.”

He stopped. Up close his face was tired and careful. “I’m sorry for your loss.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the only one I have.” He glanced past her, at the doors, at the people coming out. Something changed in his expression, a door closing. “I can’t. Not here.”

“Then somewhere else. Give me a number.”

“I can’t.” He said it again, quieter, and this time he was looking at a man in the car park, a broad man in a dark jacket who was talking to Corrigan and not looking at any of them. The grey-haired man turned his collar up. “I’m sorry. Truly sorry.” He walked away fast, hands in his pockets, and got into a small car and drove off before she could think of the right thing to say.

She stood on the path with the smell of cut grass and the sound of the doors closing behind her.

That night she sat at her own table with the printed report Corrigan had left in her letterbox that afternoon, a thin stack of pages with the fire investigator’s preliminary findings. She read it twice.

The finding was undetermined but leaning accidental. Old wiring. No accelerant detected. The report noted that the deceased appeared to have entered the premises voluntarily, that the front shutter had been forced from inside out, that there was no sign of a struggle.

Voluntarily. She read the word until it stopped meaning anything.

She got up and took the note from the drawer. Elise’s handwriting, blue ink on a torn corner of lined paper, three lines. Saturday still on. Bring the folder, not the copies. Don’t call the office line.

Ninja put it flat on the table under the lamp. A note about Saturday. A meeting. A folder. It wasn’t a riddle and it wasn’t a clue in some detective story. It was Elise being careful, the way Corrigan had said, the way she was always careful, and someone had decided that careful meant something.

She read the report one more time and found the line about the front shutter. Forced from inside out. She thought about the back room, and the alley, and the water.

Then she took out her phone and photographed every page of the report, and the note beside it, and she wrote one line in her own notebook, in pencil, and underlined it twice.

Why did she go in the front?

She closed the notebook and put it in her pocket and left the lamp on, and the question stayed open in the room behind her like a door she had chosen not to shut.

* * *

The envelope had been forwarded twice before it reached the reception desk, her name misspelled in the county's database, and Ninja opened it on her lunch break with a plastic fork still in her hand. The accident report was three pages of boilerplate and one paragraph that made her stop chewing. It said Elise had been alone in the vehicle. It said no call had been placed from her phone in the twenty minutes before impact.

Ninja knew better. She had the voicemail saved, had listened to it so many times the audio had gone soft and underwater, but the words were still there. Elise saying she was pulling over because some guy in a truck was riding her bumper, and could Ninja please not tell Aunt Mari because she'd worry, and then a laugh, and then the message ended. The timestamp was 6:41 p.m. The report put the crash at 6:52. Eleven minutes. Elise had called her eleven minutes before she died and the report said she hadn't called anyone.

She finished her sandwich. She went back to the phones. A woman named Delia Marsh wanted a package redirected and a man named Kwan wanted to know if the building had a freight elevator, and Ninja answered both and then opened her email and wrote to the public investigator's office, attaching the voicemail file and the report number and a single paragraph explaining the discrepancy. She did not cry. She had gotten good at not crying at work.

The reply came three days later. The case remained closed. The investigator, a Mr. Oyelaran, wrote that the report reflected information available at the time and that no further review was warranted. Ninja read it twice. Then she called the office and asked for him by name.

"He's not available," the receptionist said.

“Then I'd like to schedule a meeting. I have a voicemail that contradicts page two of your report.”

"I can take a message."

Ninja left her number. She left it again the following week. The third time, the receptionist recognized her voice and said, before Ninja could speak, "Ma'am, the case is closed. There's nothing to schedule."

“I'm not asking for the case to be reopened. I'm asking for the record to be corrected. There's a difference.”

"I'll pass that along."

They didn't pass it along. She knew they wouldn't. She sat in her car in the parking garage after work and listened to the voicemail one more time, Elise's voice saying the truck was too close, and then she deleted the call log from her phone, not the voicemail, just the log, because she didn't need to keep proving she'd tried. The report would say what it said. She had told the truth to the people whose job it was to write it down, and they had chosen the tidier version. That was the whole of it. She drove home and made soup and called Aunt Mari and did not mention any of it.

The favour came three years later, on a Saturday in October, from a woman named Priya who worked two floors up and who Ninja liked well enough to feel guilty about saying no.

"It's just the whole weekend," Priya said, leaning on the reception desk with both elbows. "My cousin's wedding, and the venue coordinator is a nightmare, and I told her I'd have someone there at eight to let the florist in. You're the most reliable person I know. I'll pay you."

Ninja had plans. Not big plans. She had a reservation at the climbing gym for ten, and a book she'd been saving, and a vague intention to finally sort the closet. But the reservation was hers, and the morning was hers, and she heard herself start to say yes, the way she always did, because Elise used to say Ninja would agree to hold up the walls of a burning building if someone asked nicely.

She stopped. Elise wasn't there to say it. Elise had been dead for four years, and the voice in her head that said don't be a doormat was just her own voice now, wearing her sister's cadence.

“I can't.”

Priya blinked. "Oh. Okay. It's just—"

"I know. I'm sorry. But I can't do the whole weekend."

"Could you do Saturday morning? Just the florist?"

Ninja looked at the reservation confirmation on her phone. Ten o'clock. She could let the florist in at eight and still make the gym if she left by nine-thirty, and she opened her mouth to offer exactly that, and then closed it.

“No.”

“I really can't. I hope the wedding's beautiful.”

Priya straightened up and said, "Sure. Yeah. No problem," in a tone that meant it was a small problem, and walked off toward the elevators. Ninja watched her go and felt the old pull, the urge to chase her down and fix it, and she sat with the feeling until it passed. It took about ninety seconds.

She went to the climbing gym. She climbed until her forearms shook, and then she sat in the sauna for twenty minutes, and then she walked to the bookstore on Clement and bought the book she'd been saving, full price, no coupon. She ate a late lunch at the counter of a noodle place she'd walked past a hundred times and never entered. The broth was too salty. She ate all of it. She got home at six and sorted the closet, and it turned out she didn't need the whole weekend for that, just an hour and a podcast. She called Aunt Mari that night and said, "I said no to something today," and Aunt Mari said, "Good," and that was that.

The train left at 7:40 on a Tuesday morning, and Ninja had a window seat and a paper cup of coffee that was already going cold. Aunt Mari had driven her to the station, which was unnecessary and also exactly what she'd wanted.

"Bellwether," Aunt Mari said in the car. "I've never heard of it."

“That's the point. I picked it because I hadn't heard of it either.”

"And there's a market?"

“Right under the room. The woman on the phone said I'd hear the vendors setting up at dawn. She seemed to think that was a selling point.”

Aunt Mari laughed. She had Elise's laugh, or Elise had had hers, Ninja could never keep it straight. "You'll be up at dawn, then."

Ninja watched the road.

“I'll be up at dawn.”

“It's four days. I just want to walk around somewhere I don't know and not answer the desk phone.”

"You've earned it. You know that, right?"

“I know it.”

Aunt Mari pulled up to the drop-off and put the car in park and didn't say anything for a moment. Then she said, "Your sister would have made fun of you for picking a town with a market."

“She would have. She'd have said it was a grandma vacation.”

"She'd have come with you and complained the whole time."

Ninja smiled at the windshield.

“She'd have loved it.”

They sat there. A porter went past with a cart. Aunt Mari reached over and squeezed Ninja's hand, and Ninja squeezed back, and then she got out with her bag and waved from the doors and went inside to find her platform.

The train pulled out at 7:40 exactly. Ninja watched the city go thin and then go green, warehouses and then fields, and she thought about Elise for a while, the way she did on trains, without needing to do anything about it. Elise was dead. The report still said what it said. Priya had probably found someone else for the florist. None of it was a problem she had to solve this morning.

The conductor came through and punched her ticket, and Ninja put her head against the glass and closed her eyes. Somewhere ahead was a town she'd never seen, with a market under her window and four days that belonged to nobody but her.

* * *

The rain had followed her off the train and up the hill into Bellwether, and it stayed with her while she found the hotel, a narrow place wedged between a bakery and a shuttered shop, its sign swinging a one-eyed fish. The woman at the desk had a key ready before Ninja said a word, Room Six, second floor, stairs at the back. Ninja paid cash for two nights, took the key, and climbed.

The room was small and clean, a bed, a chair, a window that looked down on the market square, wet stone shining under the lamps. She put her bag on the chair and stood at the window a while, watching the rain move across the square in sheets, the last of the market stalls folding up, a dog waiting patiently outside a door. Nobody she knew. No faces that meant anything. That was the point of a place like this, and she had chosen it for exactly that reason, but standing there she felt the quiet settle on her like a coat that didn't fit.

She showered, changed, and sat on the bed with nothing to do. No screen, no signal worth chasing, no one to report to. The evening stretched out in front of her, and she waited for something to happen, some knock, some message, some reason to move. Nothing came. The rain kept on. The market emptied. She watched the square go dark and the lamps come up one by one, and she understood that nothing was going to happen unless she made it happen.

She took a picture of the empty square. She opened the list of people she could send it to, and for a split second she went looking for Elise’s name. It came so naturally that it took her longer than it should have to put her phone away.

She had come all this way so she could decide what to do with an evening. She thought about how Elise used to look up from her own phone when Ninja walked into a room. It was possible to miss that for the rest of her life. It was also possible to go downstairs and have a drink.

The Crooked Lantern sat on the far side of the market, its windows fogged and warm, a low murmur of voices spilling out each time the door swung. Ninja crossed the square with her collar up and pushed inside. The place was half full, a good fire, a long bar, a few tables of locals who looked up and then didn't. She took a stool near the window and put her paperback on the bar and set her hands on the wood.

The bartender was a broad woman with grey hair pinned back and a cloth over her shoulder, and she came down the bar drying a glass. "What'll it be?"

“Whatever's local.”

“And hot, if you've got it.”

"We've got a cider that'll put hair on your chest," the woman said. "Ada. I'm Ada. You're at the fish, aren't you. Room Six."

Ninja looked at her.

“How'd you know that?”

"Small village," Ada said. "And you've got the look of someone who just got off the train." She poured the cider and set it down. "First night's always the strangest. Give it a day."

The cider was hot and sharp and good. Ninja wrapped her hands around the glass and let the room come into focus. Two men at a table arguing about a fence. A woman laughing at something on her phone. An old man asleep in the corner with his hat over his face. Ordinary. Warm. The kind of place where nothing happened and nobody minded.

She heard the talk before she placed where it came from, two women at the end of the bar, heads together, voices carrying the way voices do after a second drink. One of them was saying something about a man who'd come back to the village after years away, and the other one was saying he'd come back with a dog and no explanation, and the first one said that was the only kind of man worth anything, and they both laughed. Ninja smiled into her cider. She didn't know what it was about the talk that made her stay, the ordinary meanness of it, the way the village chewed over its own, but it was the first thing all evening that felt like it was happening on purpose.

Ada came back down the bar with a fresh glass, and she set it down in front of Ninja without a word. Whiskey. Amber, neat, one finger of it, and Ninja hadn't ordered it.

“I didn't ask for this.”

"No," Ada said. She picked up her cloth and tipped her head toward the far end of the bar, slow and deliberate, the way you point without pointing. "He did."

Ninja looked up. The man at the end of the bar was standing with one hand on the back of his chair. Their eyes met.
