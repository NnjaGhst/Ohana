# Elsewhere: The Hidden Valley
## Episode 1 — Before Bellwether

On the morning somebody took her photograph, Ninja had lent Elise the good umbrella and spent the entire bus ride regretting it.

It was the sort of umbrella that fit two people without either having to make a case for their left shoulder. Elise had borrowed it to walk a neighbour to a doctor's appointment. By the time Ninja got off work, the rain had come down sideways and the neighbour was home dry and warm. Ninja, who had come straight from a shift and still had a pen clipped to the pocket of her shirt, was standing outside a corner shop in wet socks.

Elise reached into the paper bag Ninja was holding and stole a chip.

“You could say thank you for buying dinner,” Ninja said.

“I could. Do you want me to say it with my mouth full?”

Ninja bumped her with an elbow. A bus went by and hit a puddle big enough to make somebody on the far side of the shelter swear. The splash stopped short of both sisters. Ninja looked around and laughed anyway.

There was a man across the road with a phone in his hand. When the bus pulled away and Ninja turned back, he was gone.

Upstairs at Ninja's flat, they ate dinner on the sofa. Elise wanted to hear how the shift had gone. Ninja gave her the version she gave everyone, which was that it had been fine and she'd got everything done. Elise went and got two plates and brought them back. By the time she'd tipped out the chips, Ninja was telling her about the thing she'd had to go into the supply cupboard to cry about.

That was how it worked with Elise. She could wait through the first answer and start eating without making you feel she'd missed it. And when the second answer came out, she'd already put the salt where you could reach it.

Before she left, Elise borrowed the umbrella again.

“I'm starting to think it was yours all along,” Ninja said.

“That's the first sensible thing you've said all day.”

* * *

Hex had a table of his own at a café behind a furniture warehouse. The woman who ran it put his tea down and asked if he'd eaten. He always said yes. She always left two slices of toast.

On most days, if somebody from the crew found him there, they sat facing him and ordered nothing. They gave him a time and a place. He might be moving a van from one garage to another, or sitting in the back of a restaurant until somebody came for a bag. Once they paid him to stand at a hospital vending machine for two hours and tell them whether a man in a blue coat showed up. He'd started asking fewer questions after that job. The man had arrived holding a helium balloon.

The first job had covered a month's rent. Within a year, the crew knew where Hex lived, who he spoke to, and how to phrase a request so he understood it had already been accepted. He got good at looking bored when someone was trying to scare him. The men liked that about him. Some days he got all the way home before he realised he was still wearing the same expression.

Then, on a Thursday, they gave him a folder and told him to collect an envelope at a closed social club beside the river. A woman called Elise had arranged to deliver it. The crew said it held a copy of their accounts.

At the back of the folder somebody had clipped a photograph upside down. Hex turned it around.

A woman was laughing outside a corner shop. She held a paper bag tucked under one arm. Beneath the photo, somebody had typed her name, Ninja, and a short instruction to call immediately if she turned up at the handoff.

“Who's Ninja?” he asked.

“Elise's sister. She probably won't be there.”

“What's it got to do with her?”

The man took a sip of Hex's tea. “Call if she is.”

When he went out to answer his phone, Hex took the photograph off the clip and slid it inside his coat. They already had her name and knew where to look for her. He wanted her to see the picture someday and know how close the man who took it had been. He still had no idea when he'd get a chance to show her.

* * *

Elise had expected to meet one man at the social club. Hex understood that from the way she stood up when she saw him and sat straight back down when the door opened again.

There was a paper cup by her hand, and someone had set a chair under the coat hooks where she could see the river. Hex asked her if she had the envelope.

“I had it,” she said. “I brought it down this morning. After that somebody rang me and read out my sister's address.”

Hex pulled the empty chair out and sat where he could see the doorway. “Is Ninja home?”

Elise looked at him sharply. “You know her?”

“I saw her picture today.”

Elise took in a breath. “She works late on Thursdays. I was going to go round when she got back.”

There were two men in the hall by then. One was the man from the café. He told Hex to go down to the car and wait. Hex stood, putting himself between him and Elise. The other man moved far enough into the room that Hex could see his face. He'd been there when Hex was first told to stop asking questions.

Hex walked out. He got down the first flight of stairs with his hands open at his sides. Halfway down the second, he heard Elise's chair scrape hard against the floor.

He turned back.

Later, he would remember the landing light and how it took a second to come on after he hit the switch. He would remember saying Elise's name through a closed door. When it opened, he'd already missed the few seconds in which he could have done anything. He stayed until he heard someone else coming up the stairs and understood that the crew was perfectly willing to leave him in that room with the people who'd died.

He went back to the warehouse café just before dawn. The shutters were down. He stood in front of them long enough to think about waking the owner, and what kind of trouble that would lay at her door. Then he took his hand off the shutter and walked to the station.

Later that morning he saw a photo of the social club on a television at the station. Three people had died. The report said the police were still contacting families.

Someone from the crew called twice. On the third call he answered. The man asked if he'd said anything to the police and then told him to come home. Hex had never told him to think of that flat as home. He hung up, bought a ticket with cash and stood out on the platform until the train came.

* * *

A police officer came to Ninja's flat before lunch.

She'd been waiting for Elise to come back with the umbrella. She remembered that for a long time afterward. If she tried to remember what the officer said first, all she could see was the empty hook in the hallway where she used to hang it.

Elise was dead. It had happened at a club by the river. There had been other people there. Several times, Ninja thought she understood what she'd been told, only to hear herself ask the officer to say one part again.

She made tea after the officer left. She stood at the counter with two mugs. One was the mug Elise used because the handle on it was big and she could get all four fingers through. Ninja poured water into it anyway. Around four o'clock she tipped both mugs away.

In the weeks that followed she discovered there were people who would hear the worst sentence of her life and then ask her to spell it. She could do that. She could give addresses and find a birth certificate. She could tell an aunt the date of the funeral and apologise to a cousin for forgetting to tell him first. By evening she was too tired to take a clean pillowcase out of the drawer, which was one of the few things it would have made sense to ask someone to do for her.

When she went back to work, she started saying yes again. Yes, she could cover for somebody. Yes, she could stay until the next person came in. Those were jobs with endings. At home there was always something else that Elise would never hear about.

Years passed. Ninja made changes that helped. She took the long way home on days when she needed twenty minutes of air. She bought a new lamp for the hallway and left it on when she went out in the dark. On good days she was still the person who laughed at other people's bad luck with puddles. On bad ones she'd get in, set her keys down and wish there were somebody in the next room who knew how to wait for the second answer.

* * *

The first night Hex spent in Bellwether, he paid for a room over the Crooked Lantern. The woman behind the bar asked for his name. He said Elias Ward. He'd practised it quietly on the train. It still sounded like the name of somebody he was looking for.

Her name was Ada. She gave him a key on a bit of blue cord and told him the passenger ferry was loud in the morning. It took him until lunchtime the next day to find out there hadn't been a morning ferry in eleven years.

“You looked like you needed the sleep,” she said when he asked her about it. “Thought if I told you the place would be making noise, you might get on with it.”

Her bar clock had been running six minutes fast. He asked for a screwdriver. As a kid he'd fixed clocks with an uncle who let him put the ones he'd broken first straight back on the bench and try again. Hex took the clock upstairs after breakfast and brought it down at three. Ada checked it against the radio twice. On the third day somebody came in carrying a clock with a handle shaped like a horse and asked if the new man could take a look.

He stayed. He rented a little shop with a stove that smoked if he put wet wood in it and got himself a second coat. He learned from Ada that if he didn't want to talk about where he came from, he could just ask where a Bellwether person had come from. They'd usually tell him enough about their grandparents for the subject to change on its own.

At night he put the photograph in the back of a drawer with his old keys. He took it out more often than he liked to admit. At first he wanted to know whether he'd missed some clue in it, something the crew had seen that he could make sense of. Eventually he could remember what Ninja looked like without opening the drawer. He remembered the paper bag, the little dip in her chin, how freely she was laughing. He wondered what had become of the laugh since Elise died.

Once he wrote a letter that began, 'I was there.' He got as far as that and put the pen down. If he sent it, she'd have a stranger's confession to carry around and nobody she could safely ask about it. He put the paper under the photograph and started the clock in the window to hear another sound.

* * *

Almost five years after that rainy evening at the corner shop, Ninja put in for three days off. She had a week's worth of plans for those three days, most of them involving other people's errands. Then she came home on a Tuesday and found herself still standing in her work shoes at eight o'clock with the fridge door open, too tired to choose between butter and the jar of mustard.

She took the next available train ticket that finished somewhere with a hotel. She picked Bellwether because the room in the photo had a chair by a window, and the person who'd written the last review had complained that there was nothing to do after six.

When she arrived it was raining, which seemed about right. The hotel had a wooden fish on every room key. Hers had one eye painted in the middle. She took it up to room six, put her bag on the strange little chair by the window and sat on the bed in her coat.

The first thing she wanted to do was take a picture of the fish and send it to Elise. That still happened. It could be a tiny, ordinary thing and her hand would move toward her phone before the rest of her caught up.

This time she took the picture anyway.

Across the market, Hex shut up the clock shop, turned off the stove and carried a clock over to Ada. He had finished it a week earlier and had walked halfway to the bar with it twice. Each time he'd turned back because something about the timing bothered him. This evening it had struck six as the radio announcer said six. He was finally prepared to let somebody else have it.

Ada took a beer out of the fridge and set it in front of him. “Will you take that clock back if I move the hands?”

“It might be better if I didn't know.”

She looked at him until he laughed. He paid for the beer and went to the end of the bar, where his usual seat faced both the window and the door. Then he stopped and took the chair on the other side of the table. There'd been times in the last five years when he could go a whole afternoon without thinking about the crew. He was trying little things to see if he could make those afternoons longer.

* * *

Ninja discovered a packet of crisps in the bottom of her bag. She ate them standing by the window. Across the market a shopkeeper came outside to pull in a crate of apples. It looked like an easy place to go for a walk. It felt harder to take her coat off and stay in that room all evening.

At the bottom of the hotel stairs there was an umbrella stand in the shape of a duck. Someone had given it eyelashes. Ninja sent a picture of it to no one and went outside.

She heard the Crooked Lantern before she saw it. The rain had thinned enough that she could hear someone move a chair on the other side of the wall. She stood under the sign and looked through the glass. There were a few people at the bar, a man trying to fold a newspaper across his knee, and a table she could have to herself. That was all she wanted. A drink and the sound of people talking about things she didn't need to fix.

Inside, Hex turned his beer glass so the wet ring on the table would dry.

He had just thought that he might get through the whole drink this time without moving his chair back when somebody pushed on the door. It stuck in wet weather. Everybody from Bellwether knew to lift the handle a little first.

He heard another shove.

“Oh, come on,” a woman said from the other side.

The latch came free.

In the next episode, Ninja orders a whiskey. Hex sees the woman from the photograph walk straight past him, and has about ten seconds to decide whether he’s going to run again.
