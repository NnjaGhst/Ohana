# Elsewhere — Episode 1: The Photograph
## Full pilot manuscript — draft

### Prologue — The Photograph

Five years earlier, the rain had been falling for three days.

At 2:17 in the morning, Hex sat in a black car outside a railway station, watching water slide down the windscreen. He was thirty-two, tired, and accustomed to being sent places without knowing why.

His phone lit up.

PLATFORM NINE. LOCKER FORTY-THREE. SIX MINUTES.

“Six minutes,” he muttered. “How generous.”

He stepped into the rain and crossed the nearly empty concourse. The station smelled of wet concrete, old coffee, and electricity. Locker forty-three opened with a code sent to his phone. Inside was a brown envelope, unmarked except for black tape.

He took it into an empty waiting room and broke the seal.

OPERATION GLASSHOUSE.

Intercept an encrypted archive taken by a courier named Elise Varen. Recover the archive. Do not engage civilian contacts.

Six photographs were enclosed. The last showed a woman outside a café: pink-purple hair, dark clothes, a septum ring, and an expression halfway between amusement and defiance.

Hex looked at that picture longer than he intended.

CIVILIAN REFERENCE N-17. NON-OPERATIONAL. DIRECT CONTACT PROHIBITED.

Her name was Ninja.

“And what are you doing in this mess?” he murmured.

His phone rang. Mara, his handler.

“You have the package?”

“Unfortunately. Why is a civilian in my operational file?”

“She’s a reference point. Nothing more.”

“That's a strange answer.”

“It’s the only one you need. Recover the archive, Hex. No improvising.”

“You say that like it’s a personality defect.”

“On you, it is.”

The call ended.

Hex returned the photograph to its envelope. He had no idea that this woman he had never met would become the one face he could not forget.

### Chapter One — The Night Everything Went Wrong

The industrial compound stood beyond the city, where roads dissolved into loading bays and abandoned warehouses.

Hex arrived before dawn. His assignment was supposed to be simple: find Elise Varen, intercept the archive, leave before anyone noticed.

Simple was a word the Directorate used when it didn't want questions.

From the service road, Hex studied the guards. They weren't positioned to keep intruders out. They were positioned to keep people inside.

“Mara,” he said into his earpiece. “Your security layout doesn't match the briefing.”

“Proceed.”

“There are armed teams at both exits.”

“Containment.”

“Containment of what?”

“Recover the archive.”

Then the first shot rang out.

Hex moved toward the building instead of away. Inside, Elise Varen stumbled through a corridor, clutching a case against her chest. She saw him, stopped, and seemed to recognise what his presence meant. Her phone screen lit up in her hand. A message from **Ninja** read: *Please just call me when you're safe. I love you.* Hex noticed the name—the woman from the photograph—before Elise shoved the phone into her pocket.

“You're too late,” she said.

A second shot struck the wall. Hex pulled her behind a steel support.

“Who are those men?”

Elise looked at him as though he had asked whether water was wet. “Yours.”

The fire started moments later. An explosion shook the windows. Hex heard voices beyond a locked steel door and fought the handle until the metal burned his palms.

“Mara! There are civilians trapped in here!”

“Stand down. The archive is the priority.”

“People are dying.”

“I gave you an order.”

He stopped answering her.

He got two people out through a loading entrance. When he turned back, flames had swallowed the corridor. Elise was dead. Hex found her phone on the floor near the door, its cracked screen still showing her sister's message. He couldn't bring himself to leave it behind. Others were missing. Six people would ultimately be confirmed killed.

Hex stood outside in the cold dawn, coughing smoke, and understood that the operation had never been an interception. It had been a cleanup.

His earpiece crackled.

“Hex. Return for debrief.”

He looked toward the burning warehouse.

“No.”

A pause.

“Do not make this worse.”

“You already did.”

He pulled the earpiece out and crushed it under his boot.

Within hours, the Directorate classified him as compromised and authorised his recovery. He knew what that word meant.

Hex disappeared with the envelope still in his coat.

### Chapter Two — The Woman Who Didn't Know

Ninja had never heard the words Operation Glasshouse.

She had heard of Elise Varen, of course. Elise was her older sister.

Elise was the one who remembered birthdays, called at inconvenient hours, and turned up with groceries when Ninja insisted she was perfectly capable of looking after herself. She was also the person Ninja trusted with the things she didn't tell anyone else.

The last time they spoke, Elise had sounded distracted.

“Something's wrong at work,” Elise admitted.

“Then leave it for tomorrow.”

“I can't. I need to sort it out.”

“Elise.”

“I know. I know. I'll call you when I'm done.”

“Promise?”

“Promise. Love you, trouble.”

“Love you too.”

Ninja sent one more message later that night: *Please just call me when you're safe. I love you.*

Elise never replied.

The police told Ninja her sister had died in an industrial warehouse fire. There had been confusion, they said. An accident, they said. The investigation was ongoing, they said, until it suddenly wasn't.

Ninja remembered almost nothing about the first days afterward except the fluorescent light in the police station, the weight of Elise's spare keys in her palm, and how people kept asking whether there was someone they could call for her.

There was Elise, she wanted to say.

But there wasn't anymore.

The funeral was small. Ninja stood through it with her jaw clenched and her hands curled around a folded photograph. Elise was laughing in it, caught mid-sentence. Ninja kept expecting to hear that laugh from somewhere behind her.

Then the paperwork started.

An account was frozen. Employment records were flagged. A complaint appeared under Ninja's name, citing transactions she had never authorised. When she tried to prove the records were wrong, the supporting documents were missing. Each person she called sent her to someone else.

Elise had once helped her correct a routine employment-record error. Ninja didn't know those legitimate records had later been copied and used to disguise a financial route connected to the archive Elise carried. When the Directorate erased the route, it left Ninja looking suspicious—and without the evidence she needed to defend herself.

Her work became harder. Money grew tight. People who had once been warm became careful about what they said around her. Every new letter felt like another accusation.

She was a healthcare worker. She could stay calm for other people in a crisis. But there was no training for losing the person who knew you best and then being treated as though you had done something wrong.

At home, she sometimes reached for her phone to send Elise a ridiculous picture or complain about a terrible day. Her thumb would find the familiar contact before she remembered.

On one such evening, Ninja sat at the kitchen table surrounded by letters. Her black headphones lay beside a cold cup of coffee.

“Right,” she told the paperwork, her voice unsteady. “Which one of you is ruining my life today?”

She almost heard Elise answer: *All of them, apparently. Put the kettle on.*

That was when Ninja cried. Not at the funeral. Not in the police station. At the kitchen table, because there was nobody left who would answer her stupid jokes in exactly the right way.

Years later, Ninja would rebuild a life around the absence. She would learn to laugh again, travel alone, and refuse to let the Directorate's lies define her.

But she would never believe the fire was simply an accident.

And somewhere far away, a man named Hex carried a photograph of Ninja, the memory of Elise's final moments, and the knowledge that he had failed to stop what happened.

They were strangers living on opposite sides of the same terrible night.

Neither knew their paths were moving toward each other.


### Chapter Three — The Man Who Disappeared

By sunrise, the Directorate had begun looking for Hex.

They searched the flat he had not returned to. They watched his bank accounts, his old contacts, the roads leaving the city. Every system he had once relied on was suddenly turned against him.

Hex knew the procedure. He had helped design parts of it.

He drove a stolen delivery van through a tunnel and left it beneath an overpass. He changed clothes in a service-station bathroom, cut the identification tag from his coat, and took the first bus whose destination meant nothing to him.

He was carrying three things: a little cash, the envelope from locker forty-three, and Elise Varen's damaged phone.

He hadn't intended to take the phone. He had picked it up while searching for survivors. By the time he noticed it in his pocket, he was already running.

Its screen was cracked like a spiderweb. The last message still glowed faintly when he pressed the button.

Please just call me when you're safe. I love you.

Ninja.

He stared at the words until the screen went dark.

In the motel where he stopped that evening, the wallpaper peeled at the corners and the heater rattled like loose cutlery. He laid the envelope on the bed. Six photographs. One woman's face.

His own phone vibrated. An unknown number.

He let it ring.

It rang again.

Finally he answered.

“You're making this harder than it needs to be,” Mara said.

“Six people are dead.”

“You don't know the whole situation.”

“I know who locked the doors.”

Silence.

“Mara, tell me Elise wasn't meant to die.”

“I can't discuss operational details over an unsecured line.”

He laughed once, without humour. “That's an answer.”

“Come in. We can protect you.”

“From the people you sent after me?”

“Hex.”

For the first time since he'd known her, Mara sounded tired rather than commanding.

“Walk away from this,” she said. “You can still have a life.”

He looked at the photograph on the bed, at the woman whose sister had sent a message that would never be answered.

“Not the one I had.”

He ended the call and broke the phone's battery connection.

That night he slept for forty minutes, woke convinced someone was outside the door, and spent the rest of the hours listening to passing cars.

In the morning he bought a paper map.

For the first time in his adult life, nobody was telling him where to go.

It was terrifying.

### Chapter Four — Bellwether

Bellwether sat between steep hills and dark pines, a village of slate roofs, wet stone, and narrow roads that curled away into the mountains.

Hex arrived there in late autumn, after weeks of moving through places he never meant to remember.

He chose the village for practical reasons. It was small. It had poor reception in the surrounding forest. And a notice outside a forestry workshop said HELP WANTED, MECHANICAL EXPERIENCE PREFERRED.

The man behind the workshop desk was called Tomas. His hair was grey, his hands were permanently stained with oil, and he studied Hex as though trying to decide whether the stranger was trouble.

“Name?”

Hex had rehearsed the answer on the bus.

“Elias Ward.”

“Ever repaired a chainsaw?”

“Yes.”

“Tractor?”

“Yes.”

“Coffee machine?”

Hex hesitated. “Probably.”

Tomas pointed to a dead machine on the counter. “Then start there. If you can make that thing work, I'll believe the rest.”

It took Hex nineteen minutes.

Tomas drank the first cup, made a face, and said, “Tastes awful. You're hired.”

For a while, Elias Ward lived like a man holding his breath.

He rented a small room above an empty shop. He woke at every noise, memorised the exits of every building, and never sat with his back to a door.

At the workshop, Tomas didn't ask why he had no references. He asked why Hex kept tightening bolts that were already tight.

“Habit,” Hex said.

“Bad one. You'll strip the threads.”

There were ordinary problems to solve. An engine that wouldn't start. A delivery truck with a broken alternator. A farmer who insisted the strange noise in his tractor had appeared all by itself.

Hex began to discover that an ordinary day could be difficult without being dangerous.

One winter morning, Tomas found him staring out at the mountains.

“You expecting someone?” the older man asked.

“No.”

“Then stop looking like you are.”

Hex almost smiled.

Years passed.

The mountains changed colour with the seasons. Tomas taught him which roads iced over first and which neighbours would leave soup outside his door if they thought he was sick. Hex learned to recognise footsteps on the workshop stairs without immediately reaching for a weapon.

But some things didn't change.

In a tin box beneath a loose floorboard, he kept the photograph and Elise's broken phone.

Every few months he took them out.

Sometimes he wondered whether Ninja had learned what happened to her sister. Sometimes he imagined going to her and telling her everything.

Then he imagined the danger following him to her door.

He put the tin box away.

### Chapter Five — The Life Ninja Built

Five years after Elise died, Ninja still had two mugs in her kitchen that had belonged to her sister.

One had a chipped handle and an aggressively cheerful slogan. Elise had bought it as a joke, then refused to take it home because, as she put it, Ninja's kitchen needed personality.

Ninja kept both.

Her life was no longer the wreckage it had been in the first year. She had fought the complaints, gathered what records she could, and managed to rebuild enough stability to work and pay her bills.

But she had never received a satisfying explanation.

Official letters used phrases like administrative irregularities and insufficient evidence. The warehouse fire remained an accident in every public record she could find. Every time she asked another question, another door closed.

She had learned to live around the unanswered ones.

Some evenings she was fierce and funny and entirely herself. Other evenings she came home exhausted, put on her black headphones, and sat in the quiet until she could bear the world again.

She missed Elise most in the ordinary moments.

A ridiculous advert. A badly behaved dog. A song her sister would have hated. The kind of tiny, pointless discoveries that had once been worth a phone call.

One rainy afternoon, Ninja found an old voice message while sorting files on her phone.

“Hello, menace,” Elise's voice announced. “I'm outside your building. I have food. If you pretend you're not home, I'll eat the good part and leave you the vegetables.”

Ninja laughed before the tears arrived.

She played it again.

Then she put the phone face down and stared at the window.

She had spent years trying to understand the night that took Elise. Somewhere along the way, she realised she had also stopped doing things simply because she wanted to.

She opened a map on her laptop.

Not a grand adventure. Not a heroic escape. Just a few days somewhere she had never been.

A mountain village called Bellwether appeared among the search results. The photographs showed misty roads, old stone houses, and a pub called The Crooked Lantern.

Ninja read the description twice.

“Mountains, rain, and a pub,” she told the empty room. “Honestly, Elise, you'd approve of at least two of those.”

She booked a room before she could talk herself out of it.

Then she sat there, startled by the small, unfamiliar sensation of having something to look forward to.

### Chapter Six — A Name in the Archive

That same week, a message reached Hex through an old encrypted channel he hadn't used in years.

He nearly deleted it.

Instead he opened it in the workshop after Tomas went home.

The message contained a fragment of an internal Directorate report. Most of the names were blacked out. One wasn't.

N-17.

Below it was a summary of consequences: financial restrictions, employment investigation, prolonged monitoring, no evidence of operational involvement.

And one line that made Hex's stomach tighten.

Relationship to deceased courier: sibling.

He read it again.

Elise Varen was Ninja's sister.

He had known the woman in the photograph was connected to the operation. He hadn't known how directly. He hadn't known the person who sent that final message was the one who had spent years living with the loss.

He reached for the edge of the workbench.

For a moment he could smell the warehouse again: smoke, hot metal, wet concrete.

He remembered Elise trying to speak over the noise. He remembered the door he couldn't open. He remembered the phone in his pocket.

He had told himself keeping it was evidence.

Now it felt like a promise he'd never made and never kept.

Tomas came back for his forgotten keys and found him standing in the dark.

“You all right, Elias?”

“Fine.”

“That's a terrible lie.”

Hex tried to answer and couldn't.

Tomas didn't press. He set the keys down, pulled out a chair, and sat.

After a while he said, “You know, you don't have to tell me what happened before you came here.”

Hex looked at him.

“But whatever it was,” Tomas continued, “it doesn't get to be the only thing that ever happens to you.”

The workshop clock ticked. Rain tapped the roof.

Hex folded the printed report and placed it inside his coat.

He didn't sleep much that night.

### Chapter Seven — Two Roads Toward Bellwether

On the morning Ninja left for Bellwether, she nearly cancelled.

Her bag stood by the door. The weather forecast was unpromising. She had slept badly and dreamed of Elise, not as she had been at the end but laughing in a supermarket aisle over something neither of them could explain.

Ninja stood in the kitchen with her keys in her hand.

“It's just a trip,” she said aloud.

The apartment didn't argue.

She put on her coat, adjusted the strap of her bag, and picked up Elise's chipped mug from the draining board. For a moment she held it, thumb against the damaged handle.

“Wish you were coming,” she whispered.

Then she set it down carefully and left.

The journey took her through towns that grew smaller, along roads that narrowed, and eventually into hills where the trees seemed to lean close to the windows.

At a roadside stop she bought terrible coffee and a sandwich that was mostly bread. She took a picture of the view, almost sent it to Elise, and instead saved it.

She could miss her sister and still have a good day. She was learning that the two things could exist together.

Back in Bellwether, Hex spent the afternoon fixing a forestry vehicle that had apparently been repaired with optimism and three incompatible screws.

Tomas watched him work.

“You're doing that thing again.”

“What thing?”

“Looking over your shoulder.”

Hex tightened a fitting. “Maybe I enjoy the view.”

“Of my dusty workshop?”

“It's a compelling landscape.”

Tomas snorted.

Outside, the first evening rain moved down from the hills.

At closing time, Tomas asked whether Hex wanted to join him for a drink at The Crooked Lantern.

Hex almost said no. He usually did.

Then he thought of Tomas's words in the workshop, and of the years he'd spent treating every ordinary invitation as a threat.

“Maybe later,” he said.

Tomas gave him a look. “That's the closest thing to yes I've heard from you in months.”

After Tomas left, Hex turned off the workshop lights one by one. The last pool of light disappeared from the floor, leaving only the grey evening at the windows.

Miles away, Ninja's vehicle passed a weathered sign.

WELCOME TO BELLWETHER.

She slowed as the village came into view: stone cottages, warm windows, a narrow bridge, and beyond them the dark shape of the mountains.

Somewhere in those streets, a man who knew her sister's last moments was locking up a workshop.

Somewhere ahead, a pub sign creaked in the rain.

Neither of them knew that their lives were about to change.

Not tonight.

Not quite yet.

But soon.

And for the first time in five years, both were moving toward something other than the past.

*End of Episode One.*
