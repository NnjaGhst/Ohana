# Elsewhere — Episode 11: What Iona Kept

### Previously on Elsewhere

Hex gave the police both versions of his statement, including the one he wrote soon after the fire. Ninja went to a room Elise used to rent above the print shop. There were two boxes, a chair with a piece of cardboard under one leg, and a cake tin with a spare key inside.

Iona had offered to tell the officer about a meeting she attended after the fire. At Ada’s gate, Ninja invited Hex in for lunch.

### Chapter One — Two Rolls

The two rolls had come in separate parcels. By the time Ninja put them on the kitchen table she had forgotten which one she meant to keep for tomorrow. Ada said this showed a sensible lack of attachment to bread. She fetched the butter and let them sort it out.

Hex stood by the sink in his jacket until Ada asked if he would be more comfortable taking it off. He thought about the question as though it might have two correct answers. Then he took it off.

“Thank you for staying.”

“You asked me in.”

“Yes. I’m trying to get better at saying I’m glad when someone does the thing I asked.”

Hex sat down. There was a smear on the table left by the bottom of a jam jar. He attended to it with the corner of a napkin while she split a roll. She knew just what he was doing. It was a way to give her the next moment if she wanted it.

Ninja asked whether he had ever been inside the upstairs room before. He said he had walked past the print shop for five years without wondering what was above it. The window looked exactly like a window a person could miss.

She reached into her pocket and set both keys on the table. They had identical teeth. One had a white tag and the other had a scuffed green plastic sleeve. Under a lamp she could see that somebody had used a file to smooth one corner.

“Why put a spare key in a cake tin instead of giving it to someone?”

“To give it to someone later, perhaps.”

That kept her quiet for the rest of the roll. She had been imagining Elise moving out of the room, putting things away because she was leaving. It hadn’t occurred to her that the key in the tin might have been for a person she expected to see.

Ada came back through with a plate of sliced apples. She had cut them from the side instead of from the top, so two pieces had a little star in the middle. Ninja told her Elise used to do that when the apple was particularly unpromising. She insisted the star made up for a dry apple.

“Did it?” Ada asked.

“Of course not,” said Hex. “But it gave you something to talk about while you ate it.”

Ada gave him the plate. There were some things that sounded as though they belonged to a person for as long as you liked, even when it was somebody else who said them.

While Ada went to find the telephone, Ninja took out a sheet of paper. She had started dividing the things she knew into two columns. In one she put things a person had told her. The other was for what could be shown to somebody who didn’t know any of them. She had decided to use pencil so that the same thing could move from one column to the other. Some days she felt as if she could spend a long time just moving a single word.

Hex asked if she would like him to help. She slid the sheet between them and pointed to a line she had drawn through the word accident. It was the word printed near the top of a letter she’d received long ago. She thought she could still find the letter. She would need to call a person at home and ask which pile it was in. She found herself dreading the part of the call where she explained she wanted it.

Hex said he could be there when she rang, if it would help to have someone waiting afterward. Ninja left a space on the page for the date on the letter, whether or not she felt like making that call today.

They went back to the apples. She wondered if Elise would have disliked the two columns. They were probably too tidy. Elise might have added a third for things that nobody was ready to say. She imagined her sister drawing it crookedly and then claiming that it curved on purpose. When she was a little girl she used to think Elise got everything right first. It had taken a while for her to notice how often Elise just went over the line until it was what she wanted.

### Chapter Two — The Polite Meeting

The officer called while Ada was folding the empty paper around the crumbs. Iona had given her statement that morning. She wanted to speak to Ninja afterward, with the officer there, and had asked whether the print shop would be all right.

“What does she want me to hear?”

The officer said Iona had kept a document from the meeting. She had explained why she kept it and why she hadn’t handed it over before.

“Yes, I’ll meet her. I’d like to hear what she says herself.”

Outside it was dry enough that the pavement had begun to show two colours. Hex walked with Ninja down the hill. At the bakery he stopped to let a man back out with a tray of buns that required both his arms and the full width of the doorway. Then they went on.

They found Iona in the print shop with her cream coat folded across her lap. Her bag was on the floor between her feet. Rose had drawn down the blind a little because the afternoon light came in hard at that time of day. There were four chairs, and Ninja chose the one from which she could still see the street.

Iona told them there had been a meeting the week after the fire. It was described in the diary as refreshments. The tea came in a pot, and someone brought biscuits with chocolate on the bottom. A man from legal affairs took the minutes. Pike explained that two versions of the warehouse register would be going to different offices. He asked everybody to sign a receipt for the set they were supposed to have seen.

“And what did you do?” Hex asked.

“I signed that I had seen the wrong one,” Iona said. “I had seen the right one as well.”

“Why did you keep the minutes?”

“Because he told the man from legal affairs to read the opening paragraph back and cut the second half. I was taking notes on the sheet underneath. When I got home there was an impression of it on my paper.”

Iona looked at the officer, who nodded. She had seen the sheet earlier. There was an incident number written on the corner of her folder, in the same clear print she had used for Alice’s envelope.

Before the fire the room had been used as a place to hold people temporarily. There were names Iona knew from the lists and names she did not. At the meeting Pike had said they had been moved. The second half of the first paragraph recorded that they hadn’t all reached the destinations on his register. Someone had asked a question about who would tell their families. Iona couldn’t remember who asked it. That was the part she wished she could remember.

For a little while after Iona finished there was only the hum of the print shop fridge in the room. The officer turned back a page and asked her when she had put the notes away. Iona looked at a corner of the table where there was a paper mat underneath a glass of water. It had begun to darken around the inside edge.

“The same night. I flattened them under a cookery book.”

She had taken them out years later because she was moving house. The book opened at the only recipe she had ever made from it. Someone had written half the amount of salt in the margin. For a while she could remember which friend had written that, and hardly anything about the folded paper underneath. Then she opened it and remembered the meeting well enough to be sick in the kitchen sink.

The officer didn’t ask her why she’d kept it there again. She asked whether the sheet had ever got wet. Iona said she didn’t think so. There had been a watermark on the dining table, but she had chosen the dry side when she put the book down.

Ninja listened. She had imagined evidence arriving somewhere in a sealed container, carried by a person who knew what to do with it. It was probably like this much more often. A sheet of paper had stayed under a recipe the right way up. Somebody had had to remember not to put a wet glass on it. You were allowed to want something to survive without knowing the first thing about how it would be used.

Hex had turned his chair so that Iona had the officer in front of her and him a little further away. Ninja was glad he did that. She could still see him if she turned her head. He had an old mark on the outside of his thumb from a cut he got fixing a radiator. He once told her the mark used to look rather more dramatic. It had been reduced to a grey line that would go on being grey long after he’d forgotten which radiator it was.

The officer took the page away in its folder and returned for her pen. She always checked the place where she had been sitting before she left a table. It was such a small professional habit that Ninja had only begun to notice it. Afterward there might be a way of finding the line from that chair to another room, and the people in the other room might open the folder and know what they had to ask for.

That, Ninja thought, would be something the people from the meeting hadn’t planned for.

### Chapter Three — A Door of Their Own

The officer said she could now ask for the complete meeting record from two separate archives. When Pike’s office received that request, it would be harder for him to say there had been no meeting at all. She made no promise about when they’d hear anything. Iona looked tired enough to be grateful for a sentence that ended exactly where it should.

Ninja put the green key on the counter.

“Do you know who this was for?”

Iona touched the plastic sleeve with one finger. She said that Elise had brought it to work to show her. The key was meant as a spare for whoever arrived late. The first chair was for Elise. The taller one was supposed to be for Ninja. There would have been a third, if she could get one that folded.

“Why three?” Hex asked.

“Elise wanted somewhere people could talk after work without passing through the Directorate door,” Iona said. “On some days she wanted the same place for the people she loved. I asked her how she was going to choose what it was for. She said she thought it might be possible to let it be useful twice.”

Ninja remembered the message in the cake tin. You can come here and do absolutely nothing. She asked if that was one of the things Iona had been allowed to do there.

“I didn’t go often,” Iona said. “Once we drank tea and watched two men trying to put a rug on top of a car. Elise insisted the men were brothers because no one would sound that cross with somebody they didn’t know the way home to.”

Hex made a sound that might have been a laugh. Iona looked at him. Then Ninja laughed properly, and it was all right to let the sound sit in the shop for a moment while the officer turned a page.

Iona asked if Ninja would like to keep both keys. She said she didn’t want anybody to think the spare belonged to her merely because she knew what it opened.

“Can you come upstairs with me for a minute? There’s something I’d like you to see.”

Iona looked at the officer. The officer said she was finished for now. Iona lifted her coat from her lap and put it back down. Then she followed Ninja up the stairs.

On the stairs Iona stopped to move a dry leaf that had blown under the bottom step. It looked like the sort of leaf a child would have brought in on purpose. She set it on the lower windowsill instead of throwing it away.

From the landing they could hear Rose talking to Hex below. She wanted him to look at a drawer which sometimes opened when nobody asked it to. Hex asked whether she wanted it fixed, or just wanted a witness. Rose said to start with a witness.

Ninja gave herself time to smile before putting the key in the lock. It didn’t seem reasonable that she should be able to recognise the feeling of opening this particular door after doing it twice. It turned out that getting used to a place was something you could do in small pieces.

Iona said that Elise had brought the pear print up the stairs on a windy day with no bag over it. Every time the wind caught it she had to hold it flat against her front like a very small shield. She got it upstairs, looked at it properly for the first time, and decided the pear leaned in the wrong direction. Then she hung it up anyway. The room needed something which had arrived before she knew whether she was keeping it.

Ninja understood that better than she wanted to say out loud. She had put the letter about the milk on the shelf of Ada’s spare room and hadn’t taken it down since. The only reason she hadn’t found a more sensible place to put it was that she was beginning to like the way she could see it when she went in.

She opened the upstairs door and let Iona follow. The window was closed. Beyond it one of the shopkeepers was bringing in a board before the street lights came on. There was clean space round the chair. The room still had the useful quality of waiting until someone arrived before asking anything of them.

### Chapter Four — The Other Chair

The afternoon light fell exactly where Elise thought it would. It reached the floor by the window and stopped before touching the chair. Ninja moved the chair about a foot to the left. The cardboard under the short leg came with it only because Iona stooped to pick it up.

They found that the patch of sunlight was wide enough for two chairs, if neither chair was particularly splendid.

Iona stood with her back to the shelves and told Ninja that Elise had hung the pear print herself. It had first been too high and then too low. They had used the same nail for both attempts. Ninja put a finger on the hole above the frame. She had seen it before and assumed there was nothing to know about it.

“I’m still angry with you.”

“Yes,” Iona said.

“I needed you to hear that from me. I also wanted you to show me where the light was.”

Iona turned her face toward the window. The street was narrow enough that she could see someone setting dinner out on the other side. Then she pointed to the far edge of the sun on the floor.

“Later it reaches further than that.”

They went back down. Hex was waiting beside a stack of proof sheets. Rose had found two stools and a little plate of the plain biscuits that came in the cheap packet. On the counter the officer had left a card with a number to call if Pike contacted any of them. Ninja put it in her pocket with the green key.

Outside, she told Hex she might look for another chair.

“Would you like me to look too?”

“Only if you promise to sit in it first. I won’t take one home if you say it looks patient.”

He smiled. “What if it does?”

“Then try to make it laugh.”

They turned toward Briar House together. Ninja had an unexpected urge to tell Ada that she thought she would stay a little longer. She didn’t know how long. It was pleasant to carry the thought far enough to find out how it felt beside the ordinary things in her pockets.
