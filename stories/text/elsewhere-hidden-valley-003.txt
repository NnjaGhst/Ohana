# Elsewhere: The Hidden Valley
## Episode 3 — The Whiskey Across the Room

Previously on Elsewhere, Ninja came to Bellwether for a few quiet days, unaware that her sister's death was part of the reason a man named Elias Ward already lived there. For five years Hex has kept a photograph from the job that drove him out of the city. Tonight, Ninja pushes open the door of the Crooked Lantern.

He could tell you which of the cars outside the garage belonged to the baker by the sound it made coming up the hill. He knew which window in the bar gave him a view of the street. Some evenings he sat with his back to it on purpose. He wanted to know what it felt like to finish a beer that way.

On the night Ninja came in, he was trying it again.

The door stuck when the weather was wet. He heard someone give it a second shove, followed by a quiet, irritated voice.

“Oh, come on.”

Then the door came free.

Then a woman crossed the room and asked for a whiskey.

“Do you mind which one?” Ada asked from behind the bar.

“I’d have to know the difference to mind.”

“You and me both, love.”

Ninja laughed. The sound did what the photograph couldn’t. It told Hex how she drew a little breath at the end, like she’d surprised herself into enjoying something.

He turned his head.

Her hair was pink, pulled up with half of it trying to escape. She had rain on the shoulder of her coat and the same little dip in the middle of her chin he remembered from the picture. Five years had passed. He knew that photograph by heart.

He knew who she was before Ada set the glass down.

Ninja paid, took her drink and chose a table at the other end of the room. She set her coat on the chair beside her and rubbed her fingers around the glass to warm them. Her eyes swept over the room without stopping on him.

Hex set down his beer. He put his hand in his pocket for the key to his room, found it, and kept holding it. He could have been upstairs in twenty seconds. His bag was on the top shelf of the wardrobe. He knew what he would take and what he would leave, down to the pair of good boots he’d only bought yesterday.

If anybody from that old job saw them together, they’d wonder what he’d told her. And if they wanted an answer, they could get it from her more easily than they could get it from him.

The thought should have got him out of his chair.

Across the room, Ninja took a sip. She made a face at the glass, then tried it again, as if it deserved a fair hearing.

Hex let go of the key.

Before Hex came up to the counter, Ninja had done three things at her table. She'd tried the whiskey. She'd read the first page of the paperback she'd brought. And she'd opened an old message from Elise, which was still there because she'd never found a good reason to clear it away.

The paperback had a castle on the front and a map inside. She liked a map in a book, even when she had no plan to follow it. At the bottom of the page there was a dotted line crossing a stretch of country the artist hadn't named. She wondered what it would be like to go somewhere that had a road and no promises attached to it. It was the sort of thought she could have had in any room. Bellwether was the first place that week where she had enough time to finish it.

Elise's message was about a plant Ninja had bought for the hall. Ninja had spent ten minutes in the shop choosing it, and then another ten trying to work out whether a very tall plant was appropriate for somebody who already had trouble getting coats past the door. Elise had suggested putting it beside the stairs where it could supervise everybody going up. Ninja had taken a picture of it there. It had been a bad plant in the sense that it dropped a leaf every time the seasons changed and then acquired an entirely new pair in an unexpected place. She'd kept it for years. One of her neighbours was watering it while she was away.

She shut the message and noticed the man at the other end of the room. He was looking at the little pocket on the inside of his coat with such concentration that she assumed he'd lost something. He moved his hand, and she saw the key between two of his fingers. He put it away, looked toward the door, and briefly met her eye. Their glances caught at exactly the wrong moment for either of them to pretend they'd never made eye contact. She went back to the map. The line still didn't lead anywhere.

Ada was wiping the rim of a glass. The pub was quiet enough that Ninja could hear the cloth on the glass and the rain on the windows, and make out a conversation about whether the baker was ever going to move the rabbit in his display. On the way through town she had noticed the rabbit. She was relieved a second person had also wondered about it. It looked as if it had been waiting for Easter to come back round.

There were people in the pub who had known each other long enough to speak mostly in references. A man who'd come in for a beer was asked how the wall was getting on, as if the wall had recently been to the doctor's. Ada had noticed Ninja looking around. She set down a little bowl of peanuts where Ninja could reach without getting up. Ninja stayed at the table and read one more page. It was the first time all day that nothing needed to happen next.

At the other end of the room, Hex let the key slide to the bottom of his pocket. He tried to think of something to say that a man could ordinarily say to a woman he'd just seen for the first time. The thought caught on the fact that, for him, this wasn't quite the first time.

When she'd got to the bottom of her drink, he went to the bar.

“Could you send her another?” he asked Ada.

“Could you ask her if she wants one?”

“Fair point.”

He was working out how to cross the room without looking like he'd spent ten minutes thinking about it when Ninja looked up.

“You can ask me from there.”

Ada became very interested in a stack of saucers.

“Do you want another whiskey?” Hex asked.

“Depends. Is the next one going to taste like a table?”

“Probably. I can ask for a nicer table.”

She looked at him for half a second, and then both of them laughed. It was loud enough that a man reading his newspaper glanced over. Hex put a hand on the back of a chair to steady himself. He hadn’t expected her to get him that fast.

Ninja lifted her coat off the seat beside her.

“Come sit down before you buy me furniture.”

He did.

Up close, the first thing she noticed about him was that his eyes went to the door whenever it opened. The second was that when she spoke, they came right back to her. He did it every time. She'd spent enough evenings with people whose eyes wandered halfway through her sentences to find that little habit instantly charming.

“Elias,” he said.

“Ninja.”

He let her see him smile. “That suits you.”

“You've known me for thirty seconds.”

“Thirty-five. I watched you threaten the door.”

“It started it.”

Ada brought two glasses of a different whiskey. Ninja tried hers and considered it.

“Better. A church pew.”

Hex laughed through his nose. She had already worked out that this particular laugh meant she'd got him before he'd had time to answer. She went for it again a few minutes later. It worked twice as well.

It was like that for most of the first hour. He told her about a customer who had brought him parts for the same broken wing mirror in a biscuit tin, one piece at a time, on four different Tuesdays. She insisted on knowing whether the customer had kept the biscuits. When Hex admitted he hadn't asked, she looked personally wounded.

“You had four Tuesdays, Elias.”

“I thought the mirror was the important part.”

“That's why they didn't give you any biscuits.”

He was still smiling when he realized he'd turned his chair all the way toward her. He could see the door only in the reflection of a glass-fronted cupboard, and even that disappeared when Ada went to fetch a plate. For a good long while, he let it.

Ninja had come to Bellwether for three nights. She'd booked the hotel after a week in which five people had asked her for things and the one person she'd wanted to hear from had still been dead. She'd arrived in the rain that afternoon, eaten a sad packet of crisps for dinner, and gone looking for somewhere warm where she could sit around other people without having to be useful to anybody.

She told him the bit about the crisps first.

“What flavour?” he asked.

“Salt and vinegar.”

“That's a decent dinner if you have two packets.”

“Thank you. Finally, someone with medical training.”

He got them bread and cheese from Ada, who added a small bowl of pickles and then left them alone. Hex put the bowl between them. Ninja ate one, looked him in the eyes and slid the whole bowl to her side of the table.

“Right,” he said. “Now I know where we stand.”

At some point between the second whiskey and the bread, Ada handed them a wooden box with a deck of cards in it. The people who ordinarily kept score were having an argument about darts. If Ninja and Elias wanted to do something with the cards, Ada explained, they'd be doing the pub a favour by keeping them away from the darts players.

Ninja opened the box. One of the kings was a little taller than all the other cards. Somebody had cut a replacement out of cereal packaging and drawn a crown on it, along with an expression of deep personal disappointment. She showed it to him. Hex turned over the rest of the deck and found a second handmade card. It was an eight, but whoever had made it had run out of room for one of the hearts and folded the corner up to fit it in.

Between them they established that they knew three different games and agreed on the rules of none. Ninja shuffled. She had a habit of shuffling slowly when a deck looked as though it couldn't take much more. Hex waited with both hands on the table. It made him look very patient. She'd find out after about ninety seconds that he was thinking through every possible way to make up a rule that would benefit him.

They made the prize a choice of what Ada put on the jukebox. There were playlists for afternoons, quiet nights, and closing time. Neither of them had any idea what was on any of them. Ninja picked quiet nights because she wanted to find out what Ada thought was quiet. The very first thing it played was a fiddle solo that seemed to have begun in the middle of a grievance.

Hex lost the first round. He suspected Ninja had been playing the cereal-box king on a little diagonal so he could see the edge and think she was bluffing. She denied it. On the next round she set the king in the middle of her hand and beat him again. He was having enough fun that he could no longer remember how many people had come through the door since he sat down.

Ninja glanced past him once, toward the dark window. A man in a light raincoat was standing under the awning, trying to shake water out of an umbrella. He put both hands on the handle and pressed. Finally it went in with enough force to make him take a step forward. He turned toward the window and saw her looking. She thought he might be embarrassed. When he didn't smile, she looked back at her cards.

Hex saw the movement in the glass. By the time he turned, the man was on his way up the road. He couldn't place him. He did have a clean memory of the grey checks on the umbrella, and of somebody at the garage earlier that week waiting outside in the same coat. A man could walk past a garage and a public house in the same village without any of it meaning anything. Hex stayed at the table.

Ninja had won, but on the last round she gave him the slightly taller king and challenged him to find a reasonable explanation for its expression. He considered a crown inherited from a brother, a cold cup of tea, or a man just beginning to realise he'd chosen the wrong kind of hat for the weather. She took the card back and looked at it for herself. It reminded her of Elise trying to see how serious she could look for a passport photograph. The game was over by the time she noticed she had been thinking about her sister for a full minute and had kept smiling.

A little later, rain started coming down hard enough to rattle against the window. Ninja watched a ribbon of water running down the glass.

“My sister would have liked it here.”

Hex laid a piece of bread on his plate. He had thought about how he'd react if Elise came up. He found he could sit perfectly still.

“She'd have known everybody by tomorrow. I used to hate that about her on holiday. You'd go down to breakfast on the second day and there she was, promising to pick somebody's nan up from an appointment.”

“That sounds like a lot of responsibility for a holiday.”

“She loved it. I liked hearing her tell me who everybody was later. I always pretended I didn't.”

Ninja pulled a bit of crust off her bread. “She died five years ago.”

Hex could have told her then. She was looking down at the table, holding the crust between two fingers. A few plain words would have been enough to get them started. He knew that once he said them, she'd have to spend the rest of her evening finding out what he meant.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

“Thanks.” She took a breath. “You can keep talking, by the way. Sometimes I tell people and they act like we’ve all got to go home.”

“What sort of idiot would go home before the pickles?”

Ninja laughed with her eyes still wet. She pushed the bowl back between them. He took one this time.

The next hour passed even faster. Ninja found out he had once brought a stubborn car radio upstairs, as if a change of scenery might make it confess. Hex found out she used to walk the long way home from the bus stop on nights when she'd spent all day being capable and needed ten minutes where nobody expected anything of her. She said she liked the houses on that road. You could tell who'd just had a baby, who was out of town, and who was the sort of person to leave a single Christmas star up until February.

“What would I learn about your place?” he asked.

“That I keep a lamp on in the hall.”

“For when you come home?”

She nodded. “It's nice when the house looks like somebody's waiting.”

He wanted to say something funny. It would have been easier for both of them. For once he let the silence sit until he knew what he meant.

“I think I'd leave one on too,” he said.

Ninja looked at him. She'd been fond of him almost since the furniture joke. Sitting there under the ugly little lamp on their table, she felt something in that fondness settle. She reached across and touched the back of his hand. He turned it over. Her fingers fitted into his loosely.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

At closing time, Ada slid two mugs of water onto the table.

“You can sit while I stack the chairs,” she said. “If you want to carry on until breakfast, one of you has to do the floors.”

A while before closing, one of the darts players put his coat on, went outside and came straight back in. He had parked by the wall at the side of the pub. The lights on the car had been on through most of the evening. Now the car didn't want to go anywhere.

Ada picked up her keys. Hex offered to look first, and Ninja got up with him. She wanted a little air. Beyond the awning the rain had thinned to a soft hissing sound. Outside it was easier to hear the river, although it was two streets away, and to see how far the village lights climbed the hill before giving out.

The car belonged to a man called Len. Ninja recognised the name as soon as Ada used it. She'd seen it on a board in the garage window that afternoon, along with the prices for things she always meant to remember to have checked. Hex put his sleeves up and lifted the bonnet while Len went to look for a torch. A woman under the awning held her hands out for Len's pint. She told him to come back with a working car or an explanation, because she disliked standing on one leg over a puddle for somebody who was going to take his beer home in the end.

Ninja went inside for her phone. When she came back she held its light where Hex needed it. They shifted round the front of the car together without either of them having to say much. At one point she moved the light to read something printed near his hand, and he waited until she'd finished looking at it before putting the little cover back. Another person might have taken the phone off her. He just rested his arm on the edge of the car until she found the right angle again.

Len came back with a torch and one knitted glove. He had picked them up from different tables and seemed to regard the glove as a slight bonus. Ada found another car to lend him a hand. By the time they had finished, there was a little space at the edge of the pavement where the headlights cast a perfectly clear reflection. Ninja liked seeing Hex in the middle of all of it. He knew the place well enough to be useful, and they knew him well enough to take his coat and get out of his way.

A car went past without slowing at the junction. Its lights caught the faces on the pavement as it turned. Hex stepped back toward the wall so quickly that Ninja saw the movement before she had time to think about what she'd seen. He said his shirt had caught the edge of the bonnet. There was nothing in his voice to suggest he expected her to believe that. She handed him his coat. The man from under the awning hadn't come back, and the road went quiet.

Len gave them a lift around the corner before taking the woman with his beer home. It was about thirty seconds in the car. He offered anyway, which made it hard to turn down. At the front door of the pub, Ninja got out first. She saw Hex check the road once, from where Len wouldn't see him looking. Then he came round to her side and made sure she got in out of the rain.

The pub felt warmer when they came back in. Somebody had put the cereal-box king by the till where its disappointed face could watch the money change hands. Ninja pointed it out. Hex laughed and went to pick up their glasses. She caught herself looking at the door for a second after he walked away.

Outside, the rain had slowed to a fine mist. Ninja pulled her coat on and got one cuff caught on the strap of her bag. Hex held the bag while she freed herself. She thanked him with such enormous dignity that he had to turn his face away to laugh.

“Where are you staying?” he asked.

“The hotel. By the market.”

“Want company on the walk?”

“I was beginning to wonder if you'd ask.”

It was an eight-minute walk. They stretched it to half an hour. The village had gone quiet and the gutters were still dripping. At the baker’s corner she stopped to look at a lopsided marzipan rabbit in the window. Hex told her it had been there since Easter. She said she'd come back in the morning to check on it.

He remembered exactly where she said it. One foot on the kerb, light from the bakery window on her cheek. He would run past that same window with her in a few hours and see the rabbit in the half second before he grabbed her hand.

For now, they stood there making up reasons why a baker would keep a rabbit on display for seven months.

They started walking again.

“You’re very easy to be with.”

He looked down at her. “I haven't heard that in a long time.”

“Maybe you've been drinking with the wrong people.”

“Maybe.”

At the hotel entrance she reached into her pocket for the key. It was attached to a large wooden fish, which she held up for him to inspect.

“Room six. All the doors are named after fish and numbered anyway.”

“What's yours?”

“I thought you’d know.”

He turned the key over in her palm. Someone had painted exactly one eye on it, right in the middle.

“That's a deeply unwell trout.”

She laughed and leaned into him. When she straightened, she stayed close enough that he could feel the warmth from her face.

“I'd like to kiss you.”

His answer came out a little hoarse. “I'd like that too.”

They kissed under the hotel awning while a drop of water kept landing beside Hex's left boot. After a moment Ninja pulled back to laugh at the duck-shaped umbrella stand they were both leaning against. Then she took hold of his coat and kissed him again.

“Do you want to come upstairs?” she asked.

He thought about the cold beer he'd left at the bar and the half-packed bag in his wardrobe. It occurred to him that he'd spent the whole walk letting her choose the pace, and hadn't once looked at a clock.

“Yes,” he said.

The stairs to room six turned twice. At the first landing there was a small square window with a view of somebody's back garden. An outdoor chair had been left folded against the wall. The rain had made a narrow silver strip across the seat. Ninja stopped to look out because she could. They were very close to her room and there was time to stop at a window if she wanted to.

Hex stayed a step below her. Through the glass he could see a little farther down the road than he could from the awning. At his usual pace he'd have passed the window and counted to the second turning by now. He let that thought go and looked at the garden instead. In one corner somebody had put three flowerpots upside down in the hope of catching a leak from the gutter. He couldn't see how it would work, but he found himself wanting to know who had put them there.

Ninja sat on the window ledge. There was enough room for somebody else if they sat a little sideways. He joined her. She could smell rain in the wool of his coat, and underneath it the scent of the soap people had put beside the sink in the pub. The evening had begun with an hour in a hotel chair and a determination to be somewhere she wasn't expected. Now she wanted to know when he had to be at the garage in the morning, and whether a person who made a habit of fixing broken things ever got a full day off.

He told her when the garage opened and which days Len arrived before him. Then he told her he could have a late start the next day. His first job was a window that only went halfway down, which had been the case for a decade and a half. An extra half hour was unlikely to change much. Ninja said she'd bought breakfast with the room. They could see what it was like before he went to work. He felt his whole evening shift when she said that. It had a morning attached to it now.

She found his hand on the ledge. He was warm from the pub. There was a little pale scar along the base of his thumb. She asked him where he got it, and he told her about a screwdriver that went through the bottom of a cardboard box at exactly the wrong moment. It was an entirely believable accident. For about a minute, he had been able to answer every question she'd asked.

This would have been another place to tell her about Elise. He could imagine her hand moving away from his, and he could imagine her sitting there anyway because she understood by then that she had a right to hear the whole thing. He wanted to be the man who gave her that chance. Instead he put his other hand around hers and held it until she stood up.

She got the key out again at the second landing. At the door she had to jiggle it once to make it go in. He tried not to watch her hands. She glanced back and caught him watching. They were both still smiling when the lock turned.

Upstairs, she sat down on the edge of the bed to pull off her boots. One came off. The other put up a fight until Hex crouched down and took it by the heel. It came loose so suddenly he tipped back onto the rug. By the time he got up, she was laughing into her hands, and he was laughing too. Then she leaned forward and drew him close again.

The village clock struck two while the light was still on in room six. By three their coats had fallen off the chair. Much later, with the lamp out and the curtains starting to turn grey, Ninja told him she'd nearly stayed in with those crisps.

“Terrible decision,” he murmured.

“Obviously. Should've bought two packets.”

He felt her laugh against his shoulder. He had a few seconds to think about how familiar it felt to lie there with her, which was ridiculous. They'd known each other for one evening. He turned his face into her hair and was asleep before he could think much further than that.

A little after seven, the baker carried a tray of rolls past the marzipan rabbit. Ada put yesterday’s glasses away and set a fresh cloth on the bar. Upstairs at the hotel, Ninja stirred when a bed spring creaked, found Hex's hand near hers and went back to sleep.

Downstairs, a man stopped beside the umbrella stand. He stood still long enough to hear someone moving in the kitchen. Then he took the steps two at a time.
