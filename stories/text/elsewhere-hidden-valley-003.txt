Previously on Elsewhere. Ninja came to Bellwether for three quiet days. At the Crooked Lantern, a man at the far end of the bar sent her a whiskey. She looked up. Their eyes met.

Ninja smiled before she meant to. She'd spent the whole train ride looking at strangers and feeling grateful that none of them wanted anything from her. Now she found herself wanting this one to keep looking.

He did. Then his mouth moved a little, and she could see he was trying to decide how much of a smile he could get away with.

She picked up the whiskey and crossed the room. By the time she put it down in front of him, he had both hands on the back of the chair.

“Do you always send a drink ahead to see if it’s safe?”

“First time tonight.”

“That’s a very careful answer.”

He pulled out the chair next to him with his boot.

“You can sit down and ask me a harder one.”

She did.

“What were you hoping I’d do?”

He glanced at the stool she’d left on the other side of the room.

“Pretty much this.”

His answer came so quickly she laughed. There was a moment when he looked pleased about it before he remembered to look like a man waiting for something else. She caught that too. She wanted to see him forget again.

“Elias,” he said, offering his hand.

“Ninja.”

His fingers closed around hers. They stayed that way for a second longer than a handshake. When she pulled her chair nearer, their knees touched below the table. He looked down. So did she. A man on his way to the bar had to ask her to move her bag, and neither of them moved a knee to make room for him.

“Do you send people good whiskey, or do you keep the good one for yourself?”

“That depends. Can you tell?”

She tried the drink. It took her a second to get enough breath back to offer an opinion. He had the courtesy to look at the fireplace while she recovered.

“I can tell you were hoping I'd swallow it before I answered that.”

He laughed. She had heard children laughing in the square when she came in and liked that she could hear them through both sets of windows. This laugh stayed with her in a different way. It started deep in his chest and got away from him at the end. She wanted to hear it again while she was still the one sitting in range.

The photograph in his coat pressed against his ribs when he leaned back. He could still stand up and leave. The door was in reach. The house key and the garage key were in his pocket. He could see all the way to his front step if he pictured it. The thing he couldn't quite do was imagine hearing that laugh from the other side of the door.

She turned the glass a little, testing the weight of it in her hand. He wanted to see what she was looking at, so he looked down too.

“I was going to have my cider and read six pages of a book I don't even like.”

“What was wrong with the book?”

“Everybody in it has a secret. They keep saying things like, 'There's something I should tell you,' and then there's a chapter break.”

“What would you have them do?”

“Buy somebody a drink. See if they’re brave enough to come over.”

He took a breath that was almost a laugh. She could have asked which part he'd found funny. She liked it better when he put his whiskey down and asked if she had ever finished a book just so nobody could tell her it got better at the end.

She had. She told him the title. He knew it from a paperback Len had left in the garage and had got only halfway through. Ninja put one elbow on the table and began to tell him the ending. He leaned in like he had been waiting years for somebody to spoil it for him.

The pub had gone soft around the edges, the way places do when the crowd thins and the lights seem to dim without anyone touching them. Elias turned his glass a quarter turn on the coaster, watching her.

“You're doing the thing.”

"What thing."

“The thing where you look at a person like you're reading a map and checking it twice.”

"I'm a careful man." He took a sip. "You have pink hair tonight."

“I had pink hair when I came in too. You’re behind.”

"It suits you."

“Careful men don't say things like that.”

"Careful men mean them." He set the glass down. "So. The song."

By the time Ada asked whether they wanted another round, they were halfway through an argument about what made a song good enough to play twice in a row.

"I'm still on the song. Your test is ridiculous."

“It's not a test. It's a measurement.”

"A song feeling too short is a measurement of what, exactly? Attention span? Weather?"

“You know by whether the second time still feels too short.”

"That’s a terrible way to measure anything."

Ten minutes later she caught him feeding another coin into the jukebox to play the same song again.

He came back to find her laughing.

"It was too short," he said, and sat down closer this time.

“You're going to be smug about this for a week.”

"I'm going to be smug about it for a year." He finished his whiskey. "You want another?"

“I want to know why you're still here.”

"Because the whiskey's good."

“Elias.”

He looked at her, and the wry thing in his face went quiet. "Because I'm not in a hurry to go home. Is that all right?"

She held his gaze. Their knees were still touching under the table, had been for an hour, and neither of them had moved away.

“It's all right.”

He got up for the bar. She watched him go, the easy way he moved through a room, and she pulled out her phone and opened her contacts and sat there with the empty field blinking at her, feeling foolish, and then typed his name in.

He came back with two glasses and set one in front of her. "You're smiling at your phone."

He took his keys out of his coat before he sat down. One had a red tag with a crescent of bare metal showing through.

“What do you unlock with that one?”

“My front door.” He flipped the keys over for her to see. “I painted it the same red as the spare. That one's on a ring with a bit of copper wire around it. It hangs inside the back door. This one loses a little paint every time I use it.”

“Then how are you going to recognise it in the dark?”

He rubbed his thumb across the chip and smiled. “I think you’ve just talked me out of changing it.”

“I'm a modern woman.”

"You're plotting something."

“I'm always plotting something.”

She put the phone face down.

“When’s your birthday?”

He sat. "Why."

“Because I'm going to be smug at you for a year and I need to schedule it.”

"March ninth."

She picked the phone back up and typed it in. He watched her do it, and something moved across his face, quick and careful.

"You're putting it in."

“I'm putting it in.”

"That's a long way off."

“Then you'd better not disappear before it.”

It lightly. She wasn't sure it came out lightly.

"I wasn't planning to." He turned his glass. "What about yours?"

“August. I'll tell you when it's closer.”

"That's not how this works."

“That's exactly how this works. You get March, I get to keep August in my pocket a while.”

He shook his head but he was smiling. "You're a difficult woman."

“I'm a measured woman. There's a difference.”

They talked. The pub got quieter and the bartender wiped down the far end of the counter and the music changed to something older. She told him about the job she'd walked out of, the one with the boss who used the word 'family' to mean 'unpaid.' He told her about the year he'd spent learning to cook and how he'd ruined a whole Thanksgiving for six people with a brine. She laughed so hard she had to put her glass down.

“Six people.”

“That’s a crime.”

"It was a learning brine."

“It was a war crime.”

"Nobody died."

“You said the turkey was inedible.”

"The turkey was inedible. The people were fine. They ordered pizza. It's a happy ending."

She wiped her eye. Somewhere in the last hour their knees had become a thigh against a thigh and the contact had stopped being a thing she noticed and become a thing she'd miss.

Then the conversation ran down, the way conversations do, and she braced for the awkward part, the part where someone fills the silence with a joke or a look at the phone or a reach for the coat.

She didn't. He didn't.

She sat with her drink and he sat with his and the pub hummed around them and it was fine. It was better than fine. She'd forgotten a person could just be quiet next to you without it costing anything, without someone needing to fix it or name it or leave because of it.

Elias looked at the bar clock. "It's not late."

“No.”

"I should probably—"

“Probably.”

Neither of them moved.

He picked up his glass and took a small sip, and she watched him do it, and the quiet came back and settled in like a cat. She realized she was breathing easy. She realized she hadn't checked her phone in a while and didn't want to.

“Elias.”

"Mm."

“Nothing. I just wanted to say it.”

He nodded slowly, like she'd handed him something and he was turning it over. "Ninja."

“Mm.”

"Same."

Ada brought over a plate of chips a little while later. She said they were the last of them and if somebody didn't eat them she'd have to carry them back to the kitchen. Elias pushed the plate halfway toward Ninja. She pushed the little dish of salt the other way.

“You don't like salt?”

“I like it when somebody else puts it on.”

He took the dish, and she watched him try to work out how much to put on her half. Salt caught in the candlelight when it fell. She tasted one. He watched her face with as much interest as he had when she tried the whiskey.

“You know you can just ask me if I like something.”

“I can. This way I get to see for myself.”

She picked up another chip. There was a table close to the window where two people had left their chairs turned toward each other. She imagined Ada finding their chairs like that after they left. She smiled, and this time when Elias noticed she told him what she was thinking.

“I put mine there,” he said.

“I saw you.”

“I meant before I sent the drink. I moved the other one back so there'd be room if you came over.”

Ninja looked down at the chair. It was an old thing with one leg that wobbled. Elias steadied it with his heel while she tipped it experimentally. She could have asked him how long he'd been thinking about sending the drink. Instead she shifted in close enough for her shoulder to touch his.

“How long were you going to give me?”

“I had it down to the end of the song.”

“And then?”

“Go home. Spend the rest of the week being annoyed at myself.”

She rested her hand on the table. He put his down close to it. There was an inch between their little fingers. She wanted to see what he'd do and was annoyed with herself for wanting to wait that long.

“You could be a little less careful now.”

His hand moved across the table. He held hers flat between both of his for a moment, then rubbed his thumb across the side of her finger, where she'd caught it on a tin at home. The cut was almost gone. He didn't ask about it. He let her turn her hand and fit it around his. Then he reached for a chip with his other hand and made her laugh so suddenly she had to lean into his shoulder.

A customer came over for the salt. Elias gave it to him with his free hand. When the man went back to his table, Ninja looked down and found that Elias was still holding on.

The Crooked Lantern had thinned out to the regulars, the ones who knew the closing hour by the sound of the chairs going up. Elias had stopped pouring for himself a while back. His glass sat half full, a prop he kept touching without drinking from.

Ninja turned her own glass by the stem. The birthday thing had landed and gone, and neither of them had filled the quiet after it, which was its own kind of answer.

“I nearly cancelled the trip.”

Elias looked at her. Not startled. Waiting.

“I spent the whole week wanting to be somewhere nobody needed anything from me. By the time I got here, I was starting to wonder if I’d made a mistake coming somewhere I didn’t know anybody.”

“Where would you have gone if you cancelled?”

“I don’t know. A cinema. Somewhere dark where nobody talks.”

“What changed your mind?”

“The train doors closed before I made up my mind. I watched the platform go by, and I thought, well, there you go. You've managed to make one decision today.”

“And then you picked this place.”

“You were a bonus.”

“I’m glad the train didn’t wait.”

“Careful. That almost sounded sentimental.”

The clock behind the bar ticked toward eleven. Someone near the door laughed too loudly and then apologised to nobody in particular and left. Elias drew a circle in the water his glass had left on the table.

“Can I tell you something stupid?” he said.

“I’d be insulted if you didn’t.”

“I keep a list. In my head. Of things I’d do if I ever had a free weekend and a bit of nerve. Learn to sail. That’s on it. I’ve never been on a boat that didn’t have a motor and a timetable.”

“You? Sailing?”

“I know. I picture myself very capable and windburned. The reality is I’d capsize in the harbour and have to be rescued by a teenager.”

“I’d pay to watch that.”

“Everyone would.” He wiped his finger on his jeans. “Your turn. Something ordinary. Not the venue, not the work. Something from a Tuesday.”

Ninja thought about it. Took her time.

“I have a neighbour. Fourth floor. She’s ninety-something and she grows tomatoes on the balcony and she’s convinced I’m a spy.”

“Are you?”

“She thinks I come home at strange hours. She’s not wrong. Last month she left a note under my door that said I should be careful of the man in the blue car.”

She paused.

“There is no man in the blue car. I checked. Twice.”

“You checked twice.”

“I’m thorough.”

She drank.

“Anyway. Now when I see her in the lift I tell her I’ve been at the library. She nods very slowly like she’s filing it away. It’s the best part of my week, honestly. Being a spy who reads a lot.”

Elias was grinning now, the real one, the one that reached the corners. “Does she have a name for you?”

“She calls me the lady from four. I’ve decided it’s a codename.”

“It’s a good codename.”

Ada collected the empty glasses and left them with the candle in the middle of the table.

“Are you still wondering?” he asked.

Ninja looked at him for a moment.

“Ask me when I leave.”

He nodded, slow, like that was a fair answer and he intended to hold her to it.

They didn’t say anything for a bit. The fridge hummed. Somewhere upstairs a pipe knocked twice and settled.

“I should warn you,” he said. “I’m not good at this part.”

“Which part?”

“The part where the night’s over and I have to say something that isn’t about the weather.”

“You’re doing fine so far.”

“I’m building to something. Give me a minute.”

She laughed, and it surprised her, and she didn’t try to take it back. He reached over and straightened a coaster that didn’t need straightening, and his hand brushed hers on the table, and neither of them moved it away.

Much later, when Ada brought their coats over without being asked, he did.

“So. Mistake?”

Ninja turned her glass one last time and set it down.

“I haven’t left yet.”

She could have said it from the table. It might have come out as a joke there. Across the room she had to watch him understanding what she meant. He put his coat on and walked over to where she was standing. For a second he looked as though he had something to ask her, and she realised she'd put a hand out for him before she knew what it was.

“You can come as far as you like.”

He looked at her hand. “I'd like to get past the door first. Then I'll tell you.”

She thought of him all alone at the far end of the bar, making up his mind about her. There was enough left of the candle to light his face when he turned one last time to thank Ada. She wanted to be standing right where she was a second longer. Then he held the door for her. The street was cold, the rain had stopped, and a bright strip of light from the pub lay across the wet pavement.

They stood there a second. Then she reached over and took his hand, laced her fingers through his, and he closed his grip like he’d been waiting all evening for permission.

“Come to the hotel with me.”

Elias looked down at their hands, then at her.

“I’d like that,” he said.

They started walking.

It was a five-minute walk to the hotel, up past the shuttered bakery and the bus stop with the timetable peeling off the glass. It took them longer. Halfway there she felt him slow down and she did too, and for a while they walked so slowly it might have been easier to stop.

“What are you doing tomorrow?”

“I was going to change the oil in a car that hasn't started in two years.”

“Were you looking forward to it?”

“Until about ten seconds ago, yes.”

She laughed, and he turned his head toward her. A car went by slowly, its tyres lifting water out of a shallow puddle, and he moved to the outside of the pavement. He did it without losing the thread of what he was saying. She liked that. She also liked that a few steps later she did exactly the same thing to get him out of the way of a gutter that was pouring water from a loose joint.

“I thought you'd save me and then pretend it was an accident.”

“I was trying to save your coat. You're welcome to get wet.”

He looked down at the coat with a face so grave she nearly believed he'd been offended on its behalf. Then she felt his thumb move over hers. The street was quiet enough to hear the water falling into the drain. Ninja wanted to remember what it sounded like in a week if she walked along here alone.

They took one room. Upstairs, he unlocked the door and let her go in first. He was still holding the key when she turned around. All evening he’d had something to say whenever she surprised him. Now he just held the key out to her.

She took the key and tossed it onto the floor. Then she caught the front of his coat and kissed him. He kissed her back, then drew away far enough to look at her. She was smiling before he did.

“Wait,” he said softly.

She loosened her hand. Then she saw his smile widen, and he came closer again.

“I wanted to see if you were going to pull me back.”

“I’m considering it.”

He kept his eyes on hers. A few seconds went by. He was playing at being patient all over again, and she watched the exact moment he lost. This time he kissed her first. The coat hung open around her hands. She felt his fingers at the side of her face, and she forgot she had turned the lamp on until she saw him close his eyes in the light.

When they finally took off their coats, she sat on the edge of the bed to pull off her boots. He crouched to put the room key on the table, then sat down on the carpet at her feet with his back to the bed. She put her elbow on his shoulder. It felt absurdly normal. More comfortable than a first night together had any right to be.

“Have you ever been so pleased about something you wanted to tell somebody, and the only person you could think to tell was the person right there?”

He put his hand over the wrist that was hanging down over his shoulder.

“Just now.”

She leaned over and let her cheek rest against his hair.

“What would you tell them?”

“That I was going to catch the last bus out of town. I could see the stop from where I sat. Every time I thought about leaving, you did something that made me want to see what you would do next.”

“What did I do the first time?”

“Put a finger on the page of your book to hold your place while you looked up. Then I saw you take it out and shut the book.” He was quiet a moment. “I wanted that to be for me.”

She could remember that now. The book had been in her lap. The stranger across the room had been watching her, and she could have kept a finger between the pages. She hadn't even bothered to note the chapter.

“It was.”

Elias let his head fall back against her elbow. She looked down into his face. At the bar this close it would have felt like an excuse to kiss him. Now she just wanted to see what he looked like hearing it.

Later, she boiled water in the kettle. The mug by the sink had a hairline crack. He poured his tea into that one and gave her the other. He drank standing, one hand underneath it in case he needed to catch a drip. Ninja could see the first pale light from the hotel sign reflected in the glass of the window. She switched off the lamp and the sign got brighter. Elias came to stand beside her.

They talked about the things people usually skipped when there were only a few hours to make an impression. What he ate when he couldn't be bothered making dinner. What she listened to while folding washing. How he found out there was a train in the middle of the night if he left the window open in summer. She leaned against him as they talked. Every now and then he turned his face toward her hair and she had to ask him to repeat something.

“I go home in three days.”

“I thought it might be tomorrow.”

“Were you going to let me leave without asking where I live?”

“No. I was trying to decide if breakfast was too soon.”

“For my address, yes. For my number you missed about six opportunities.”

She held out her hand. He reached into his coat, and she saw him look down as his hand touched the inside pocket. He had his head turned far enough that she could only see one eye. For a moment he looked far away.

Then he got his phone out. The home screen was a photograph of a car with its side door off. Ninja put in her number and called herself. Her own phone began to buzz across the bedside table.

“You’ve got until breakfast to come up with something good to say.”

“Can I have another five minutes after that?”

“Depends how good breakfast is.”

She set her phone down. They got into bed. Rain had started again, tapping the sill in a way that made the room seem warmer each time she noticed it. He asked her which side she preferred and she said she usually spread herself all over the place. He said he'd throw in the chair for free if she needed any extra room. When she turned to face him, he was already smiling at her in the light from the window.

They stayed awake long enough that she heard the bakery van deliver to the shop across the road. After a while Elias started telling her about the first car he'd had. Every time her eyes drifted shut he went a little quieter. She could have asked him to stop. She liked hearing his voice, even after she had lost all track of the car. The last thing she remembered before sleeping was reaching across the sheet for his hand and finding it halfway to hers.

A man came up the stairs just after dawn. He passed the crooked picture on the landing and stopped outside room six.
