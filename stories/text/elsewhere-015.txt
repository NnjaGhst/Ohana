# Elsewhere — Episode 15: Window Hours

### Previously on Elsewhere

The station copy showed that Pike and Iona had signed for keys to the meeting room. Elise’s name appeared on the office copy, added in a different spacing. Evelyn told the officer she had used the words without incident to describe the return of a key. In a later summary, those words had been used to describe the night of the fire. There was also a notice about a ferry which had left early. Ninja carried a copy of the relevant line back to the print shop. On the upstairs door she put a small card that said when a person could come in.

### Chapter One — Two Chairs and a Third

The first person Ninja let upstairs on Thursday was Hex, arriving with two bags that clinked at different intervals. One contained the red cups Ada said they were welcome to keep. The other contained four spoons and a jam jar. Ada wanted the jar back because it made a good measure for the washing powder. The spoons, she said, could make up their own minds.

Ninja had washed two cups already. She washed the new ones while Hex put a third chair near the end of the table. He thought it ought to go where a person could look out without turning their back to the door. Ninja tried sitting in it. The window had a view of the pavement below, including the spot from which a woman had said goodbye five minutes before she actually went home. The door was open enough to see the first three steps.

There was a cupboard under the sink, stuck at the bottom from a leak nobody remembered. Hex opened it only as far as he had to before sliding the tea bags inside. Ninja asked what would happen if they put the tea on the table and let people do it themselves. He said they’d be able to find out if he moved his elbow. He moved it.

The two red cups had very narrow handles. She picked one up to see whether she would want to carry it by the handle if it was full. Her smallest finger fit through the gap and the next one got stuck. Hex took the cup by the bottom and set it down nearer its friend. Then he helped her get the finger out.

“I feel like I should have put the rules on the door.”

“What rules have you got so far?”

She had been trying to invent one about how much a person was supposed to tell her. Nothing sounded quite right if she imagined somebody actually reading it. She knew the fire was likely to come up. People might be afraid that she had come here to make all of them explain it. All she really wanted anyone to be able to see from the landing was that they could walk away again.

Hex went downstairs and came back with a folding wedge Rose used when she wanted light from the stairwell. He put it under the door. You could see through to the window from any of the top three steps. A person could call from there if they needed to ask what the room was before they came in.

At four minutes past, someone on the staircase said, “Oh, is that where the music comes from?” Ninja looked at Hex. There was no music. A moment later they heard Rose say something about a radio she kept in a drawer to stop it falling down. They went back to setting the cups out.

At the next chime from the street, Ninja slid the station copy into an old envelope and put it in the bottom drawer. She put her aunt’s photograph on the sill beside the one she had left there earlier. It showed Elise standing at the outdoor tap, spraying dirt from the boots of a child who was plainly wearing one shoe from a completely different pair. Ninja didn’t know the child. She could see her sister had already got one sleeve wet before anybody thought to take the picture.

She told Hex that if nothing happened in the next hour, she might ask Rose if she wanted a third photograph printed. If somebody came in, she could ask Rose later. She didn’t think the room had to be very busy in order for it to have been a good idea to open it.

### Chapter Two — The Woman Who Waited

The next person didn’t make a sound on the stair. Ninja looked up from a cup and saw the shoulder of someone’s coat where the door began. It was Iona, who had come all the way upstairs before she decided whether she wanted to put her head round.

She had brought a clean tea towel. She said it belonged to Elise and then she said that wasn’t quite true. Elise had borrowed it from her once and brought it back so clean that Iona decided it probably belonged with Elise. When she finally put it in a drawer, she hadn’t known she would ever want to give it to anybody.

Ninja took the tea towel. It was folded to show a thin red stripe near one end. She had no recollection of it. She put it on the table and smoothed it with both hands so it could be used the next time somebody put a wet cup down.

“Are you here because the officer called?”

Iona said she had a time to speak with the officer the following morning. She had called earlier to say that she didn’t think seeing the new papers could help her remember the room any better than she already did. There was a detail she’d got wrong in part of the older statement, and she thought if she started to take guesses about it now, it would be harder to say what she’d actually seen.

Ninja asked which detail, if Iona was happy to say. Iona went over to the table but didn’t sit. She had remembered leaving the spare key on the side of the station counter. In the past few days she’d remembered the click of somebody dropping it into a tin. She had taken that to mean it was Evelyn. The thing she could see in her head, the actual hand, had turned out to be her own.

Hex moved away from the windowsill so Iona could have a clear way to the chair. She sat down after a while. The hot water was still in the kettle. Ninja carried a cup over to her and let her decide whether she wanted any.

Iona remembered a poem Elise kept pinned above the standing desk at work. She had no idea who had written it. She remembered a line about sitting close enough to people to forget how silent the room was. She had taken some pieces of paper down from the wall after the fire because she saw an officer coming with a camera and thought nobody else would bother to put them back. In the end she hadn’t put that one back either.

“If you find it, could you leave it here a while?”

“If I find it, I will bring it in myself.”

Ninja understood the difference between the promise they’d made and saying any of it was forgiven. She’d asked for a poem, if it still existed. Iona could keep her word about that. Then Iona looked past the window at the narrow yard and asked if Ninja thought of it as a good thing that she was coming here.

“I think it’s good that you asked.”

Hex brought his own cup back from the sill and took the chair by the door. Rose wanted to know whether the top of the stairs was warm enough, so he went out there to find out. Ninja saw him across the landing, leaning on the stair rail while Rose looked for the place in the ceiling where everybody knew heat went missing. She and Iona shared the rest of the tea without talking about the fire. It turned out she liked the cup with the awkward handle because she had a small hand.

### Chapter Three — Something to Bring

After Iona left, Rose came up to tell Hex that an elderly gentleman was looking for a person who’d had a workshop by the east road. She didn’t know whether to send him upstairs. Hex went down a few steps and saw glasses with a wire frame, thin enough at the nose that he could see the color of the man’s skin under them. He knew it was Leonard before he got close enough to see the face.

Leonard asked whether Hex was still fixing things. Hex said he tried, and asked if he could show him the room. Leonard said he thought he’d failed to bring something. He came up with a bag from the chemist which contained one bar of soap and a strip of paper folded into six. The paper was an old staff pass with a hole burnt at each corner. He spread it out on the table with his fingers and waited to see if anybody remembered it.

Iona had left a little warmth in the chair by the window. Leonard took the one nearer the door. Hex told him the officer had heard his name from Iona. Nobody here needed him to say anything he’d come to say to the officer another day. He was welcome to stay until he had drunk his tea, if he thought that might be all he wanted.

Leonard remembered Hex as the man who brought a door handle into the offices in the middle of an inspection. It had been to show that the front latch wasn’t made for a door as thick as that one. In the middle of the busiest room, an inspector had turned the knob in his hand as if he expected it to open some other door. Leonard had gone out to find a ruler so there might be something on the table that everybody knew how to use.

He asked Ninja what Elise was to her after Hex introduced them. She told him. He straightened the old pass again because it was trying to fold up.

“I don’t know as much about her as I used to think,” he said. “She had a man in the office who wanted orders carried upstairs before she’d finished reading them. I saw her give one back to him with a little bit of tape where he’d tried to tear the end off. That was the first time I understood who was asking whom to hurry.”

“Was that Pike?”

“No. It was his assistant. Mr. Pike was the one who had to send the same order down to the other office again to be stamped.”

It was the stamps that Leonard had come to talk about. When they moved the filing racks, he set aside one card for every office that asked to receive a revised copy. There was a room in the old building where they tied up the outgoing bags. The last column of the card was for the name of the person who had come downstairs to say their copy was missing. He had asked for the cards when the records moved. A manager sent him a letter saying they were property of the office and that he had no reason to want them.

This pass was from the day he helped the movers. It had a date written on it, and the name of the man who came to collect the last box. That might be enough for the officer to find the cards he’d been thinking about. He could tell her where the boxes had been when he signed himself out for the last time.

Ninja asked if he wanted a fresh envelope. She had a plain one from Rose, just the right size to hold the pass flat. He finished his first cup of tea before he answered. Then he took the pass between two fingers and slipped it in himself.

### Chapter Four — One Name at a Time

Leonard left a number for the officer. Hex went all the way down the stairs with him, carrying the folded bag with the bar of soap. At the front door Leonard told him he could still remember how heavy everything in the old building felt. Door handles, the keys, the wood behind the keys. He’d gone into lighter buildings since then and caught himself leaning into a door that was already open.

Hex waited until Leonard got to the corner. When he came upstairs again Ninja was arranging three empty envelopes at the end of the table. One for the officer’s number, one for people who left something they wanted back, and one for photographs. The middle one had a smudge where she had written the word evidence and rubbed it out.

Hex picked up the clean tea towel and rubbed at his thumb. He still had the mark from the radiator repair. She watched him stop when it didn’t come away. If one of them had to decide whose papers would go in which envelope, he asked, how would they know whether there was room to change their mind?

“I’ll ask before I write somebody’s name down. If someone wants to tell me their own thing, I’ll hear it. If they want the officer, I can give them the number.”

Hex agreed. He put his phone down on the table. It was turned face down. She reached over and moved hers the same way, which meant they couldn’t both see how long there was before they were allowed to shut the door.

She said she wanted to tell him something without writing it down. Today Leonard had spent less than twenty minutes by the door and he looked shaken when he left. If there were other people who used to come here to speak to Elise, she thought they might be just as shaken if somebody asked them to put their names on a page. She liked knowing the time they would go home could have something to do with when the buses ran. It didn’t all have to come down to the time she had written beside the latch.

Hex said maybe they'd put that in a separate notice when she knew whether she really wanted one. A chair could be near a door because Rose had saved it from the rain. It could stay near that door because Leonard had sat in it. People didn’t have to hear the second reason unless they asked.

Ninja went to get the other two mugs from the sink. They left a ring on the new tea towel. Iona had brought it upstairs tidy enough for a parcel. Ninja left the rings where they were until Hex had finished his tea and then took the towel over to the radiator to dry. She asked if the window could be open a little. He had agreed earlier to help her shut the door if she wanted. He opened the window all the way before closing it part of the way, letting a square of wet air come in first.

There was a street light beside the print shop. They could see it from the table at an angle. It came on a little at a time, then went out altogether. Hex said the man who looked after it was supposed to come on Monday. Ninja took him at his word, even though on Monday he might need the street light to be going wrong at exactly the time he was there to look at it.

She could see somebody standing below, nearly far enough out from the shop for the rain to wet their collar. After a while the person came in under the light from the downstairs window. She heard Rose open the inner door and speak in just the same voice she used when the fire didn’t come up at all.

### Chapter Five — The Visitor on the Landing

It was five fifty-eight when a woman reached the landing. She wore a yellow waterproof under which she was still wearing the embroidered patch of a ferry employee. She had folded her hood down without shaking it and a little water ran out when she leaned to look at the card. She told Ninja her name was Jo. She was fairly sure she’d met Elise on the ferry and wanted to make sure she had the right sister before she went any further.

Ninja asked Jo in. Jo looked at the time and said she only meant to leave a message. Hex put the kettle on again. She stood on the mat another second and then lifted one foot to see whether both her boots were wet. They were. Ninja found a flattened shopping bag for her to stand them on if she wanted them off. Jo said they might as well sit while the rain did whatever it wanted to.

She’d heard Evelyn had been looking for Sal. They had worked alternate shifts at the ferry. At the time there were so few permanent people on the desk that the lost-property cupboard had a key anybody with a waterproof might use. People put dry gloves in there so often that workers tended to look inside before buying new ones.

Jo drew an old envelope from her inside pocket. It was addressed to the ferry shelter but had a line through the name of the ferry company. Beneath it there was a little box with an L written in pencil. She said Sal had kept it in his old blue folder for property collected after the ticket office closed. About four years ago, when he was getting ready to go off the roster, he’d remembered a call from the station and asked Jo if she knew anybody called L.

She didn’t. Leonard, she told Ninja, was the only person she could think of who used to collect things for the Directorate. Most people called him Len. But Genesis, who had worked the widow shift at the ticket desk, used the first letter of the place where a thing went, and for most things that was Locker. Jo knew there were at least two plausible meanings for that letter.

“Can I see what’s inside?”

“Sal put it in a second envelope. I asked if I could bring the outside one. I think he wants to be there if somebody opens the other.”

Ninja found she could still sit with her hands flat in her lap. Whatever was in the other envelope, Jo had brought something she was allowed to bring. She thought she knew now how much work that could take.

Jo looked across at the window. Her next shift was on Saturday morning at the far bank. If Ninja wanted to come down, she would ask Sal to meet them. She could try to make sure both of them knew he was going to be there. It was a hard thing to take an envelope to somebody who had packed away the rest of that afternoon before anybody told him it might matter.

“Tell him I’ll come. Please tell him I’ll stop if he wants to stop.”

Jo took a breath which made the hood rustle. She said she might need that said too, when she called him. Hex found a clean cup, poured tea, and put the handle the right way round for her. She drank three mouthfuls, then took a long enough look at the room to know what she would be telling Sal about it.

### Chapter Six — Two Minutes Past

By the time Jo left, it was twelve minutes past the time on the door. Ninja thought they’d only gone two minutes over. It was possible the extra ten minutes had belonged to the part when she’d decided she was going to be able to wait. Hex closed the door behind Jo and removed the wedge. He set it down on the sill where Ninja could reach it from the chair.

She could see a little yellow light at the ferry landing through the rain. It came on behind the place where the roofs went lower. The buildings hid the actual river from where she was standing. She only knew she was seeing the light over water because the boats carried it across the gap on nights they were out.

Hex asked if she wanted him to tell the officer there was a time for Saturday. Ninja said she would call after walking home and asked if he would sit with her while the phone rang. She didn’t think she would need him to say anything. She knew herself well enough to know she might change her mind, and he knew her well enough to know he could wait a while anyway.

They found a teaspoon on the table and two more on the sill. Hex put them in Ada’s pot and folded the tea towel even though it was still damp in the middle. He shook it open a second later. Ninja laughed. She had been watching herself do the very same thing the week before.

He took her empty cup, then put his free hand out for hers when he came back. She took it and stayed by the window. The ferry light had a blue edge when she blinked. She said she wanted to ride as far as the landing if the brake was still working. Hex said it would be a good distance for the first ride if she took the long side of the square. He lifted the switch on the little lamp, which made the window a little less like a mirror.

“If I leave while it’s bright, I think I could ride far enough to see the river.”

“If the hill’s very steep, even a small amount of light will get you there.”

She asked if he was planning to hold onto the coat like he’d said. He said he’d keep it dry. She turned to see him properly and found that he had his newer coat on, the one that rode up on his neck on one side when he did the zip up too quickly. She put it right. His hand stayed where it was while she did it. A minute later the street light below started properly, and the window turned from a square reflection into a view again.

She checked the door card before she went. The reverse was blank. There was space to write something about Saturdays if they ever decided they wanted to be there. She left it blank, turned the two mugs upside down on the dry part of the cloth, and carried the clean envelope home in the wide pocket of her coat.

Down by the gate she could hear the noise of the bicycle cover, pulled half tight over the rail, though she couldn’t see the bicycle from there. Hex had put the bike inside the print shop and left the little bell where it had always been, with a clean patch of rust round the screw so he would know which way it turned. For the moment that was as far as the job needed to go.
