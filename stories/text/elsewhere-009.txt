# Elsewhere — Episode 9: The Ferry Shelter

### Previously on Elsewhere

Ruth remembered a card with a blue swallow in Elise’s pocket. It led Ninja and Hex to Rose at the print shop. Rose had delivered the first copy of the records to a woman now known as Alice Reed, and she had kept a letter Elise wrote to Ninja. In it Elise talked about Sunday lunch, milk, and a cake tin she was planning to argue over.

Alice agreed to meet them at the old ferry shelter. She wanted both of them there.

### Chapter One — A Coat on the Back of a Chair

Ruth came through the kitchen with a coat over her arm. Ada looked at it, looked at the weather outside, and continued to scrape marmalade from the side of the jar.

“I think I ought to go home,” Ruth said. “My daughter will have started worrying about what I’m eating.”

“Tell her you’ve been getting cake for breakfast,” Ada said. “She’ll take it very badly and come and get you.”

Ruth set the coat on the back of a chair. She did not sit down. Ninja was beginning to recognise the things people did when they wanted to leave and were waiting for someone to tell them they could.

“Did you tell her about the recording?”

“I said there was something I had to give back to someone. She asked how long I’d had it. That was the first time I said five years out loud.”

Ada put a cup in front of the empty chair.

“What did she say?”

“She asked if I’d like her to drive.”

Ruth did sit down then. She wrapped her hands around the cup without drinking from it. In the end her daughter said she would collect her at four. Ada began making sandwiches for the journey on the slightly excessive assumption that there might be two separate journeys.

Ninja showed Ruth the corner of the letter that asked her to buy milk.

“You know,” Ruth said, “I think that was the third reminder she’d written that week. Once there was a receipt sticking out of the top of her bag and it said milk in block capitals. She’d bought batteries and come home without it.”

Ninja wrote that down on another sheet of paper. Since hearing Elise’s voice, she had felt a new urgency about the ordinary things people remembered. She could keep them beside the hard parts. A detail could be true without being useful to a case.

Ruth caught sight of what she was doing and laughed.

“Tell you another thing. Your sister could never open a packet of biscuits without making it sound like she’d broken into a house.”

Ninja wrote that down too.

After Ruth took her cup back to the kitchen, Ninja went upstairs for the letter. She had thought about taking it to the ferry shelter, then thought about the damp paving and what would happen if she pulled it from her pocket too close to the river. She found a clear pocket from an old folder and slid the envelope into it. Then she slid it out again.

There was no particular reason for Alice to see the letter. There was a good reason for Ninja to have it near her. Somewhere in between those two thoughts was an unfamiliar one. The letter could stay in a room she knew, and she could go to the ferry as herself, with questions she’d decided to ask while there was light.

She placed it on the bedside table. At the last moment she took a pencil instead. The pencil had a rubber on it with an uneven edge. She had made the edge that way over a whole evening because the robber in the paperback had turned out to be the horse owner’s cousin and she thought that sort of plot deserved a comment.

Downstairs, Hex was waiting under the porch roof. He had brought two rain jackets, one folded over his arm. She held up her own coat and he put the spare over his shoulder. On the walk he told her that he had nearly bought a third rain jacket the week before, because Tomas had called the first two fine and he’d taken the remark to mean they would both leak.

They paused at the top of the steps. In the distance there was a long flat strip of water and a parcel of light over the roofs. People were placing crates outside a shop. Ninja wanted to remember going down the steps on purpose, as a thing she had decided to do herself. She took her hand off the rail once, then put it back because the steps were slippery, and followed Hex down to the water.

### Chapter Two — A Little Shelter

The old ferry stop had a square roof and three sides, which meant that when it rained from the east the shelter was more of an opinion. The village had stopped running a ferry years ago. You could still see the edge of the steps, cut neatly down into the river, and the bollard where someone had once tied a boat before going to get the change for a passenger.

Ninja and Hex waited under the shelter with two cups of tea from the café. The morning was cold and bright in short bursts. The wind dropped every so often, and in the gaps they could hear water around the bottom step.

“Did you ever take the ferry?”

“Once. When I came to look at the cottage. The man next to me brought a houseplant.”

“Was it going somewhere in particular?”

“He said it needed an outing.”

She looked at the opposite bank. “I respect that,” she almost said aloud. Then she realised it didn’t need to be said. He knew from her face.

A woman came along the riverside path carrying a supermarket bag. She wore a red wool hat and a blue jacket with reflective strips along the cuffs. Her walk looked very purposeful until she got to the last paved slab before the shelter. Then she stopped.

“Am I early?” she asked.

“Exactly on time, if you go by my phone.”

“I’ve been walking back and forth since ten.”

Hex moved his tea to the floor and made room for the bag. Alice sat at the end of the bench. She kept the handles over her wrist, as though she were on a train and had been told her stop was next.

Ninja offered her the other tea. Alice took it with both hands.

“Does it have sugar?”

“No. I bought a packet in case you wanted one.”

“Keep it. I used to have three,” Alice said. “It’s astonishing how many things about me have changed without anybody changing my description on a form.”

The river moved past them in a broad piece of grey light. They could see the small café safely behind the shelter and a man walking his dog by the railings. Alice took a sip and let her shoulders drop.

### Chapter Three — The Other Side of a Form

Alice told them she used to sign off the information the council sent out when a person disappeared. She learned to ask whether anybody was still waiting to hear from them. Sometimes the answer was yes, and the yes came from a person who had already been put on hold twice.

Her own name appeared in an internal register after she refused to sign a transfer form for a woman she had met the week before. A colleague showed her the two versions of the register. One said she had been transferred. The other already had a date beside her name.

“The date was the following Tuesday,” she said. “I rang my sister and told her I’d see her Monday. I thought we had that much time.”

She placed the tea by her knee.

“Elise rang me on Sunday. She didn’t explain why she was worried. She asked me if I wanted to meet her somewhere that served breakfast all day. I said I’d rather have a cup of tea at my own kitchen table, so that’s where she came.”

Ninja could imagine her sister accepting a cup of tea with her coat still on. She could imagine the funny little twist of her mouth before she said something that sounded straightforward and would change the rest of a day.

“Did you know she was in danger too?”

“I knew she had stayed too long in my kitchen. I remember that because when she left I had to stand up and turn on a light.”

Alice slipped the bag handles off her wrist. She said there was an envelope in the bag. Rose had delivered it six days before the warehouse fire, when Alice was staying with someone who sold fruit from a lay-by and asked no personal questions until the second week.

“Rose told me that if I didn’t open it, I could go back to my life. I think she thought that would make it easier to decide.”

“What did you do?”

“I opened it. Then I went out and picked apples for the rest of the afternoon, because it was the only thing I knew how to do with both hands.”

Ninja looked at the river. The wind put a small wave across the grey light. On the other bank somebody had taken the rain cover off a bench and hung it over a fence to dry.

After a while Alice asked Hex whether he was still willing to look at a form he didn’t remember signing.

They let the bag sit between them. Alice stirred her tea with the handle of the wooden spoon because the spoon end had a knot in it. It was the kind of movement somebody makes when they mean to say something as soon as they can find an ordinary way to begin.

She told them about working under a different name. The first time she needed new glasses, she went somewhere far enough away that she could feel reasonable about turning back. The optician asked how she’d heard of the shop. Alice said she just saw it from the bus. He took it as a compliment and asked which way the bus had been going. For several years after she hated questions like that. There was usually an answer people were expecting, and she had to spend enough time finding it without making the silence noticeable.

Now she lived close to allotments. The people there called her Alice because she’d introduced herself that way. One of them had asked her to water some beans, and she’d carried on watering them for a week before finding out their owner came on evening shifts and had been watering them himself. The beans did magnificently. Alice wasn’t sure whether the two of them had got anything right apart from turning up.

Ninja watched a line of ripples travel over the steps. On the opposite bank a small red jacket showed up in the trees and disappeared. For a little while she didn’t need to ask a follow-up question. She wanted Alice to know she’d heard the bean story as carefully as the part about the order.

Hex asked whether she’d like the tea warmed up. Alice said she’d started enjoying it cold once she could leave a cup wherever she wanted, knowing it would still be in the same place when she came back.

Then she took the envelope from the bag and laid it flat across her knees.

### Chapter Four — An Arrow in the Margin

The form was inside the envelope in a clear sleeve. Alice let him hold the sleeve at the edges. There was a dated instruction, a place, and an ordinary rectangular box for a signature.

Hex recognised his own name. The last letter was written too low because the person who signed it had seen only one sample of his signature, on a different kind of paper. It was the very first thought that came to him. It took another moment before he realised that the signature was false.

“I didn’t sign it,” he said.

“I know.”

He looked up.

“Elise knew you hadn’t signed this copy. She’d seen you draw an arrow in the margin of the one they gave you. I used to draw an arrow too. You want someone to look at what’s missing, you can’t point at nothing, so you point at the place it should have been.”

Alice set the envelope across her knee. “I want to know whether you’ll tell the police that you signed the first version. They may try to make this about a forgery, which is easier to explain than what the instructions were for.”

Hex turned the sleeve over. On the back he could faintly make out the place where somebody had made a mark before the copy was taken.

“I will,” he said.

“Were you there when your question came back with the answer taken off?”

“Yes.”

“Then tell them that too.”

Ninja listened to the water and the wind. His eyes had gone almost entirely to the page. She waited until he had put it down and then asked whether he needed a second.

“Perhaps two,” he said.

She waited with him. Alice untwisted the supermarket bag once, then put her hands flat on the bench.

“My sister left eleven names on her recording. She said the twelfth person had to get home safely after telling her story. If you want to go to the officer, I can walk with you. You can also arrange it yourself.”

Alice gave a small nod.

“I’d like somebody to come along. You can both stay out of the room when I get to the part I haven’t told anyone.”

“Of course.”

They packed the paperwork back into the envelope and walked toward the café to make the call. Alice went between them for a while. Where the path narrowed, Ninja slowed down and let her choose which side of the fence she wanted to walk on.

### Chapter Five — A Table Near the Door

The café had a bell exactly like the one at the print shop and a machine that sounded as if it objected to making coffee before lunch. Alice chose the table nearest the door, then asked the woman behind the counter if she could borrow a pencil.

“What are you writing?” Hex asked when she had drawn a line down the middle of a napkin.

“The things I’m able to say today, and the things I need to hear myself say first.”

“And is a napkin enough room?”

“It’s got two sides.”

Ninja bought a piece of fruit cake and, without really thinking, cut it into three. Later she would remember the white plates and the little white trails where the paper napkins had stuck to the damp tabletop. It was one of those mornings that would have sounded unbearable if someone described it to her in advance. What she remembered best as they got up to go was how good the cake was, and how Alice asked for an extra slice to take home.

The officer arrived in a dark blue coat. Alice introduced herself under her current name and asked to see an identity card. The officer showed it to her. Then they sat by the other end of the room so Alice could talk with the door still in view.

Ninja and Hex waited at a table beside the window. A little while later Ninja asked whether they could stop somewhere on the way home where neither of them had ever been.

“I know a street with a vending machine that will take a coin and return the same coin,” he said.

“Do you think it would give us two back if we asked nicely?”

“No, but we can give it an afternoon to think about it.”

They waited long enough for the light to shift on the floor. When Alice returned, she tucked the cake carefully into her bag and said she had given the officer the original papers. She’d asked for a receipt. It was in her other pocket.

Before she left, she touched Ninja’s elbow.

“Your sister told me what you look like when you’re listening. She said you go very still and people think you’re cross.”

“Sometimes I am.”

“I know,” Alice said. “It helps that you listen anyway.”

While the officer packed her folder, Alice stopped by their table. She said she’d told her sister about going to the ferry. Her sister had asked whether she wanted to come round for supper. So far she had responded with a question about the buses, which her sister had correctly taken to mean yes.

Hex came outside with her while Ninja settled the bill. They watched two people with walking poles consider the board outside the café. It listed soup of the day without saying which soup. One of them seemed to be asking whether that meant the soup was only known once the day was over.

“I’m glad you came,” Hex said.

Alice folded down the top of the cake bag. “I thought I’d be glad when it was over. I haven’t found out what that feels like yet.”

“You can always have supper first.”

She smiled. “That’s quite a sensible thing to say.”

They heard the café door open, and Ninja came back out. Alice took the riverside footpath because the sun had broken through on that side. She lifted the cake bag once in farewell. Then she went at her own pace without anybody walking close behind her.

Ninja and Hex stayed where they were until a passing dog pulled its owner round the edge of the bench and briefly put its head between them. It looked at each of them in turn, found neither had cake, and decided that further investigation would waste everybody’s time.

On the walk up, they passed the place where Alice had first come into view. A fallen leaf was pinned under a stone. Ninja could see which way the wind had bent it. Nothing about the afternoon was as complete as she wanted it to be, and still it meant something that Alice was free to choose soup, or cake, or supper at her sister’s. She took the longer turning up the hill, where there was more light.

### Chapter Six — The Long Way Up

On the way back toward the hill, Ninja and Hex came to a noticeboard with a map so out of date it still showed the ferry. Ninja ran a finger over the little blue line that crossed the water. At its far end was an arrow in the shape of a swallow.

“I wish we could have taken her across.”

Hex knew which her she meant.

A few metres further on an old postbox stood in a patch of light. Hex took his hands out of his pockets.

“There’s something else I can give the officer,” he said. “My own statement about the operation. I started one years ago. Most of it is about what other people did.”

“Would you want to read that version if you were me?”

“No. I wouldn’t.”

Ninja looked at him without rushing to help him past the moment.

“Let me know when you’ve sent it.”

He said he would. Then he pulled out his phone and showed her an old photograph she’d never seen. Elise was beside a bicycle with a milk bottle in the front basket. She had one hand up to shade her eyes, smiling at somebody just out of frame.

“Could you send me that one?”

“Of course.”

She waited for the picture to arrive before putting the phone in her pocket. Somewhere on the climb up the hill the sun went behind a cloud. The pavement looked the same. They took the rest of it more slowly.
