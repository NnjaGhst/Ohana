# Elsewhere — Episode 5: The Things We Don’t Say

### Previously on Elsewhere

Ninja came to Bellwether for a quiet holiday. She met a mechanic named Elias Ward and had begun to look forward to running into him. Elias is really Hex, a former Directorate operative who was there the night Ninja’s sister, Elise, died.

After they talked at the old bridge, someone sent Hex a photograph of Ninja taken from the far end of the crossing. Beneath it came a message.

You can’t watch every road.

Hex put his phone down on the workshop bench and told Tomas they needed to talk.

### Chapter One — Take Your Time

Tomas switched off the generator. Its last little shudder rattled a teaspoon against a saucer on the workbench.

“Start wherever you can,” he said.

Hex had spent five years imagining how he would explain himself if it ever came to this. In his head he always sounded calm. Standing in the workshop, he could only think that Tomas had lent him a good coat his first winter in Bellwether, and that he’d never actually given it back.

“Elias Ward isn’t my name.”

Tomas looked at him for a moment. Then he moved the teaspoon off the saucer.

“Do you want me to use a different one?”

That was the first question. Hex had prepared for considerably worse.

“Hex,” he said. “Most people who knew me before called me Hex.”

“All right, Hex.”

Hearing it in Tomas’s voice, in a room where he knew which drawer stuck and which floorboard complained, was almost too much.

He told him about the Directorate. How useful it was to them to have people who could make a terrible thing sound like a routine afternoon. He told him Elise’s name. He told him about the moment he’d understood what the operation would cost, and how by then the people in charge had decided the cost was acceptable.

“I tried to stop it,” he said. “I also helped get it that far.”

When he finished, Tomas sat down on an upturned crate. He needed both hands to hold his tea. Hex couldn’t tell whether that was because it was hot or because of what he’d just heard.

“Does she know you knew her sister?”

“No.”

“Does she know a stranger took her picture?”

“No.”

Tomas looked up at him.

“Which of those would you want to be told first?”

Hex looked toward the window. Outside, a delivery driver was leaning against his van eating an apple. The woman from the florist had just come out to sweep the pavement. He knew the driver’s name. The florist knew how he took his tea. He had always thought of these things as evidence that he’d learned to live somewhere.

Now he was trying to work out whether they were things he could leave behind again.

“First I tell her about the photograph,” he said.

“And after that?”

“I don’t know how much she’ll want to hear.”

“Then give her a chance to tell you.”

Hex took his coat off its hook. The pocket lining still had a blue thread where Tomas’s wife had mended it years before.

At the door he looked back. “I should have given you this coat back.”

Tomas had picked up the teaspoon. He was turning it between his fingers.

“I gave you the coat,” he said. “I lent you a screwdriver. And if you’re about to leave again, I want my screwdriver.”

Hex laughed once. He hadn’t expected to be able to.

“I’m going to Briar House,” he said. “I’ll be back.”

### Chapter Two — The Good Shelf

At Briar House, Ninja was helping Ada choose which books deserved the good shelf.

There were two shelves in the sitting room. As far as Ninja could see, they had equal access to daylight, and neither had a particularly impressive view. Ada insisted that one was good and the other was where books went to think about what they’d done.

“What did this one do?”

“Three hundred pages, and the woman still married the man who stole her horse.”

“Maybe she thought she could get it back in the settlement.”

Ada considered that with the paperback held halfway between the shelves.

“You can put it on the good one, but it goes at the end.”

Ninja took it. She had an unreasonable tenderness for Ada’s rules, most of which could be overturned by making her laugh. Every house she’d stayed in since Elise died had felt like a place she ought to leave exactly as she’d found it. Here she had already moved the armchair closer to the window, and Ada had put a little table beside it without a word.

Outside, somebody pushed open the gate.

Ada peered over her glasses. “Your mechanic’s here.”

“He’s the village’s mechanic. I haven’t bought him.”

“Good. Where would you keep him?”

Ninja found herself checking her reflection anyway. Her hair had dried in three directions. She tucked one stray strand behind her ear and decided the others could fend for themselves.

Hex was closing the gate behind him. He put a hand on the latch to keep it from slamming. When he looked up and saw her, the little smile she’d been expecting didn’t come.

“Is Tomas all right?”

“Yes. He’s fine. Do you have a minute?”

“I’ve got several. Ada thinks I should spend one of them defending a horse thief.”

He looked so entirely bewildered that under any other circumstances she’d have left it there and watched him try to work it out.

“Come in. You can tell me what’s wrong in the kitchen.”

“Would you rather talk somewhere people can see us?”

That was a strange thing for him to ask. Ninja thought about the kitchen, and the windows at the front of the house, and the fact that she could hear Ada in the sitting room putting a book back where it belonged.

“Kitchen’s good. Ada will be in the next room.”

“Okay.”

He waited for her to go first. She noticed that too.

She set the kettle going. Hex pulled his phone from his coat pocket, but he didn’t unlock it until she had turned back toward him.

“Someone sent me a photograph of you,” he said. “Today, at the bridge. I think you should see it.”

She reached out her hand.

### Chapter Three — The Far End of the Bridge

For a second, Ninja thought the photograph was of somebody else.

She recognised the pink hair. She recognised the old parapet and the slight bend in the river. But she knew the day from the other side of her own eyes, from where she’d been standing, and it took her a moment to put the two views together.

“That’s when I took a picture for Elise.”

Hex was standing on the other side of the table. He didn’t say anything.

“I mean, I forgot. Just for a second. I took it and went to send it, and then I remembered I couldn’t.”

She pinched the photo larger. At its edge there was a scrap of a woman’s shopping bag, white with red lettering. She tried to remember someone passing her. All she could remember was the river and the man in the delivery van who had lifted his fingers from the wheel to say hello.

“How long were they there?”

“I don’t know. The first message came after you left the Lantern last night.”

He showed her that one too. The car pulling away didn’t appear in it. For that part she had only his word.

“Why would someone send this to you?”

“I think they’re trying to get me to leave.”

“Then why didn’t you?”

“I wanted to tell you first.”

She set the phone down very carefully on the table. The heat from the kettle was making one of the windows mist up at the bottom. There was a clean tea towel folded on the sill, and she wanted to do something with her hands, so she went over and wiped a clear patch in the glass.

“I need to ask you something. When we met at the pub, did you already know who I was?”

“I suspected. I was sure when you told me your name.”

She kept looking out the window.

“Where had you heard it before?”

“From Elise.”

The kettle clicked off. The kitchen was quiet enough that she could hear the first few drops of rain striking the tin lid on the bin outside.

“Say her name again.”

“Elise.”

No one in Bellwether had had any reason to say it aloud until now. Ninja was surprised by how much she hated that he knew how. She remembered a man at the funeral getting the stress wrong. She had corrected him, and then she’d felt awful, because it was hardly the man’s fault and because there would be fewer and fewer occasions to correct anybody as time went on.

“How did you know my sister?”

“We worked together. Not for very long. Long enough for her to tell me about you.”

Ninja gripped the folded tea towel.

“And do you know what happened to her?”

“Yes.”

The answer came so quietly she could hear the rain through it.

She turned around. Hex was still in the same spot, his hands flat on the table where she could see them.

“Tell me one thing about her first. Something you couldn’t know from a report.”

### Chapter Four — What the Apricots Were For

Hex had thought about what he would say if she asked him about Elise’s last day. He hadn’t thought about what he would say if she asked him to remember an ordinary one.

“She kept dried apricots in the glove compartment,” he said. “There was a packet in every car she borrowed. I asked her why, and she said men were much easier to deal with after you’d fed them.”

Ninja’s mouth twitched. It was the kind of smile somebody could mistake for pain if they’d never seen her try to hold one in.

“I bought those for her. She hated bananas because they made everything in her bag smell like bananas.”

“I never knew that.”

“Did she talk about me a lot?”

“Once she told me you’d put a chair together without the instructions and been furious when she pointed out it was a footstool.”

“It was a really tall footstool.”

Hex’s laugh was barely a breath. Ninja looked at him, and for a moment there was a third person in the kitchen, standing just where the afternoon light came in, waiting for one of them to make the story worse by adding another detail.

Then it was only the two of them again.

“The report said it was an accident.”

“It wasn’t.”

One of the legs of Ninja’s chair caught on a ridge in the floor. She gave it a second tug and sat. Hex waited.

He told her he had worked for the Directorate. He told her the fire had been planned and what he himself had done to make the plan possible, before he knew Elise was in the building. He had taken an instruction at face value that he should have questioned. By the time he asked, nobody was answering him.

“Did you go in?”

“I did.”

“Did you find her?”

His eyes went to hers.

“Yes.”

Ninja folded the tea towel again, though it was already folded.

“I’m going to ask you more about that. Just not today.”

“Okay.”

Hex knew there were more things he should say. He could have filled the room with them. But she’d given him a clear answer and he could begin by respecting it.

“What is your name?”

“People called me Hex.”

“And when I tell you to go, which of you am I telling?”

“Me,” he said. “It’s the same man either way.”

She stood up. For a moment he thought she might take his coat off the chair and put it in his hands. Instead she went to the door and opened it.

“I want you to leave me the messages and the photograph. Then I need you to go.”

He sent them to her from where he stood. He waited until she checked her phone and nodded. When he went out she was looking past him at the rain.

“And don’t leave Bellwether without telling me.”

He turned back.

“I won’t.”

She closed the door herself.

Hex took three steps into the rain before he realised he was still holding the phone he had meant to put back in his pocket. The screen was bright enough to light his hand. In the entry for Ninja, saved only a minute ago when he sent her the photograph, the name sat above an empty space where a picture would go.

He locked the screen.

At the corner he stopped under the shelter of the old bus stop. On its noticeboard was a flyer for a harvest supper, pinned over a timetable. He had repaired the stove in the hall where they held it every year. In one corner the paper had curled, revealing part of the timetable underneath. There was a bus at five thirty. If he walked to the station afterward, he could be two towns away by the time anybody had cleared the supper dishes.

A few years earlier he’d left a room with a cup of coffee still warm on the table. He remembered that because the landlady had brought it up herself, in a mug with a picture of a badger. She’d made him a second cup the next morning before remembering he wasn’t there. He only knew because he’d called her two months later to send her the key and she’d told him. He had spent those two months thinking she would have noticed the empty room first.

He walked past the bus stop.

The road curved downhill and came out near the bridge. He was going to see what the photographer could have seen from the far side. Then he remembered telling Ninja he would leave her the messages. She had asked him for something she could keep and make her own decisions about. A new photograph, a list of suspicions about a car, or the name of someone he hadn’t spoken to in years wouldn’t make her afternoon any easier if he threw all of it at her now.

At the bridge he looked over the parapet anyway. The water had risen a fraction since morning. The largest stone was almost covered. He had seen something in Ninja’s face that morning when she’d looked back at him, when she’d said the village couldn’t repair itself. She had liked being able to make him laugh. He had liked it too.

He let himself remember that for a moment.

Then he rang Tomas.

“Keep the workshop open a little longer,” he said. “I’m coming back the long way.”

“Take an umbrella if you pass the pub. Maeve’s got about forty that nobody claimed.”

Hex had left his own umbrella at the Lantern three winters ago. He went round to the side door and knocked. Maeve poked her head out, saw him standing there dripping, and handed him a red one with a bent handle.

“Bring it back whenever,” she said.

It was his.

### Chapter Five — The Chair by the Window

Ada came into the kitchen ten minutes later, wearing her gardening shoes. She took one look at Ninja and took the shoes back off.

“What do you need?”

“Could you sit in here for a bit?”

“Yes.”

Ada fetched the mending basket, found a pair of socks in it that she’d been meaning to deal with since spring, and sat. She took a long time separating her threads. They stayed like that until Ninja could hear the quiet as something other than a space she had to fill.

“Someone followed me this morning.”

Ada put the sewing down.

“Are they outside now?”

“I don’t think so. They might have been earlier. I’m going to show you a picture.”

When Ada had seen it, she got up and checked the back door was locked. She came back without pretending she’d only gone to pick something up. Together they called the local police number. The officer took Ninja’s name, asked for the original message and photograph, and said someone would ring back. It was immensely ordinary, giving a stranger her name and the address of the house while Ada wrote down a reference number on the back of a shopping list.

Afterward Ninja carried her mug into the sitting room. The horse-thief book was still on the end of the good shelf.

“I was really happy this morning.”

Ada sat in the chair opposite. “I know. You put marmalade on two pieces of toast at once.”

“I always do that.”

“You’d done it before you remembered to make the toast.”

Ninja laughed, and then she cried, and Ada had the good sense to look out the window and complain about the gutters while she did it.

When Ninja could speak again, she told Ada a little about Elise. Not the things she had just learned. She told her about the way Elise would ask for directions and then offer the person directions back. She told her about the chair, which was apparently going to follow her for the rest of her life.

“I wish there was a way to be angry with somebody and still ask them to tell you the rest of a good story.”

Ada reached over and adjusted an uneven corner of the rug with her bare foot.

“You can ask for the story,” she said. “You can still be angry when it’s finished.”

It was nearly dark when Ninja went upstairs. She took off her shoes, sat on the bottom of the bed, and opened the photograph she had taken of the river herself.

At the far edge of the picture was the dark car. It was small and slightly blurred. The back window was down. She couldn’t see a face, but on the seat inside was something pale that looked like an open map.

She sent the picture to the officer with the reference number. Then she took her book downstairs and claimed the chair by the window again. Ada had left the lamp on.

On the low table beside the armchair, Ada had left a small pad with a picture of a robin at the top of each sheet. Ninja took the pad and a pencil. She wanted to write down the questions she hadn’t been able to ask in the kitchen, before they got tangled up with questions she thought of much later.

She started with the fire. The pencil made a little dent in the paper. Underneath that she wrote down the car and the time she’d been on the bridge. For a while she sat with the pencil poised and looked at what she’d written. Both questions were important. They were also questions somebody else might be able to answer.

There was one she wanted to ask him.

The last time Elise rang her, Ninja had been carrying a supermarket bag up the stairs. The lift was broken. A jar of something heavy kept banging into her knee, and she had answered the phone crossly, as if her sister might somehow have arranged the lift as a personal inconvenience.

Elise had asked if she’d be home on Sunday.

Ninja said she probably would. Elise told her to make it a definite yes, because she was going to bring something she wanted her to see. Then they spent half the call working out who had borrowed whose cake tin. If Ninja had known it was their last conversation, she would have sat on the stairs for it. She would have let the jar roll all the way down to the ground floor.

For years that Sunday had ended in her mind the same way. A cake tin in the cupboard. A patch of light on the kitchen floor. The sound of a neighbour letting themselves into another flat and for half a second it sounding like her own front door.

Ninja looked down at the robin on the paper.

She wrote, What was she bringing me?

Then she drew a small box around the question, tore the sheet from the pad, and put it in the pocket of her cardigan.

### Chapter Six — What Arrived Before the Rain

At the workshop, Hex found Tomas at the sink, warming two plates under the tap. A pot of soup sat on the workbench, on top of a newspaper folded into quarters.

“Did you tell her?” Tomas asked.

“Enough for today. She asked me to go.”

“So you went.”

“Yes.”

Tomas dried the plates. “Soup?”

Hex took one. It had a shallow chip along the edge. The soup was too hot to eat at first, so he sat with the plate in both hands and watched the steam.

“She asked me to let her know if I was leaving.”

“Are you?”

“I don’t think I could get very far before I started wishing I’d stayed.”

Tomas sat down across from him. The generator had cooled enough for the metal to tick every so often. Neither of them spoke for the first few spoonfuls.

Then the post slot rattled.

There were no late deliveries in Bellwether. In fact, there were scarcely any deliveries after lunch, mostly because the postman considered lunch an adequate end to the working day. Hex set his soup down. Tomas walked to the door with him.

A plain brown envelope lay on the mat.

“Did you see anybody?” Tomas asked.

Hex looked through the window. Across the road the florist had turned the lights off, but somebody had left one of the lamps in the window burning among the empty flower buckets. Rain made long bright lines on the pavement.

He took a picture of the envelope where it had landed. Then he picked it up. There was no stamp, and the name on the front was the one he had given up five years ago.

Hex.

Inside was a photograph of Elise, standing on a station platform. He had seen this photograph before. In his own copy her coat was buttoned up to the throat and she was looking past whoever held the camera, as though somebody had just called her name.

This copy had a wider frame.

Beyond Elise’s shoulder, standing at the far end of the platform, was a woman with a cream-coloured coat. Hex remembered her. He’d worked alongside her for four years. She’d been the first person he called after the fire. She hadn’t picked up.

Until that moment he’d believed she was in a different country on the day the photograph was taken.

On the back, in a handwriting he recognised immediately, were six words.

Ask me who opened the door.

He placed it flat on the bench.

“Another threat?” Tomas asked.

“I don’t know.” Hex looked at the writing again. “It might be someone asking for help.”

He reached for his phone. He had almost begun typing a message to Ninja when he remembered her in the kitchen, both hands around a tea towel, choosing how far she could go for one day.

He put the phone back down.

Across the village, Ninja read the same page of the horse-thief book three times. On the fourth try she managed to turn it. The woman on the page had gone out in the early morning to look for her horse. Ninja found that she wanted her to get it back, even if she had to find it without the man.

When Ada came in with another mug, Ninja moved her feet off the little table and made room.
