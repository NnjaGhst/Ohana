Previously on Elsewhere. Ninja and Hex fled Bellwether after a stranger arrived at their hotel room with Hex’s house keys. On the hill above town, Hex admitted that he’d used a photograph of Ninja to make Elise come to the warehouse where she died. Ninja took the photograph with her. The last thing they heard before going through the field gate was a car slowing down on the road.

From the top of the field, the footbridge had looked close enough to reach in a few minutes. Then the path sank between two walls, and for a long while the only thing Ninja could see ahead was the back of Hex’s coat. The grass was wet enough to shine where they put their feet. It had rained all over the valley, in places she couldn’t see and places she’d never been. All that water was coming together below them. They could hear it the whole way down.

Hex stopped where a branch lay across the path. He lifted one end and held it while she went underneath. She reached up to take the weight from him without thinking. There was a moment when both of them were holding the same branch, in exactly the way two people hold a door when they’re unsure who should go first. Ninja ducked through before she had to look at him.

There was nobody at the bridge. It was narrow, with a wall at each side just a little too low to give anyone much confidence. Water folded around the stones underneath. Ninja had grown up in a place where people talked about rivers rising and then went out to see if what they’d said was true. She put her palm against the wall and watched a branch come round the bend. It caught at the far bank for a moment and fell away again.

Hex stood behind her. There was enough room beside her for him to come up and see what she saw. He stayed where he was. When she turned he asked if she wanted him to cross first, and this time she could tell he’d wait for her to answer.

“I’ll go. If I stop, give me a second to work out why. I’ve spent the whole morning finding somebody’s hand on me before I knew what they wanted.”

While she crossed, he waited. It took nine steps to reach the place where she could see around the bend. Below the bridge, the river had got into a field and made a pond of the first few feet. A blackbird landed on the top wire of a fence there. It looked perfectly content to consider the rain someone else’s problem. Ninja looked at it a moment longer than she needed to. Then she crossed the rest of the way and turned to check on Hex.

He was moving more slowly than she had. About halfway across he glanced back up the slope. She had an impulse to call to him to look where he was putting his feet. She swallowed it. He reached her side, ran a hand over the rain on the wall, and said he’d thought he heard a door. She’d heard the same thing. Neither of them knew how far away it was.

Ninja’s thoughts
The minute I look away, I want to check he’s still there. That started at the bar. I liked it then. Now it could be habit, or fear, or the fact that some part of me still wants to know whether he’d have turned up at breakfast. I can put one foot in front of the other without settling it.

There was a signpost on the far bank. Someone had put a new white arrow on an old wooden post. It said there was a bus stop a quarter of a mile away. Hex was looking at the other arrow. That one pointed back toward Bellwether. Ninja thought she could read the name of the town without seeing the letters properly now. She took the path to the bus stop. At the first gate she stood on her side of it until Hex caught up.

She asked whether the road they were going to came out near his house. He said it did, if you followed it east. There was a little row of shops in the other direction, and someone had put a pavement in most of the way to it. He used to pass through on Sundays when he wanted a newspaper. He told her which direction that was. She was grateful he stopped there, which was a different feeling altogether from being grateful he knew.

From the top of the track they could see the bus shelter. The timetable had gone cloudy inside its plastic box. A person could sit there and watch an empty road in both directions, which was what Ninja wanted to do until she called home.

The shelter roof made the rain sound nearer. There was a bench with a mark on it where a wet bag had been left to stand. Ninja sat on the end farthest from the road and took her phone out. Her aunt was the first face she saw in her messages. There was a picture of a bowl of oranges and a question about whether she had eaten. The question had been sent recently enough that she could picture her aunt setting the phone down to make whatever she’d make if Ninja said no.

She called. When her aunt answered, Ninja opened her mouth to tell her about Elise. What came out first was that she’d lost her bag. There was a thump on the other end of the line. Her aunt had put something down and was listening now.

Ninja told her where she was, what had happened at the hotel, and that there was a man with her who had once worked for the people involved. It took her a while to get the last part out. Hex had gone to look at the timetable from the other side of the shelter, too far off to hear more than the sound of her voice. She could see the tips of his boots under the bench through a gap by the window.

Her aunt wanted to come. Ninja could hear her moving around the kitchen, probably looking for things she would take without having to think. Her aunt had always had a way of going from tea to shoes in the same minute.

“I’m going somewhere with people in it. I’ll tell you when I get there. Please stay home and keep your phone with you. I need to be able to call you twice today and know exactly where you’ll be. The other thing I need is Mara’s number, if you still have it. Elise used to go round there when she wanted to talk where none of us could interrupt.”

Her aunt did have the number. Mara had sent her a Christmas message, very late on Christmas evening, because she’d worked through to the end of the day and come home with enough flowers to fill a washing-up bowl. There was quite a lot more to that story. Ninja let her aunt tell it. It was a way of listening to her breathe. When the number came through, Ninja read it and waited until she heard her aunt put on the kettle before hanging up.

Hex was still looking at the timetable. He’d found a time written underneath the plastic in blue pen. Nobody had put a day beside it. He came close enough to show Ninja where it was. The time had passed, if it meant today. She could see the road reflected in the plastic behind the ink, without a vehicle on it.

“Is there somebody you want me to ring?”

He lifted his head. It was the first question she’d asked him all morning that would have been equally reasonable before the knock at the hotel. He said he’d already sent a message to someone who helped out at the garage, to say to take the day off. The man wouldn’t ask for a reason unless Hex gave him time to.

Hex’s thoughts
I can still see my keys in that man’s hand. I know how far he had to go inside the house to lift them. If I stand here much longer, I’ll start deciding what I’m going to say to him. She has a phone in her hand and a choice to make. She doesn’t need to see me practising being somebody she’s already met.

Ninja dialled Mara’s number. While it rang, she remembered how Mara sounded in the background of a call home from Elise, calling out a question about the oven. She’d seen her about twice a year while Elise was alive. Since then she’d seen her once, in the car park outside a supermarket. Mara had asked how her aunt was. Both of them had known the first thing either could say about Elise would make the other late for wherever she was going.

Mara answered by saying the name of her shop. Ninja said who it was. There was a small silence, and then Mara said her name again. She said it exactly the way someone says a name when they’ve kept a place for it in a room.

Ninja told her she’d heard something new about the night Elise died. She didn’t try to fit the whole story into the call. She told Mara about the photograph and asked if Elise had ever mentioned being afraid for her. Mara said she’d rather answer when she could see Ninja’s face. She wasn’t far. Her van was already loaded for a delivery she could put off. If Ninja could get as far as the laundrette, Mara could pick her up there.

Ninja looked at Hex. He was watching a stream of water run off the far corner of the roof. He could hear there was somebody on the phone. He’d been careful to give her the space to choose how much of him to mention.

“I’m with the man who told me. He was there, and he can say what he saw. I don’t know yet if I want him in the room. Will you give me the choice when we get there?”

Mara said it was her choice. There was a scrape like keys against a countertop. Then she told Ninja she’d be in a blue van, and that she’d pull right up to the door if it was still raining.

They walked down the side of the road. In one place the pavement gave way to little square bricks laid to cover a drain. A bit of water came up between them when Ninja stepped on it. At the next house a man in slippers was dragging a bin to the gate. He wished them a good morning with the deep conviction of someone who had the option of going indoors.

The laundrette was next to a corner shop and a closed hairdresser. The air inside smelled of soap, wet carpet and warmth. A row of dryers faced the window. One drum was turning with a single yellow towel inside. Each time the towel got to the top it hung there for an instant and fell on itself. Nobody in the room seemed to find this remarkable. Ninja sat near the door where she could see it happen.

Hex asked if she wanted coffee. When she said yes he put his phone on the bench between them and said he’d go next door. She would have been fine with him taking it. She understood what he meant anyway. He came back with two paper cups and a teaspoon he'd had to ask for. He had remembered how she took her coffee from a conversation neither of them had expected to matter.

She waited until he set his cup down. Then she asked what he could tell her about the names that were in Elise’s envelope. Hex said he never saw the sheet well enough to read it. He’d heard Carver speak a surname as he opened it, but only the first syllable had stayed with him. He had turned it over in his head many times since. He could give her six possible names, and every single one would feel like a fact once he’d said it out loud. She told him she preferred to hear the part he was sure of.

“What I’m sure of,” he said, “is that Carver could tell what he had by looking at one page. He didn’t check the envelope for anything else. There was somebody he expected to find on it.”

Ninja put both hands around the coffee. Her fingertips were starting to hurt as they warmed up. She thought of the names people learned without meaning to, the person who used the corner shop at always the same time, the one who walked a dog in any weather, the family whose Christmas lights went on early enough to see them walking home from school. They could go missing and leave a mark in the day that nobody knew how to describe.

Elise would have been able to describe it. It was one of the things that took a person a while to notice about her. When someone stopped coming around, she'd ask where they were. That was sometimes all it took to make the rest of them realise how long it had been since they’d seen them.

Ninja asked whether Carver ever said why her sister had those names. Hex said he’d been told how to get the envelope back. He had kept to what he’d been told for years before that night. Part of the work, he said, was learning when someone wanted a person who would take a piece of information from one room to another and forget everything he’d seen in either room.

“I can hear when you switch into the voice you used at the market. You’ve just done it on the words “part of the work.” I need the answer from the man who was there. If there’s a piece you’re ashamed to say, let me hear you have trouble with it.”

He looked down at his cup. The teaspoon was still balanced across the lid. He picked it up and laid it flat on the bench. Then he said Carver had made the job sound small enough for him to do without asking how it could end. He’d been relieved to be given something simple. That was what he was ashamed to tell her.

She let him finish his coffee. The towel was still going round in the dryer. A woman with two bags of clothes came in, tipped both onto the folding table, and went back out to make a call. There was a clean cotton shirt in the pile. One of the sleeves hung almost to the floor. Ninja tucked it up before the fabric touched the tiles. She did it the way she’d have done it anywhere, without asking herself whether a woman who had just heard what she’d heard could still care if someone’s shirt got dirty.

Hex watched her come back to the bench. He wanted to tell her that he remembered Elise saying please once in the warehouse. It had sounded tired. He’d been thinking about the word since he saw Ninja at the bar, and he knew if he offered it now it would feel like another piece of her sister he’d kept in his pocket for five years. He waited until she asked what else he could remember.

He told her about the please. He’d said he’d stay. Elise had thanked him, quite plainly. That was the last thing she said to him while they could still hear each other. Ninja put down the coffee and went to the window. He could see the effort it took her to get there without wiping her eyes.

The longest part of waiting for a person is the time you’re ready to leave and there’s nothing new you can do. Ninja had never thought this in exactly those words before. She thought it now with her coat still buttoned and her coffee gone cold. Every time a vehicle slowed there was a little lift behind her ribs. Then it went past. Once it was a lorry going so slowly she saw the driver take a bite of an apple. The sight of somebody with an apple seemed absurdly unlikely. She wanted to laugh and managed a breath that was close enough.

Hex had moved to the far end of the bench. His sleeve was darker where he’d leaned against the wall in the field. He got a message and read it. Ninja could tell he’d remembered the phone was on the bench and considered leaving it there. When he put it back he said his friend at the garage had replied. He was all right and had gone out to get something to eat. Hex looked more tired after he told her. Until then he’d had an ordinary worry he could do something about.

Ninja asked what he thought they wanted now. He didn’t answer until a dryer came to the end of its cycle. A low chime went through the room. A man stretched and got up from the other bench, went over to a drum, and turned it off. Hex watched him wait for the latch.

“I don’t know why they came this morning,” he said. “I know they wanted me to understand they could come whenever they liked. I’ve spent years pretending I’d know what to do if that happened. I mostly pictured being by myself.”

She asked how long he’d had a life here. He told her what he could fit into a few sentences. There had been a rented room at first and a long stretch of work he could take without giving much of himself away. He’d found it hard to sleep in the first room because the refrigerator clicked unpredictably. After he carried it into the hall in the middle of the night, he found it hard to sleep because somebody could ask him in the morning what he thought he was doing. He said he had bought the house partly because it was quiet and partly because there was room at the back to take things apart.

She could picture him taking a refrigerator into the hall. It would be completely dark, and he’d be holding the cord so that it didn’t hit the stairs and wake everybody. She could also picture the man at the landing holding the keys beside her bed. The two images could both stay in her head for longer now. One didn’t wipe the other out when she blinked.

Hex told her he’d started leaving a spare key inside the door because it saved a trip to the front. He could remember deciding where to put the hook. He could remember standing there with a drill in his hand, moving it a few inches to see if the keys would brush the wall when he opened the door. He felt stupid to be thinking about the hook now, and he said that too. The first time he said something he could have made sound impressive, and hadn’t, she heard it.

Ninja’s thoughts
I could say I’m sorry they went into his house. I am. I could put my hand down between us and see what he does. I’d still wake up tomorrow knowing what he did to Elise. I think I finally understand why holding his hand feels like it’s going to cost me something, even though it shouldn’t.

The man with the laundry passed them on his way out. He held the door for a woman coming in with a child, and the child thanked him much louder than she needed to. Ninja looked down at the rings of dried rain on the toes of her boots. She said she couldn’t give Hex a clean answer about whether she wanted to see him again after today. When she looked up he was holding his empty cup with both hands. She saw him stop himself from saying something and wait instead.

“I don’t need you to give me one today,” he said.

Ninja thought that was probably true. She could tell how much he wanted one. It was possible to tell the truth while wanting a different answer. She supposed that was the first thing she’d understood about him that day that didn’t make either of them feel worse.

Outside, a man from the corner shop went round the window with a rag. He made a clear circle, wiped it a little wider, and went inside to see how it looked from there. A finer rain had begun. Ninja could see it in the foot or so just below the top of the glass. When a blue van crossed the end of the street, she sat up, watched it pass, and then saw its brake lights come on.

It was a different blue van. It stopped at the corner and a man climbed out with a folded newspaper under his arm. He reached back through the open door to switch the lights off. Ninja sat down again. The little movement it had taken to stand had sent a sharp feeling through her knee. She rubbed it with her palm. There would be a bruise there later, and she might have no idea which part of the morning she’d got it in.

Hex asked if she’d like him to see whether Mara had sent a message. Then he realised he was looking at the phone in Ninja’s hand and said she could check herself. She did. There was a message, but it was from her aunt. It said the kettle was on and she had decided to leave it at that. Ninja could almost see her making the decision. The kettle really would be on, even if nobody was coming in.

She typed that she was with people and would ring again. She thought about sending the picture as well. It was one thing she could show her aunt that would take less than a minute to explain. Then she imagined her aunt looking at it alone in the kitchen where the kettle was on. She put the photograph back in her pocket.

There had been a long time during the years after the warehouse when she hated having one story in her head and a perfectly cheerful message on her phone. She would get off a bus and tell her aunt she’d got home safe when she was still two streets away. It meant a person could finish making tea without listening for her key. It was the sort of thing that felt kind until someone did it to you.

She told Hex about that, because she was waiting and he was there, and because she wanted him to understand what she meant when she asked him to tell her things he didn’t have to tell her yet. He said there were things he could have said when he bought her the drink, and things he could have said when they crossed the street to the hotel. He didn’t try to think of a cleaner order to put those chances in.

A dryer clicked. The woman who owned the shirt opened it, took out a basket of things and rested it on the bench while she found her keys. Ninja thanked her when she moved the basket without being asked. Through the glass, the man with the newspaper was still standing at the corner. She watched him turn a page nobody else could see. Then the man folded the paper, tucked it under his sleeve and went inside. Another blue van turned down the street. This one had a bunch of painted flowers on the door.

A blue van pulled in and stopped right outside. The hazard lights came on. An ordinary woman in a dark jumper pushed her window down and looked at the door. She wore her hair shorter than Ninja remembered. A few strands stuck to her forehead from loading something in the rain. On the dashboard there was a faded photograph of a dog with its nose on the back of a car seat. Ninja knew at once it was Mara.

She said Hex could come as far as Mara’s shop. She wanted him to wait downstairs until she said she was ready for him. She thought she could find out what Mara remembered, and that talking to her about Elise would be easier if she knew Hex was far enough away that she didn’t have to watch his face.

He asked if she wanted him to find his own way there. She looked at the van. There were three seats across the front, and Mara had moved the bag off the middle one without being told. Ninja shook her head. She wanted to see how he sat beside her now that neither of them had any need to pretend it was last night.

When they came out, Mara got down out of the van. She put one arm around Ninja, waited, and let her make the rest of the distance up herself. The hug was at a funny angle because of the open door. It was all right. Ninja could feel the edge of a pocket in Mara’s jumper and the keys in it. They were very ordinary keys. It helped to hold steady for a moment and know exactly whose door they belonged to.

Hex’s thoughts
There’s somebody she can lean against. I’m glad. I want it to be me. I’m going to have to learn to leave both of those things where they are.

Mara took the road away from Bellwether. She told Ninja the bakery by the roundabout had closed since Elise was alive and the light in her shop had to stay on overnight because half the people in town looked for it when they got off the bus. She took a turn that let Ninja see the river once more. Then the roofs slipped between them and the water was gone.

Ninja watched the shopfronts go by. Once, when the van turned, the sleeve of Hex’s coat brushed her wrist. He moved his arm immediately. She wished he hadn’t and appreciated that he had. She decided she could live with the two feelings until they arrived.
