# Elsewhere: The Hidden Valley
## Episode 5 — The Road Under the Rain

Previously in Elsewhere. At the Crooked Lantern, a mechanic named Elias bought Ninja a whiskey. By breakfast she knew that his real name was Hex, that he had worked with her sister Elise, and that he still had a photograph from the night Elise died. Daniel brought Ninja a letter that Elise had left for her. When a man stopped outside the pub to look in, the three of them set off on foot for the hidden valley.

The road out of Bellwether curved twice before it made up its mind to go uphill. Ninja had walked the first turn on her own the previous evening, when she was still a visitor with a room key and a book that was taking its time getting interesting. She hadn't noticed how soon the pavement ended. This morning she had to pick between wet grass and the road, and after ten steps of trying to keep her boots clean she gave up and walked on the grass. Hex came up beside her with the paper bag from the bakery held under his coat. Daniel followed, a few paces back. He had taken off his cap, although it was beginning to rain, and was squeezing the brim between both hands.

At the last house there was a broken bird bath with a little wooden gate behind it. A path rose beyond the gate. Hex touched the latch and looked at Ninja before he opened it. She had spent the morning being watched for signs that she might change her mind. This was the first time she could see the difference between somebody waiting to see what she'd do and somebody waiting for her answer.

“How far is it once we're over the hill?”

He told her about two miles if they cut across the old orchard. The bridge after the orchard sometimes went under in a really bad rain. If it had, there was a higher way, passing a house with a copper weather vane. Daniel said the copper weather vane was a fish. Hex had always thought it was a bird. Neither appeared to want to settle it by looking when they got there.

Daniel stopped to do his lace up at the gate. Ninja waited with her hand against the damp wood. Beyond him through the last gap between houses she could see the edge of the market. The bakery window was a yellow rectangle, and for a few seconds she tried to pick out the rabbit from this far off. It was impossible. She knew exactly where it was anyway.

The path climbed past the backs of two gardens and a field with a blue barrel tipped against the hedge. Water ran out of the barrel's open end and took a perfectly direct line for the ditch. After the field the three of them went in single file. There was room to walk two abreast, but there were patches where a row of grass had grown up the middle and she wanted to keep both boots on the firmer side. Hex let the distance open between them. When he saw her testing a flat stone he stopped behind her instead of reaching past to show her where to put her foot. She was grateful for that. It let her be annoyed at the things she actually wanted to be annoyed at.

Higher up the wind turned and she felt the first real rain on her cheek. She pulled on her hood. All morning she had been able to picture the room at the hotel waiting for her exactly as she had left it. The glass on the table, an arm's length of curtain, the vacant space where her bag had been. When the rooftops disappeared behind the hedge, her mind stopped putting her back in that room every few minutes. It was still there if she wanted to think about it. She found she was starting to think about where they were going.

At the top of a short rise the path came to a fork marked by a gatepost with no gate. Daniel pointed left. They could get out of the rain in the old pump house, he explained, and then look at the sky before making up their minds. A fold in the hill protected the building from a wind in one direction. From the other direction the rain went straight in the door. He had no idea which direction today's wind was coming from, because it had been ten years since he had bothered to learn the names of directions he couldn't see from his kitchen.

Ninja turned her face into it and closed one eye. For a second everything took a little more effort than she wanted it to. She knew there was nobody back at the hotel whose bad news could get worse if she took her time up here. Elise had been dead five years before any of them started walking up the hill. It made a difference to remember that she could take an hour over the next decision and it would be the same morning when she made it.

She asked Daniel to go ahead and see if the pump house was open. He put his cap back on and went. Hex waited by the gatepost while she adjusted the strap on her bag. She had been carrying it too high, so the buckle fell right against the first bone in her shoulder. He watched her pull it loose. There were dry gloves in the paper bag, he told her. Len had put a spare pair in at the last moment.

She held her hands out for them. The right one had a small lump of thread at the base of the thumb where somebody had stitched it. She turned it over to get the thumb in and let him take her wet pair. With her hands warmer she felt a little less like saying whatever would get her to the end of the conversation fastest. She told him she still wanted to know who took the photograph. He told her he didn't know, and that he could tell her a few things about the people who might. She told him to save the few things until they were under a roof. He nodded. They started down after Daniel together.

* * *

The pump house had one window and very little pump left in it. A rectangular mark on the floor showed where something heavy had stood for a long time. The rest of the floor was covered with dry leaves that had come in through a missing pane. Daniel had booted them into a corner and found a place to put his cap where only a very particular kind of drip could get at it.

On the sill stood a blue enamel mug. Ninja picked it up to look at a white chip in the rim. Daniel said there had once been two mugs up here, with the handles pointing in opposite directions so you could pick either one up on the way past. Elise had brought them when she found that the place was used by walkers. Neither mug had belonged to her before, Daniel explained. She had found them in the box the pub kept for things people left behind, asked Ada how long they had been in it, and decided a mug ought to spend its life somewhere people were pleased to see it.

Daniel rubbed a patch of condensation off the window with the side of his hand. Ninja put the mug back so it would still be there if a walker came up behind them. She pulled the letter out of her bag and looked at the date, because she wanted to be sure it was still the same thing she had read at the hotel. The way the page had been folded meant she could see the underline and the word Sunday on the same side without opening it.

She sat on the deep stone step inside the door. Outside the rain was moving across the grass in visible lines. From where she was sitting she could see it reach the footpath and take the colour out of it. Hex took out the paper bag and asked if she wanted the sandwich now or afterwards. She wanted it now. He gave Daniel a third and handed her the paper with the rest of it in. There were three gloves in the bag if you didn't count the wet ones. Len had accidentally given him a pair and a spare thumb to keep it company.

They ate before anybody spoke. Then Hex told her about the back office where Carver had handed him a folder. There had been pick-up instructions in it, a time, an address and the name of the person he was supposed to speak to. The photograph hadn't been attached in any usual way. Somebody had slipped it loose into the back. He hadn't understood what it was for. He had kept it in part because the look on the woman's face had stayed with him, and in part because if she mattered to an assignment he thought she ought to know. By the time he met the woman it had become much easier to ask whether she'd like a drink.

The rain found a new hole in the roof and began to fall into a shallow depression in the concrete. Each drop made two sounds: one when it hit and another when the water settled against the side. Ninja listened for a while. She found she could think best if nobody tried to tell her whether she was making progress.

“Were they watching me, or was my picture there to frighten her?”

Hex told her he thought the picture had been there for Elise. He didn't know if the men who took it knew the name of her neighbour or the way she went to work. He didn't know which part of being watched was supposed to reach her. The instructions were to hand over an envelope, and somebody might have thought a sister was a good reason to make sure she did. He paused after that. Ninja looked at him. He had a little grit on the side of his boot. The man who had stood at the hotel that morning and told her he had been at the club was the same man holding his hands on his knees now. She wanted to be able to keep both pictures in mind without any help from him.

Daniel got up and put his cap between his knees. He said there was a woman in the valley called Ruth, who had handled post there before the old counter was taken out of use. Elise had known her longer than either of them. If Ninja wanted to hear more about the photograph, Ruth might have a better idea where Elise had been the day before the job. Daniel had come to find her himself, not knowing what he'd be asked and hoping she'd let him ask his own question first.

Ninja asked what his question was. He had kept the copy of the figures because Elise left it with him. For five years he had known what to do with the fact that she had left it. He could make sure it was somewhere unremarkable and check it was still there from time to time. The thing he'd come to ask Ruth was what Elise had wanted to happen after somebody saw it. He had never found a safe way of finding that out. He said it quickly and then took another bite, as though he hadn't intended to be the person who put a question in the room.

Ninja felt a sudden generous impulse toward him. A man who had brought her a bicycle had walked up a wet hill and waited all morning to say he didn't know what to do. She could work with that, perhaps because there was so much in her own head that began with the same sentence. She took the third dry glove out of the paper bag and slid it across the sill toward him. He had one hand warm from the sandwich. He put the glove on his other hand.

They waited a few more minutes. The rain eased enough that nobody had to look at the sky and decide whether it was going to stop. Daniel took his cap down. Hex flattened the bag and put it back in his coat pocket. Ninja returned Elise's letter to her own. There was nothing she had to leave in the pump house but the marks her wet boots had made on the step.

* * *

In the rain, the valley was hard to see until they were in it. They came down through a piece of woodland that narrowed to three bare trees, and beyond the trees a low stone wall dropped away into open ground. On the far side of the slope a whole orchard stood in grey light. The nearest branches were dark with rain. Behind them Ninja could see the roof of a cottage and three yellow squares that she took for windows. She had imagined a place hidden by great cliffs, as though geography would want to keep as much of a secret as the people. The place itself looked like somewhere a person could grow apples and get cross with a gate.

The path ran toward a river and crossed on a stone footbridge that was narrower in the middle than at either end. Daniel went down ahead to see if it was still clear. He came back and held his hands apart to show there was space between the water and the underside. Hex looked at the sky behind them, then down at the stream. He suggested taking the footbridge while they could. Ninja looked for herself and saw a raised shelf of stone on the far side that would give her somewhere to put her foot. She nodded. They went down together.

From the bridge it was possible to see the water pushing itself into the base of the orchard bank. There was a small hollow where dry earth had been that morning, and a loose root trembled with every wave. Ninja put the palm of her hand on the parapet. It was wet and rough and colder than it looked. In the middle the sound of the water was large enough that it took up all the room she normally used for remembering things. She crossed the last part looking at the stones on the far side, and the noise of the river grew smaller behind her as suddenly as it had grown.

Hex came across last. She heard the scrape of his boot and saw that he'd put the paper bag into his coat to use both hands. If anybody had asked her whether she wanted him to fall in, she could have made a reasonable joke out of it by now. The thought of it happening still made her watch his feet. He got to the other side and put his palm on the wall beside hers without touching it. Then they both took their hands away.

Daniel knew an easier route to the cottage through the orchard. The nearest tree had a narrow strip of blue cloth around a branch. There was another one four trees farther up. He went to look at it and called back that the path still existed. It didn't seem important to either of them to see what he meant by existing until they got to a patch where the grass reached the knees. There the path became three higher pieces of ground with standing water between them. Ninja got over the puddles by looking ahead and taking the next step before she had time to regret the last one. By the time they reached the lane she'd got one damp sock and still felt quite pleased with herself.

They stopped beside a low wall to get their breath. Hex pointed out a longer building behind the cottage. He told her that's where the old post counter had been. People didn't go through the main house if they had a letter to send. They walked round the back and rang a bell by the little door. Ruth would have been able to judge how much postage they needed by the way they carried the parcel under one arm. If she thought she could get your change out in time, she'd keep a spare kettle going and give you a cup of tea while you waited.

Ninja was surprised that he knew this. He told her he had done some work on Ruth's van a few years ago and had ended up sitting in the passenger seat while she drove him round to return a book. It had taken forty minutes and they passed the house twice. She had wanted to show him the stand of trees where there used to be beehives, and then to show him the same thing from the opposite direction. If he wanted to know about his own village he generally had to ask somebody from three miles away.

“Did she ever ask about Elise?”

He said Ruth hadn't said Elise's name to him until he told her who he was. That had been a winter morning when he helped her get two bags of salt out of the van. He couldn't tell how much she knew about the club, and he didn't think she was trying to find out all of it at once. She asked whether he'd ever posted a letter in that valley. He said he hadn't. She said that if he ever did, he should remember to put the name of somebody who could receive an answer on the back. He had thought she was talking about how the post worked. Five minutes later he was no longer sure.

Ninja put her bag down on the wall. There was only one more thing she wanted to ask him while there were still no windows close enough for anybody to overhear. Had he known Ruth would recognise her? He shook his head. He thought Ruth might know her name. He didn't know if Elise had ever shown her a picture. Ninja let that sit with the earlier answer. He hadn't tried to make whether somebody knew her name into a very small question and she was relieved about that.

The lane turned toward a gate with a latch you could lift with one finger. Daniel went no farther. He waited while Ninja got the bag back in the right place on her shoulder, then held it open for her. She went through first. There were yellow leaves sticking to the path in front of the cottage, and when she looked back she could see exactly which ones Hex and Daniel had trodden on. The thought crossed her mind that she could get the whole way back to the stone bridge without asking either of them which direction to take.

* * *

The front door opened just before Ninja touched it. A woman in a grey cardigan stood inside with her sleeves pulled up to the elbows. There were flour marks in two places on the cardigan, and Ninja knew exactly how it had happened because she had worn an old sweatshirt through the same argument with a bowl of dough. Ruth asked if they all wanted somewhere to put their coats. As her eyes came to rest on Ninja she stopped holding the door quite so tight. Other than that she let her face do what it was already doing.

The hallway had a short bench with woven rushes on its seat. They lined their boots up underneath it and, at Ruth's suggestion, put their wet socks on a tray that had been intended for cutlery. Daniel turned his cap over to see where else it might be wet. Hex stood in stocking feet near the inside of the door, waiting to see whether it would be useful to go into the kitchen or stay where he was. Ninja could see that Ruth had noticed him doing it. She asked him to bring in another chair without saying what room it was in. He found it on his first try.

There was a little square room at the back of the house with a pane of ribbed glass in the door. A person on one side could see that somebody was on the other, but not what they were doing with their hands. Ruth put the light on and showed Ninja the old counter. The square gap where there had been a brass service bell was now being used to keep pens that were still worth trying. On the customer side somebody had turned the bench round so it faced a low window. Beyond the window she could see the orchard they'd walked through. The drops on the glass were big enough to turn every branch upside down for an instant.

Ruth explained that Elise first came out here to help her with a van load of undelivered post after a storm. The relief driver forgot one of the roads came to an end in wet weather. By the time somebody got hold of Ruth three mail sacks had been sitting in a warm garage for two days. Elise came on the Saturday and took the jobs she could manage on a bicycle. She didn't ask how much there would be to do on the Sunday. She came back with a ring of someone's keys that had fallen out of a damaged envelope, put them down, and asked whether there was anyone else they'd missed.

Ninja pictured her sister cycling through the orchard, then remembered the road they'd taken in would have been a poor place to ride even in very good weather. She asked which route Elise had used. Ruth pointed out the window to a piece of higher ground. There was a bend a person could find by an old ash tree, and once you got the hang of it you could ride most of it without having to put your feet down. Ninja would like to see that bend when it was light enough. She would like to see if she had been right about which jacket Elise would have put on to go and do something ridiculous with a bicycle on a Sunday morning.

Ruth asked her if she took sugar. Ninja said she usually didn't. Sometimes when she'd been out for a long time she took one spoonful in the first bit of tea and tipped the rest of the pot over the same leaves to see what was left in them. Elise thought this was one of the things people did because they remembered somebody older doing it. She'd asked three people in their family about it once and found all of them drank tea in completely ordinary ways. Ninja had no idea where the habit came from. She was pleased on the way back from telling Ruth that she'd been able to put it in a new person's head.

There was a wooden frame propped at the end of the counter. Ruth turned it around. It held a map of the old delivery routes drawn on a piece of school graph paper. People had put their houses on it themselves when they came in for stamps. One man entered his place twice, because there was a little house his daughter used when she came home, and if either of them got something with a handwritten address it was good to know who might have been waiting for it.

The two places were joined with a short line in pencil. Ninja read them carefully. Neither of the names belonged to anybody she knew, and for a few moments she could look at the map as just a map. How far it was to the next farm. Where the road gave out. A corner somebody had drawn too narrow to fit a word. She thought the place was smaller than she'd expected. Then she looked at how many ordinary lives had room to fit on the other side of that window. She understood why her sister had wanted to come back even after the post was delivered.

They stood there quietly. Hex set the chair down in the other room. The sound travelled through the ribbed door as a single soft scrape. Ruth picked up one of the pens as if to try it on the back of her hand, thought better of it, and returned it to the gap in the counter.

“Why did she come back here before she died?”

Ruth said that was something she could tell Ninja once they'd had a chance to get warm. There was something she could show her first, if she'd like. She bent to open the narrow drawer beside the counter. It was stiff at one end. She pressed with the heel of her hand until it gave a little. Inside lay two envelopes with stamps from the years when she was still sending post. Under them was a third, a little darker in colour, addressed in Elise's hand.

The envelope at the hotel had Ninja's name on it. This one was addressed to Ruth. Next to the stamp Elise had drawn a small square in blue pen and put a dot in the centre. Ruth tapped the dot, and said this was how they marked post they would deliver themselves if it was important enough. No need for it to go the long way round and come back in a van. She'd waited for Elise to come round to tell her whether she could open it. When she didn't come, Ruth had opened it. She knew that if they spoke through the rest of the afternoon she'd have to tell Ninja why she hadn't brought it to her then.

Ninja looked down at the handwriting. Elise had never needed to practise the capital letter R. It was the one letter she drew with great confidence whenever someone came to see if she could write in icing on a cake. Ninja remembered asking her to write Roberto once when the cake was very small. Elise had written Rob and then turned the end of the b into a wave.

Ninja asked if she could put the envelope on the table herself. She took it carefully by the two sides, went through the ribbed door and laid it down where there was a dry patch between the mugs. Daniel came in after her and sat very still. Hex took the seat with the wobbly leg, although there were two others. Ruth untied her apron. By then, in the warm room, each of them could hear the rain again.

In the next episode, Ruth opens the blue page on the kitchen table. One of the names is still used by somebody who has a key to the valley's old post office.
