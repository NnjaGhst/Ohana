Previously on Elsewhere. Elias bought Ninja a whiskey at the Crooked Lantern. By closing time neither of them wanted to say good night. They shared room six at the hotel. Just after dawn a man came up the stairs.

Someone pounded on their door.

Ninja had one hand under the pillow and the other reaching across the bed. Her fingers found the sheet where Elias had been. She opened her eyes in time to see him pick up his trousers. The second knock came as he was pulling them on. He picked the key up off the carpet.

The man on the landing spoke in a language Ninja didn't understand. She thought at first he'd got the wrong room. He used the same tone she used at work when somebody asked whether he needed to be there at a particular time. Casual. Expecting a yes.

Elias answered in the same language. She'd heard him hesitate over asking if she wanted breakfast with him. There was no hesitation now.

She sat up and found the stranger could see her when she moved. He looked her over, then reached into a pocket. Something brief and bright caught the light. There was a red tag on the house key, and copper wire looped around the ring. Elias had shown her the other set at the bar. He'd told her the set with the copper wire hung inside his back door.

The man held the keys out. Elias didn't take them. He stood so still the stranger had to move his own hand past the door to get a better look at her.

Then he said her name. Ninja heard it in the middle of the sentence. It was the only part of the sentence she could repeat, and suddenly she wanted to forget the sound of it.

Elias opened the door a little farther. He spoke again, low and even. The man took his hand off the frame and folded it around the keys. Ninja had met a version of Elias who asked a hard question and made a face while he waited for the answer. This version stood looking straight at the man with his arms at his sides and gave him as much time as he needed to move.

Finally the stranger stepped back. His footsteps went down the stairs. Ninja waited for the thump of the front door. She knew what that sounded like because it had shut behind them in the night while she was trying to get their wet shoes off the mat.

They heard nothing.

“Get your coat,” Elias said. “We're leaving.”

“Who was that?”

“Put your shoes on first.”

“No.”

She got out of bed. She wanted both feet on the carpet when she spoke. She was scared she might sound as though she was trying to be difficult. For a second that seemed like something she ought to worry about. Then he reached for her bag without asking her, and she set her hand on it first.

Elias turned to look at her properly. He still had the crease down one cheek from the pillow. She'd seen it a minute before the knock and thought she'd kiss him there when he woke up.

“Last night you asked me to stay. Now you’re telling me to run. Give me a reason.”

His eyes went to her hand. He let the bag go. She was ready to wait there until he told her what the man had said. She thought he could see she meant it.

A chair scraped across the tiles below. Somebody spoke in a low voice, then fell quiet. Elias reached for his coat and took a photograph out of the inside pocket. He held it out by one corner, where it had gone white from being handled.

It was Ninja, five years younger, outside a corner shop in the rain. Her shoulder was up to her ear, and she was laughing at someone just out of frame. Elise. She could remember getting wet that day. She could remember the sleeve of her coat after Elise bumped her. The person who took the picture had been across the road.

“Where did you get this?”

“I know about Elise’s death.”

She looked up because for a second the words didn't sound as though they could have come from him. Then she thought of him at the bar seeing her for the first time. He'd recognised her. She knew it now as surely as she'd known she'd wanted to sit beside him, and it did something terrible to the memory.

She turned the photograph over. There was nothing on the back that told her what to do. She put it in her own pocket. Then she took her coat off the hook, put on her shoes, and caught her bag up by the strap.

“I'm coming with you. The first place we can stop, you're going to tell me what you know.”

He nodded. He held out his hand the same way he had outside the pub.

This time she walked past him on her own.

The stairwell door clicked shut behind them. Concrete steps spiralled down into a dim well of grey light. Elias moved first, one hand on the rail, his footsteps quick and even. Ninja followed, her bag clutched against her ribs, her pulse loud in her ears.

“You don’t get to say that and then walk.”

Her voice low.

“I’ll tell you when we can stop.”

“Explain it to me, then.”

He stopped before the next set of steps and held up a hand.

Below them, two floors down, a figure stepped out onto the landing. A man in a dark coat, his head tilted up toward the sound of their footsteps. He didn’t shout. He just started climbing, unhurried, like he had all the time in the world.

Elias stopped. His hand found Ninja’s arm and pulled her back against the wall.

“Up,” he said.

“We came from up.”

“Now we go up again.”

They turned and climbed. The man below kept coming. His footsteps were steady, patient. Ninja glanced over the rail and saw his face for half a second, pale and blank, before Elias yanked her forward.

At the top landing, a fire door led to a service corridor. Elias pushed through it and they emerged into a narrow hallway that smelled of bleach and old carpet. At the far end, a window looked out onto the street below. Elias crossed to it and looked down.

“There’s another one,” he said. “By the staff entrance.”

Ninja came up beside him. A man in a grey jacket stood at the corner of the alley below, his hands in his pockets, his gaze fixed on the hotel’s side door. He wasn’t moving. He was waiting.

“They’re covering the exits.”

“They’re covering the obvious exits.”

Elias turned and walked back the way they’d come, past the fire door, past a linen closet, to a small door marked STAFF ONLY. He tried the handle. It opened.

“Laundry chute?”

“Service stairs. The kitchen gets its deliveries from the market.”

“And if they’re covering that too?”

“Then we improvise.”

The service stairs were narrower than the main stairwell, the walls close and warm. They went down fast, Elias counting floors under his breath. At the bottom, a door opened onto a short corridor that ended in a swinging double door. Beyond it, the clatter of pots and the hiss of steam.

Elias pushed through first. The kitchen was busy, cooks in white coats shouting over the roar of extractor fans. A man near the delivery door looked up as they passed, his eyes narrowing. Elias didn’t slow. He walked straight to the door, shouldered it open, and pulled Ninja through into the alley beyond.

The alley was narrow, choked with crates and bins. At the far end, Bellwether market opened up, a sprawl of stalls and awnings and crowds. Elias turned left, away from the grey-jacketed man at the corner, and broke into a run.

At the market entrance he caught the edge of a stall and stopped. Ninja almost ran into him. She followed his gaze to a man finishing a cup of coffee by a florist's van. He put the cup down on the bonnet and came toward them without looking away.

“How many of them are there?”

“I've seen three.”

“Is that supposed to make me feel better?”

“No. I want you to tell me if you see a fourth.”

She had seen him be quick at the hotel. Now she saw the difference between quick and afraid. He stood there looking at the man by the van as though he meant to walk over and ask him something. Then he turned to her and said, very quietly, “See the yellow awning? I'll meet you there.”

“We're staying together.”

He looked at her. A woman in a blue apron passed between them carrying flowers wrapped in newspaper. The man by the van stopped to let her through. When Ninja looked back, Elias was already reaching for the edge of the yellow canvas to hold it up for her. She went through first.

Ninja ran. Her bag bounced against her hip. The alley mouth opened onto a wide lane lined with fruit stalls, the air thick with the smell of oranges and diesel. People pressed in from all sides. Elias wove through them, his hand still locked around her wrist, pulling her along.

Behind them, a shout. She didn’t turn to look.

They cut between two stalls, a fishmonger and a flower seller, and emerged into a cross-lane packed with shoppers. Elias slowed to a fast walk, his grip loosening but not letting go. Ninja pulled her wrist free.

“Don’t.”

“Fine. Keep close.”

They pushed through the crowd. A stallholder ahead of them, an older woman with a tray of apples, stumbled as someone barged past her. The tray tipped. Apples scattered across the cobbles. The woman went down on one knee, her hand catching the edge of a crate.

Ninja stopped. She crouched, grabbed the woman’s arm, and helped her up.

“You all right?”

“Bloody runners,” the woman said. “Always in a rush.”

Elias was already turning back, his face tight. “Ninja.”

She pressed one of the apples into the woman's hand. She'd have stayed to help with the rest, but she saw the man from the hotel coming down the lane they'd just crossed. He took the woman's shoulder and moved her out of his way. Ninja got up so fast she felt it in her knee.

“We have to go,” he said.

“I heard you the first time.”

This time she was the one who caught Elias's sleeve. She took him through a gap between two tables, straight into a covered lane that smelled of hot bread. Someone was reversing a white van into it. Elias looked up when the reversing alarm started. The van was about to block the whole lane. If they crossed behind it now, the man would lose sight of them before it stopped.

Ninja got there first. A hand closed on the strap of her bag and she felt it go tight across her chest. She slipped the strap off her shoulder. The man stumbled backward with the bag. Elias caught her hand and pulled her round the corner before he could get his balance. The last thing she saw in the lane was her bag on the ground and a stranger going through it in broad daylight.

“My train ticket was in there.”

“I'll get you another one.”

“I wasn't asking you to. I needed to say something normal.”

They reached the narrow lane behind the Crooked Lantern. She recognised the pub by a peeling sign for deliveries. Elias stopped at the side door. He had both hands free and was watching the end of the lane. She realised she'd been holding on to his sleeve since the corner. Neither of them said anything when she let go.

He knocked. Three quick raps, then two slow.

The door opened. Ada stood there, her hair tied back, her sleeves rolled to the elbow. She looked at Elias, then at Ninja, and stepped aside.

“Get in,” she said.

They stepped through into a dim corridor that smelled of beer and old wood. Ada closed the door behind them and slid the bolt.

Ada brought them into the back room of the Crooked Lantern. It was bright enough now to see how small it was. She switched the kettle on and put two mugs on the table. Ninja took the chair facing the door. Elias stayed standing.

“Sit down, Elias.”

He pulled the other chair out. Before he sat, he said, “When I worked for Carver, they called me Hex.”

She waited for the rest. He sat. For a moment she was back at the bar watching him pull a chair out for her with his boot. She could still feel herself smiling at that. It made her angry enough to put the photograph down between them hard.

“You knew exactly who I was when you sent me that drink.”

“Yes.”

“How long have you had this?”

“Five years. Carver handed it to me the night Elise died.”

“Why did he have my picture?”

“Because you were her sister. He said if she refused to bring what he wanted, I should show it to her. She'd understand he knew where to find you.”

Ninja took her hands off the table. She could see a corner of the shop sign in the photograph. As a kid she'd been sent there for milk. There'd been a time when Elise could call her from halfway down that street and find her without having to look at a phone. Carver had sent somebody there to wait for her, and Ninja had walked straight past him.

“Did you show her?”

“Yes.”

There was no delay. She heard him the first time and her mind offered her a moment to pretend she hadn't.

“What did you say to her?”

“I said Carver knew where you lived. I told her to bring the envelope.”

“And she came because of me.”

He drew a breath. “I gave her a reason to think you'd get hurt if she stayed home.”

Ada knocked once and came in with the tea. She realised they were talking as she put down the mugs. Hex thanked her. Ninja kept looking at the photograph until she heard the door close again.

“What was in the envelope?”

“A list of people Carver's men had taken. Elise had written down their names and where they'd last been seen. She was going to give it to someone who could prove what happened. Carver wanted it back before she did.”

“Did you know my sister?”

“No. I met her that night.”

“And you thought he'd let her go?”

“I thought it was a handover. People paid. People gave things back. I'd done it before.” He looked down at the table. “That night I watched her hand Carver the envelope. He told me I was done and sent me out. By the time I understood what he'd done, the door had shut behind me and there was fire on her side of it.”

Ninja thought he might touch the picture again. He didn't.

“I tried to get the door open,” he said. “It held. I ran around the building looking for another way in. By the time I found one, I couldn't see five feet inside.”

She reached for the tea. The mug rattled against its saucer. She put it down without drinking and held one wrist in the other hand.

“You got her there.”

“Yes.”

“Do you know how many times I went over that night? Things I'd said to her. Times I'd let the phone ring. I used to think if I'd called her at the right minute she might have gone somewhere else. And the whole time there was you. You knew where she was going because you told her to go there.”

He had spent five years trying to think of something he could say to the person on the far side of that photograph. Now she was sitting a foot away from him and what he'd found to say all sounded like a man asking to be let off. He kept his mouth shut until she looked up.

“Why did you talk to me last night?”

“Because I wanted to.” His voice came out hoarse. “I kept getting as far as thinking I should go. Then I'd look at you again.”

It was the first thing he had said since the hotel that she wished she could take away with her and keep. She folded the photograph into her palm, building a crease right down the centre of her own face.

“I wish you'd gone.”

He looked at her. She got the feeling he understood that she meant it and that it hurt to mean it. She put the picture in her coat.

“You owe me the whole thing. How you got into it. What happened after. I don't want to find out one piece at a time every time somebody tries to grab me in the street.”

“You will. Ask me whatever you want.”

“I will. I'm still furious with you.”

“Okay.”

The door opened again. Ada had an older man with her who braced a hand on the frame, looked at Ninja, and stopped. She had never seen him before. His shoes were wet through, and there was a thin pink mark on one side of his face where he'd been out in the cold too long.

“This is Daniel,” Ada said.

Daniel sat opposite Ninja. His hands were steady. “You have her face,” he said. “Round the eyes.”

“You knew her?”

“I did. She gave me something to hold for you.” He reached into his coat, brought out a sealed envelope, and slid it over to her side of the table. “Five years I've been carrying that between my house and my coat. Today somebody told me you'd come to Bellwether.”

She left it on the table long enough to read her name. The first letter ran into the second, the same way it did on every birthday card she had in the drawer beside her bed.

“Who told you I was here?”

“Ada saw your name when you signed for the room last night. I've asked her to tell me if you ever came through town.”

Her thumb found the flap. It was still sealed.

“Do you know what's in it?”

“Only that she wanted you to have it if she couldn't bring it herself. She asked me to remember a name. Ruth.” He waited while Ninja moved the cup to give herself room. “I never opened it.”

Ninja slid a finger under the flap. Her sister had pressed it down so firmly it tore in two places.

The letter was two pages, folded small. Ninja read the first line twice. Elise had never been able to write her a letter without telling her something useless at the beginning, and she'd done it here too. I found your earring, she'd written. It's in the biscuit tin. She had drawn a little arrow above the word your, as if Ninja might still insist it belonged to someone else.

Ninja put a thumb over the arrow for a moment. Then she read the rest.

If I don't get back, go to the old post office in the valley. Cross the mill bridge and ask for Ruth. Tell her I sent you. She knows what I was trying to do.

Elise had written that much on the first page. The second page was different. Near the bottom she'd added another line in darker ink. I don't know if it's going to work. I should have told you sooner. The signature was just an E.

“How long before she died did she give you this?”

“A couple of days,” Daniel said. “She came round after work. I asked if she wanted something hot and she said she'd already eaten. I let her go because I thought she'd be back.”

Ninja looked down at Elise's signature. She had wanted for years to know what her sister was doing on those last days. Now she knew she'd gone to see a man named Daniel and let him think she'd be back. She wondered how many other people had waited. She couldn't think about that for very long with Hex sitting where she could see him.

“Do you know where the old post office is?”

“I know the way. I can take you there.”

Hex raised his head. He hadn't said a word since Daniel walked in. “The valley road goes past my house.”

“There's another way over the ridge.”

Ninja put the letter back in the envelope. When she reached for her bag, she remembered the man at the market picking it up off the ground. Her phone was in the pocket of her coat with the photograph. It felt strange how little she had taken with her from the hotel. The first thing she'd thought she'd need that morning was a toothbrush.

Ada came through from the front. “There's a man by the lamp post. Grey coat. He was there two minutes ago and he's still there.”

Hex eased the blind back far enough to see. Then let it fall.

“That's one of them.”

“What did the man at the hotel say?”

Hex's eyes found hers. “He asked how I'd been sleeping in Bellwether. Then he told me you were a long way from home.”

The tea was still warm. Ninja drank it standing up because she didn't want to give these men another thing she'd wanted and hadn't got to have. Then she put the cup down.

“I want to see Ruth.”

Ada pulled her coat off the hook. “Out through the yard. The wall's low by the bins. If you get to the lane behind the baker's, you can cross the top end of the market and get to the high road.”

Hex was listening to the front of the pub. Ninja heard it a moment later. A knuckle rapped against the glass, just once. Ada put her coat over a chair.

“Will he come in?” Daniel asked.

“If he does, he can buy something,” Ada said. “Go on.”

Ninja stopped at the door. Ada was already taking their mugs out front.

“I’ll pay for the whiskey when I come back.”

Ada turned. “He already paid.”

“Then tell him he can buy me another one when I’ve heard the rest.”

Hex held the door for her. She walked past him into the yard. A second later he was beside her.

They went over the wall one at a time. On the other side the cobbles were wet, and the market was starting to fill again. The florist's van was gone. Rolls of newspaper lay by the kerb where it had been.

“Ahead,” Daniel said quietly. “Past the flower stall, then right before the corner.”

A man stood with his back to them at the mouth of the lane. He had a paper bag in his hand and was picking at the edge of it. Ninja would have gone on if she hadn't noticed he never looked down at the bag. He watched the square in the glass of the shop beside him.

“Wait.”

Hex looked where she was looking. The man took a step away from the window and raised a phone to his ear. Below it he was wearing the same grey coat as the man outside the pub. Ninja tried to find the other one and couldn't.

Daniel pointed to a passage on their left. “Through there. We'll come out above him.”

They came out behind the last row of stalls. From there Ninja could see the line of the high road climbing between two houses. A girl in a school coat had stopped in the middle of it to tie a shoelace. Ninja watched her for a moment, then realised the man in the grey coat was still on the phone. She put her hand on Daniel's arm and they walked across. For the first time since she got out of bed, there was nobody between her and the way out.

The houses thinned. Farther up, gravel gave under their shoes. Ninja heard Hex slow down so he'd stay beside her.

“If I ask you something, can you tell me the truth even if it makes me want to leave?”

He looked at her. “Yes.”

“Was any of what you said last night because you wanted to find out what Elise told me?”

“No.”

She could have looked at him to see how he said it. She kept her eyes on the road. She thought she might believe him, and some part of her was still furious about that too.

Daniel stopped where the gravel gave way to grass. He pointed to a gap in the trees. “We cross there. There'll be a bridge when we get down the other side.”

Ninja could still see the hotel behind them. From up here, she could tell which window was hers. She remembered the light they'd left off, and the kettle, and Elias turning his face toward her hair when he thought she'd fallen asleep. The morning had happened too fast to grieve for the small things. She gave herself a second now.

Then she put the letter deeper into her pocket and followed Daniel up the hill. Hex came with her.
