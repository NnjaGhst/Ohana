# Elsewhere: The Hidden Valley
## Episode 2 — The Name on the Garage

The garage on Delaney Street smelled of hot oil and old cigarettes. Hex had the hood of a ten-year-old Camry propped open, hands buried in the engine block, when Carver walked in past the front desk like he owned the air.

Carver was fifty, soft-spoken, wore a windbreaker that never changed with the weather. He stood at the edge of the bay and watched Hex work for a while.

“You’re good with your hands,” Carver said.

“You’re good at standing there.” Hex didn’t look up. “Customer?”

“Not exactly.”

Hex pulled the dipstick, wiped it on his rag, checked it against the light. “Then you’re in the wrong bay.”

“You’re in the wrong bay,” Carver said. “Three years fixing other men’s cars, and you still owe rent on the first of the month. That’s the wrong bay.”

Hex straightened. He looked at Carver properly then, rag still in his fist. “You selling something?”

“I’m offering something. Work that pays what it costs to live here. No forms, no interviews, nobody deciding what you’re worth from behind a desk.”

Hex laughed, short and flat. “I’ve heard that before. Usually it’s a guy with a van.”

Carver smiled like he’d told a joke worth keeping. “Come by the shop on Halstead Thursday. Six o’clock. Ask for Ray. If you don’t like what you see, you walk, and I won’t come back.”

Hex should have said no. He knew it even then, the rag going still in his hand. But he was twenty-six and tired of being told he wasn’t the right fit, and rent was rent, and nobody had ever offered him anything that looked like a door instead of a wall.

“Thursday,” he said.

The Halstead shop sold nothing. It was a front room with a counter, a back room with a table, and men who came and went without ever looking at the merchandise. Carver introduced him to Ray, who had hands like a bricklayer and a voice like a radio at low volume.

The work started small. A car moved from one lot to another and Hex drove it, nothing in the trunk he was told about. Then a package picked up from a locker and left on a bench in a park, and Hex didn’t open it, and got paid in cash folded into a newspaper.

The money was good. The money was the problem. It made the next thing easier.

The line came in the third month. Ray put a phone on the table, a cheap one, and said a family needed to be told a man had drowned off the pier at Whitlock. There would be paperwork. There would be a body eventually, from somewhere, and it would be the right body for the story. Hex’s job was to make sure the man’s car was found where it should be found, keys in the ignition, wallet on the seat, so the story held together.

“He’s not dead,” Hex said.

“He’s dead to them,” Ray said. “That’s the point. He’s gone, and they get to grieve him instead of looking for him. You want to tell me which of those is kinder?”

Hex stood in the back room with the phone in his hand and understood, very clearly, what he was being asked to do. Not to hurt anyone. To make a lie that would hold a family’s grief in place like a splint. He thought about the man’s wife, who would get a call, who would cry, who would never know.

“The car,” Hex said.

“The car,” Ray agreed.

Hex did it. He drove the car to the pier lot at four in the morning and left it angled wrong, nose to the water, and he wiped the wheel with his sleeve even though he’d worn gloves, because that was how a scared man would have done it. He walked back along the seawall with his hands in his pockets and did not let himself think about the wife. That was the first thing he learned to do, and he learned it fast, because the alternative was to feel it every time.

The cold came later, and it came all at once.

There was a man named Soren who owed the outfit and had decided he didn’t want to pay. Ray sent Hex to a warehouse on the south side with two others, and Soren was already there, sitting on an overturned crate, hands tied in front of him. He was maybe forty, with a soft face and a shirt that had been ironed that morning.

One of the others, a thick-necked kid called Pike, started talking about Soren’s daughter, her school, her route home. Just talking. Soren came apart in the chair, begging, and Pike kept talking in that same flat voice, and Hex stood by the door and felt his own pulse slow down like water going still.

“That’s enough,” Hex said.

Pike looked at him. “Ray said make him understand.”

“He understands.” Hex crouched in front of Soren, and his voice came out even, no heat in it at all. “You’re going to pay what you owe, and then this is done, and nobody looks at your daughter again. Say it back to me.”

Soren said it back. His eyes were wet and his voice shook, but he said it.

Hex stood, and the cold was still there, settled in him now like a thing he owned. He had learned that if you let your face do what your chest was doing, they read it as weakness and they pushed. If you kept it flat, they stopped. It was a tool. He told himself it was a tool.

The records room was on the second floor of the Halstead building, and it was where Hex learned how deep the thing went. Shelves of file boxes, a desk with a lamp, and a woman named Elise who ran the whole floor like a librarian who had once been a soldier.

She was forty-odd, gray at the temples, and she did not look up when he came in.

“You’re the car boy,” she said.

“Hex.”

“I know what they call you.” She slid a folder across the desk. “Sit down. Tell me what’s wrong with this.”

He sat. The folder held a death certificate, a police report, a hospital intake form, all for a man named Ferris who had supposedly died of a heart attack in his own kitchen eleven weeks ago. Hex read it twice.

“The intake form’s dated after the death certificate,” he said. “By two days.”

Elise looked at him for the first time, and something in her face shifted, a small recalibration. “Good. Most of them don’t catch that.”

“What is this?”

“This is what happens when the story doesn’t fit the paperwork.” She took the folder back and set it in a different pile. “Ferris is alive. He’s in a house upstate, and his family thinks he’s in a box. This form is the mistake that would have made them ask questions. I fix the mistakes.”

Hex looked at the shelves. “How many?”

“Enough that I don’t sleep well.” She said it plainly, no self-pity in it. “You want to help, or you want to stand there?”

“What needs helping?”

She pulled a single page from a drawer and put it in front of him. A transfer notice, a signature at the bottom that was supposed to be a coroner in Whitlock. “This goes out tomorrow. If it goes out with that date on it, a woman named Ada Reyes is going to get a letter that tells her the wrong thing about her brother, and she’s the type to write back. I need this page to not exist.”

Hex took the page. He folded it once, twice, and put it in his jacket.

“That’s it?” Elise said.

“That’s it.”

She watched him go, and he felt her watching, and he did not turn around.

He burned the page in a drum behind the garage on Delaney Street that night, and he stood there until the ash went gray and the wind took it. He did not feel cold. He did not feel much of anything, which was the point.

On his way back through the garage, he saw Pike standing by the open door with an unlit cigarette in his hand. Pike looked past him at the drum. Hex shut the door. He knew Pike had seen enough to be asked about it later.

* * *

Carver slid the folder across the table. It was a back room with a short window too high to see through from a chair. If a person stood up, they could see the pub’s yard. Carver had been sitting there long enough for the tea in front of him to go cold.

The photograph was loose in the folder. A woman outside a corner shop in the rain, laughing at somebody the picture had cut off. She had bright pink hair, and she’d raised one shoulder against the weather. Hex had heard about her from Elise. At some point she’d told him that Ninja laughed hardest when she was trying to be cross with you. He could see what she meant.

“Who took this?” he asked.

Carver sat back. “She’s her sister. You’ve got that much.”

Hex laid the picture down. A few days ago he’d given Elise a ride after work. They’d talked about a man in her accounts who had been paid for days he swore he hadn’t worked, and about when Ninja liked to finish a bag of crisps. Elise had made him guess the flavour. He’d spent almost all the drive getting it wrong. Carver knew enough to make that conversation feel like something he should have kept secret.

“What do you want from Elise?”

“An envelope. She has a list she put together at work. The men on it have families who’ve been asking questions. The families can keep asking. What I need is for Elise to stop giving them better questions.”

He spoke as if he were going over the bill with somebody who had worked through lunch. Hex went on listening until he knew exactly what Carver wanted him to agree to. He was supposed to bring Elise and the envelope to the closed club by the river. There would be people waiting in the warehouse behind it. They’d take the papers, tell Elise how things stood, and let her go. If she was going to be given a reason to stop looking, it ought to come from somebody she knew.

“You've asked her already,” Hex said.

“I have. You're the man I’m paying to ask her again.”

Hex touched the edge of the photograph. “What have you done to her sister?”

“Nothing. She finished a shift last night and went to the corner shop. You can imagine how easy it was to find out.”

Carver wanted him to hear the rest of the sentence in his own head. It worked. Hex had seen people become cooperative after being given much less to think about. The last time he'd helped Carver make that happen, he’d repeated an assurance he wasn't in a position to give. He’d got home and let his hands sit in hot water until it seemed possible to use them for something ordinary.

He could leave now. Carver would call somebody else. That was a true thing about the outfit and a useful thing to remember if you wanted to do a job without having to decide what you were doing it for. He thought about the next man standing in the street with this picture in his hand. It was easy to picture that man being worse than him. For a moment he could almost believe he'd made a good decision.

“I'm staying with her until she gets home.”

Carver got his coat. “Fine. I want the envelope. Get her to bring it.”

Hex put the photograph into his inside pocket. He went to get his car in the rain, and twice on the way he came close to taking it out and ringing Elise to tell her what had happened. The second time he stopped with one finger on her number. If she heard him and went straight to Ninja, he had no idea what would happen on that pavement. He put the phone away. There might have been a third choice. By the time he got to the car, he’d stopped looking for one.

Elise came up the street toward the closed club with her bag held close against her coat. She saw him by the door and turned her head far enough to see whether anyone had come out behind him. No one had. That got her as far as the edge of the pool of light where he was waiting.

She told him she was taking the papers somewhere they could be used. He said the men inside would take them. She asked him how long he’d known she had them. He told her, and she laughed once through her nose at the idea that she’d actually thought he was helping her. He was close enough to see that she still had a bit of ink on her finger from writing in the office.

Then he showed her the photograph.

She reached for it without thinking. Hex let her take it. Her hands went very still a second later.

“She was waiting for me,” she said.

“Carver showed me that today.”

“What have you done to her?”

“He hasn’t got her. I can’t tell you what he’ll do if you walk.”

She lifted her head. He had never seen her look at him for that long. Her hand tightened across the photograph until it buckled in the middle.

“Look at me when you say it,” she said. “I’d like you to remember who you said it to.”

He looked at her. It was the only thing he gave her freely in the whole conversation.

“I can’t promise they’ll leave Ninja alone if you walk away.”

Elise handed the photograph back. She hadn’t folded it. She said she’d take the envelope in herself, and that she wanted Hex beside her when she asked them to leave her sister alone. He told her he would be there. She went to the club door and waited for him to catch up.

* * *

The back door of the Riverside Club was steel, painted over so many times the keyhole had shrunk to a pinhole. Hex had the key in his hand, cold from his pocket, and the envelope under his arm.

"You're sure," he said.

Elise looked at the door, not at him. "You showed me the picture. I'm sure."

He turned the key. The lock gave with a soft knock and the door swung in on a corridor that smelled of old beer and river damp. A single bulb burned at the far end, over a fire door propped with a wedge of wood. Beyond it, the warehouse proper, all that dark space and the low ceiling of the loading floor.

They went in. Hex pulled the door shut behind them and the bulb hummed.

"Carver," Elise said, quiet. "You said he'd be here."

"He said he would."

The corridor opened onto the loading floor. Pallets stacked high, a forklift parked crooked, the river smell stronger here, cold off the water. A work lamp on a stand threw a hard white circle on the concrete, and in the circle stood a folding table with a laptop and a cardboard box, and behind the table, Carver, in a good coat, hands loose at his sides.

"Hex," Carver said. "And Miss Arden. You brought her."

"She brought herself," Hex said.

Carver smiled at that, a small thing. "Close enough." He nodded at the envelope. "That's it?"

Hex put it on the table. Carver did not reach for it. He turned the laptop around instead, and the screen showed a form, fields filled in, a photograph of Elise at the top, her name spelled right, an address in another city, a date of departure.

"What is that," Elise said.

"Paperwork," Carver said. "It's how it works. People go, and the paper says they chose to. Landlords get a letter. Employers get a resignation. The bank gets a change of address. Nobody looks for someone who left on purpose." He tapped the screen. "This one's not finished. It's waiting on a decision."

Hex felt the floor shift under him, the way it does when you've walked into a room and the room has more people in it than you counted. "A decision about what."

"About who stays on the paper and who comes off it." Carver looked at Elise. "You asked to come. So ask."

Elise's hands were at her sides, still. "The names in that envelope. The ones you've been moving. I want them left alone. All of them. And I want the girl in the photograph left alone. She walks, she stays walked, nobody touches her."

"And what do I get."

"Me," Elise said. "On your paper. Whatever you need it to say."

Hex said, "Elise."

She didn't look at him. "That's the trade. You wanted a handover. This is the handover."

Carver considered her for a moment, the way a man considers a sum. Then he closed the laptop. "All right," he said. "The names stay. The girl stays. You come off the board and onto the paper." He picked up the envelope and put it inside his coat without opening it. "Hex, you can go. Your part's done."

Hex stood still. The work lamp buzzed. Somewhere out past the wall a boat went by and the water slapped at the pilings.

"Go on," Carver said, pleasant. "Door's where you left it."

Hex looked at Elise. She gave him a small nod, one that meant get out, and he understood it, and he hated it. He picked up the laptop, because it was the only thing on the table worth taking, and Carver let him, which should have told him something. He walked to the corridor and the bulb and the steel door, and he was three steps into the corridor when he heard the wedge of wood scrape on the concrete behind him, and the fire door swing shut, and the bolt go home.

He turned. Through the wired glass he could see the loading floor, Carver walking away from the door toward a side exit, unhurried, and Elise standing in the white circle of the lamp with her hand at her mouth.

"Elise!" He hit the door with the flat of his hand. The bolt held. "Get to the side door, go now, go—"

She was already moving, but not toward the side door. Toward him. She got to the wired glass and put her palm flat against it and he put his against the other side, and behind her the far end of the loading floor was going orange, low down, along the pallets, quick and quiet, the way it goes when it's been set and waiting.

"Back up," he said. "Get back, I'll kick it—"

He kicked it. The door rang and did not give. He kicked it again, and the glass cracked in a spider from the corner, and through the crack he could see her face, and the smoke coming now, rolling along the ceiling and coming down.

"Hex." Her voice came thin through the crack. "Don't. The frame's steel. Go."

"Get to the side door."

"It's chained. I saw it when we came in. He chained it before we got here." She coughed. "Go, Hex. Please."

He kicked the door until his leg went numb. The crack widened. He got his fingers into it and pulled and a piece came away and cut him and he didn't feel it. The smoke was thick on her side now, and she was low, on her knees, her hand still up against the glass, and he could see her and she could see him and there was nothing between them but a door that would not move.

"Elise. Get up. Get up and get to the river side, there's a—"

"There's nothing," she said. "There's nothing. Go."

He got both hands into the gap and heaved and the door shifted a hair and settled back. The heat came through the crack like a hand on his face. He heard other sounds now, from the far end of the floor, voices, someone hammering on something, someone calling a name that wasn't hers. There were other people in the warehouse. There had been all along.

"Elise—"

"Go." She said it flat. Her hand came away from the glass. "Tell her I'm sorry about the picture."

He did not remember the last of it. He remembered the smoke coming through the crack in a hot rope and his own hands on the door and then the door giving, all at once, the frame buckled by the heat, swinging in and hitting him and knocking him back down the corridor. He remembered crawling. He remembered the steel door at the end of the corridor, and getting it open, and the cold river air on his face, and the sound behind him, which was not a sound he could describe and would not try to.

He was on the dock. His hands were wrong. The warehouse windows were orange and the river was orange and somewhere a siren was coming, too late, the way sirens come.

He got up. He walked. He did not run, because a man running gets looked at, and he went along the dock and up the steps and into the streets where the night people were and none of them looked at him. He had the laptop under his arm. He did not remember picking it up again. He had Carver's voice in his head, saying your part's done, and he understood now what his part had been. A witness who leaves. A man who walks out of a burning building and is therefore the man who set it, if anyone needs a man who set it. The paper would say Elise left on purpose. The paper would say whatever Carver needed it to say. And Hex would be the one who was there and walked away.

He got as far as the bridge and stopped, because his hands had started to shake and he needed them still.

His phone rang.

He looked at it a long time before he answered. Carver's number. He put it to his ear.

"Hex," Carver said. He sounded like a man at the end of a good day. "You made it out. Good."

"You left the door."

"I left the door," Carver agreed. "And you left it. That's the story now. You understand the story."

"I understand it."

"Good. Then come home. We'll sort the rest in the morning. Come home, Hex."

Hex stood on the bridge with the river going under him and the cold coming up off it, and he knew exactly what coming home meant. It meant a room and a table and a laptop with his name in the fields. It meant the paper saying he left on purpose. It meant the door, and the bolt, and nobody looking for him after.

"No," he said.

"Hex."

He ended the call. He put the phone in his pocket and he stood there a moment longer, and then he turned off the bridge and walked, and he did not go home.

* * *

The motel room smelled of bleach and old cigarettes. Hex sat on the edge of the bed with his boots on, watching the gray light come up behind the curtains. He had not slept. Every time he closed his eyes he saw the warehouse ceiling coming down and heard his own voice shouting her name into the smoke. Elise. He had told her to go. He had put the photograph in her hand and pointed her at the door and she had trusted him because that was what people did when you sounded certain.

His duffel sat by the door, packed before dawn. Everything he owned now fit in it twice over. He took the photograph from his jacket pocket and looked at it again. The woman in it was walking, caught half-turned, her face sharp and unguarded in a way that suggested she did not know she was being watched. Ninja. He had never met her. He knew her name from Carver and her face from a camera and that was all, and now Elise was dead because of what he had done with both.

He put the photo back in the inner pocket, zipped it, and left the key on the nightstand.

The bus north took six hours and he did not sleep on that either. The road climbed out of the city and the towns got smaller and the trees got closer until the driver called out Bellwether and Hex stepped down onto a gravel shoulder in front of a gas station with two pumps and a hand-painted sign. The air was cold and clean and it hurt his throat. He stood there with his bag and let the bus pull away and did not look back at it.

He gave his name as Elias at the motel that rented rooms by the week. The woman behind the desk did not ask for anything but cash and he paid her for two weeks and took the key. In the room he sat on the bed and looked at the photo again and then put it away and went out to find work.

Len's garage was three blocks off the main road, a low cinderblock building with a bay door that stuck halfway up and a sign that said LEN'S in letters that had been repainted so many times they were soft at the edges. Hex stood in the doorway until a big man in his sixties rolled out from under a truck on a creeper and looked at him upside down.

"You need something?"

"Work."

Len sat up. He had grease on his forearms and a rag hanging from his back pocket and he looked Hex over the way a man looks at a used part.

"You know engines?"

"Enough."

"Enough is a word that hides things."

Hex said nothing. Len watched him for a while and then pointed at the truck.

"Timing's off. Tell me what you hear."

Hex listened to it turn over. Then he said, "Sounds like the chain's stretched. And your number three plug is fouling."

Len grunted. He got up and wiped his hands and said, "Come back tomorrow at seven. We'll see if you're worth a wage."

Hex came back at seven. He worked that day and the next and by the end of the week Len had stopped watching him every minute. By the end of the month Len had given him a key to the bay door and stopped counting the cash drawer in front of him. That was trust, Hex knew, and he knew also that he had done nothing to earn it except keep his head down and his hands busy and his mouth shut. It was the same trick he had used his whole life. Be useful. Be quiet. Let people fill in the rest.

He did not tell Len about the city. He did not tell him about Elise or Carver or the photograph folded in his jacket. When Len asked where he had come from Hex said, "South," and Len nodded like that was an answer.

The room at the motel got old fast. He found a cabin at the end of a dirt road, one room and a woodstove and a porch that sagged, and he paid the owner cash and moved his duffel in. The first night he sat on the porch and listened to the trees and thought about writing to her.

He had paper. He had a pen. He sat at the table with the photo propped against the salt shaker and he wrote, I'm sorry about Elise. She was there because of me. Then he looked at it and tore it up. He wrote, You don't know me but I know what Carver is and you should too. He tore that up as well. He wrote nothing for a long time and then he wrote, I have a photograph of you. You should have it. He looked at that one the longest. Then he burned it in the stove.

The truth was he was afraid. Carver had people everywhere and a letter was a thread and a thread could be pulled. If he wrote to her and Carver found it he would put her in the ground the way he had put Elise in the ground. And if he found her himself, if he walked up to her and said what he had to say, then he would be the one who told her. He would be the one whose face she saw when she learned that a woman had died in a warehouse because a stranger had sent her there with a photograph.

He did not know which was worse. So he did neither.

The years went by in a way that surprised him. He had not expected to stay. He had expected to heal enough to move and then to move, but the seasons turned and he was still there. Len taught him to fish in the creek behind the garage and Hex taught Len to use the diagnostic scanner he had bought off a man in town. They drank beer on the tailgate on Fridays and did not talk much. Len's wife had died years before and he never said her name and Hex never asked. That was the shape of their friendship. Two men who did not ask.

He got good at the work. He got good at the town. He learned which roads flooded in spring and which farmer paid late and which one never paid at all. He fixed a tractor for a woman named Ruth and she brought him a pie and he ate it on the porch and watched the light go down and thought, I could keep this. That was the first time he let himself think it. It scared him more than Carver did.

He still had the photo. It lived in the inside pocket of whatever jacket he was wearing and when the jacket wore out he moved it to the new one. Sometimes weeks went by and he did not look at it. Then a night would come when he could not sleep and he would take it out and look at her face and think about what she deserved to know. She deserved to see it. She deserved to hear it from someone who had been there. He just did not want to be that someone. He did not want to be the reason her life split in two.

On the fifth spring Len shook his hand and said, "You're all right, Elias," and Hex felt the words land somewhere deep and did not say anything back because he did not trust his voice. That night he went home and looked at the photo and did not burn anything and did not write anything. He put it away and slept.

The fifth year ended on a Friday. Hex closed the bay door and locked it and checked the padlock twice the way he always did. The evening was warm and the light was going gold and the town was full of the sound of somebody's radio. He took his keys out and looked at them in his hand. The house key had a red tag on it, chipped at one corner, that Len had given him the day he moved into the cabin. Hex had never replaced it. He turned it over once and put the keys in his pocket and started walking.

The Crooked Lantern was two blocks down and it had a green door and a window that threw light onto the sidewalk. He could hear voices inside. He walked up to the door and put his hand on it and stopped. He did not know why. He stood there with the warm wood under his palm and the noise of the place coming through it and he thought about the photograph in his jacket and the letter he had never written and the life he had built out of silence. Then he reached for the handle.

* * *

The Crooked Lantern smelled of wet wool and old beer, and Ada had his drink on the bar before he'd finished shaking the rain off his coat. Rye, neat, the good bottle she kept under the counter for the three people in Bellwether who could tell the difference. He wrapped his hand around it and let the cold outside drain out of his shoulders.

The place was half full. Two men were arguing about a fence and a woman was feeding coins into the jukebox. Hex sat with his back to the wall, the way he always did, and watched the door.

It was habit. The door had never opened on anything worse than a man who owed Ada money. But habit was a coat you couldn't take off, and he wore it every night.

He was two swallows in when it hit him. No reason. Just the floor dropping out, the way it did sometimes, his heart going like a bird in a box. His hand tightened on the glass and he made himself breathe, made himself count the bottles behind Ada's shoulder, red label, green label, the dusty one nobody drank. It passed. It always passed. But Ada was looking at him now, her eyebrow up, and he shook his head at her, the small shake that meant nothing's wrong, and she went back to her customer.

The door stuck the way it always stuck, and somebody outside put a shoulder into it. First push, nothing. Second push, and the door swung in and the rain came with her.

He knew her before he knew he knew her. That was the thing he'd carry out of the Lantern that night. The shape of her, the way she came through a door like she was already three steps into the room, the coat, the dark hair wet at the edges. It was a photograph he'd looked at too many times, folded soft at the corners, a girl standing in front of a wall he'd never seen, squinting into a sun he hadn't been there for. He'd looked at that picture so long he'd started to think he knew the face. He didn't. The face in the door was older. Tired in a way the picture wasn't. Alive in a way no picture ever was.

She'd come. He'd never once let himself believe she'd come.

She didn't look at him. She took the stool by the window, the one with the draft, and shrugged out of her coat and laid a paperback on the sill, facedown, spine cracked. Ada was finishing with the kid at the end of the bar, taking his money, saying something that made him laugh and look ashamed at the same time.

Hex sat very still. His rye was warm now and he didn't want it. Every part of him that had spent years being careful was saying the same thing in the same flat voice. Go home. The bag's by the door. Passport, cash, the other name. Go home and be gone from Bellwether before the morning shift changes. It was a good plan. He'd made it a hundred times. He'd never had a reason to use it and now he had one, and the reason was sitting by the window with a paperback she wasn't reading.

He put on his coat. It was the right thing, the only thing, and his hands knew it even if the rest of him was slow. He pulled the cuff down over his wrist and the seam gave, a soft rip along the inside of the sleeve where the stitching had gone years ago, and he stood there with his arm half in and half out of the coat like a man caught doing something.

Because he couldn't leave. That was the truth of it, and it sat in him like a stone. He'd carried the guilt so long it had worn a groove and he'd stopped feeling the edges of it. But seeing her, the real her, the wet hair and the cracked spine and the way she held her shoulders like she expected the room to come at her, the guilt got sharp again. It wasn't a photograph anymore. It was a person who'd come to a town she had no business being in, and he knew why, and he knew what he'd done, and the knowing was worse than it had ever been.

And still, under all that, something else was moving. Something warm and stupid and slow, the way feeling comes back into a hand that's been asleep. She tucked her hair behind her ear. She did it the same way she did in the picture, and he thought, that's her, that's actually her, and his chest hurt in a way that had nothing to do with the panic.

He didn't look her in the eye. He couldn't. He stood there with his torn cuff and his coat half on and watched her out of the corner of his eye and told himself he was still leaving. The plan was still good. He'd go home, get the bag, be gone. He'd leave her to whatever she'd come for and she'd never know he'd been in the room.

Ada came down the bar, wiping it with a rag that had seen better decades.

"You're going," she said. Not a question.

He nodded.

"Rain's getting worse."

He nodded again. His throat was tight and he didn't trust it. He looked at the rag, at the ring of water it left on the wood, at anything but the window.

"You know her," Ada said. Quiet. Ada didn't miss much and she never made a thing of it, which was why people told her things.

He didn't answer. His hand was on the back of his chair, the wood worn smooth where a thousand hands had held it, and he couldn't make himself let go and couldn't make himself sit.

Then he said it. It came out of him before he'd decided to say it, low, like it cost him.

"Send her a whiskey."

Ada stopped wiping.

"No message," he said. "Just the whiskey."

She looked at him a long moment. He kept his eyes on the bar. He could feel her deciding something, and he didn't want to know what.

"Which one," she said.

"The good one."

"That's your bottle."

"It's hers now."

Ada put the rag down and reached under the counter and brought up the good bottle, and the sound of the cork was very loud in the room. He still had one hand on the back of his chair. He was still going to leave. He was going to walk out the stuck door and up the hill and put his ready bag over his shoulder and be somebody else by morning, and he'd never see her again, and that was the right thing, that was the only thing.

But he didn't move.

Ada poured. The whiskey caught the low light and went amber and steady, and she set the glass on her tray and came out from behind the bar. Hex watched her go, watched the tray pass the loggers and the jukebox woman and the kid who'd stopped laughing, and the whole room seemed to slow down around that one glass.

Ada set the whiskey down in front of Ninja. Hex was standing with one hand on the back of his chair when Ninja looked up. Their eyes met.
