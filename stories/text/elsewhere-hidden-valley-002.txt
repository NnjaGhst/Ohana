# Elsewhere: The Hidden Valley
## Episode 2 — The Name on the Garage

Previously on Elsewhere, Ninja lost her sister Elise, and spent five years learning to get through the days without her. Now she's taking a few days away. On her first evening in Bellwether, she pushed open the door of the Crooked Lantern.

Five years earlier, Hex was lying on the wet floor of a garage trying to persuade a bolt out of a wheel.

His uncle used to say that when a bolt refused to turn, you should put the wrench down and work out what was holding it before you took it personally. Hex always took it personally for a minute first. It was good to get that out of the way.

The van belonged to a woman who sold flowers from a stall near the station. The other garage had told her she'd have to leave it until Monday. Monday would have been three days too late. Hex had heard her on the phone while he was waiting for a cup of tea at the café behind the warehouse. He'd asked what kind of van it was and then spent an evening in the florist's brother's garage with a light balanced on the edge of a bucket.

He turned the wrench. The bolt came loose.

When he finished, the florist asked him how much he wanted. He gave her a number. She gave him that, plus a bunch of flowers she'd taken home from the stall because one stem had broken.

“There's nothing wrong with the others,” she said.

At home he cut them down to fit in a jug. They brightened up a kitchen he had barely used that month. He had enough money to pay his rent now. He also knew that at some point the man who'd provided most of it would ring him and tell him where to be. Sometimes the flowers on the kitchen counter and the phone next to them felt like things belonging to two different people.

He'd got into the work through the warehouse. One evening he'd been asked to drive a car from one place to another, drop off the keys and come back by train. It paid four times what he'd made that week. The second time, he'd asked what was in the boot. The man paying him said, “A good reason to keep your radio on.”

Hex ought to have put his keys down then. Instead, he started getting good at working out which questions a man expected him to ask and which ones it was safer to save for later. The crew liked him best when he looked as if nothing could surprise him. Eventually he'd learned to wear that expression into the supermarket. Once a little girl said hello to him in the cereal aisle and he had to think quite hard about how to make his face answer her.

On the way into the café one Tuesday, he held the door for a woman whose paper carrier bag had split. A lemon got halfway to the road before Hex caught it under his shoe.

“Very dignified,” she said.

“You've got three more in the gutter.”

“Then this one gets to tell the others what happened.”

He brought the bag in and the woman behind the counter found an empty bread wrapper to put the lemons in. Hex took his tea to his usual table. The woman he'd helped took a seat two tables away. He would see her at the café again. He remembered her because she thanked him for catching the lemon and then paid for his toast without mentioning it.

A week later, somebody from the crew handed him a set of car keys and a note. He was to pick up a clerk named Elise and drive her to a building across town. When he pulled in at the address, the woman with the lemons opened the passenger door.

“Fancy seeing you here,” she said.

He asked her if she knew whom she was working for.

“I have a fairly good idea.”

“What do you do?”

“Paperwork. What do you do?”

“At the moment, I'm driving you across town.”

“Excellent. Let me know if that changes.”

By the end of the drive, he knew she had a younger sister who kept lending her things and claiming she would never do it again. He knew because Elise had been planning to buy an umbrella on the way back, and when he asked why she wasn't carrying one, she'd said she was already on her sister's third.

“We get on very well, as long as it's dry,” she said.

He liked her. She made him feel like somebody offering a lift. It had been a long time since anybody from work had done that.

* * *

Before there was an envelope to collect, there was a Saturday when Hex took Elise to the warehouse to look for a man who had been paid twice.

The man was the florist's brother. Hex knew this before he knew what the two payments were for. He'd heard the name while he was repairing the van. The florist had been telling him that if the light went out, her brother was the only person who knew where he kept spare bulbs. It had been a name with a job attached to it then. Someone you could ring when the garage light went out.

Elise showed Hex the sums on a notebook page while they waited for the warehouse to open. Two identical payments. One invoice number. The rest of it would have made sense if either number happened to be a mistake. Both had been approved, with time to read the description before the second approval went through.

She wanted to ask the man which payment he had actually received. Hex asked her what they would do if the answer was both. She turned to a fresh notebook page and said she supposed she would write that down too.

Inside the warehouse somebody had set up a folding table with work gloves and a box of doughnuts. It was the part of the morning when people came in to get something out of the rain and stayed to see if anyone was putting the kettle on. A forklift passed at the far end. Hex knew two of the people near the table, and both looked at his companion a little too long. He took a doughnut and handed it to her before he got one for himself. She sat down on a spare chair as if she'd had breakfast there every day that week.

When the florist's brother came in, Elise showed him the amounts and asked whether she could borrow him for two minutes. He looked at Hex first. Hex put the food down and went over to look at a rack of bicycle lamps. A few weeks earlier he would have stayed by the table without thinking about it. It was a little thing to leave somebody enough room to say what he meant.

He heard the man say the second payment hadn't gone to him. Somebody higher up had asked him to write his name on a sheet that had both numbers on it. He'd tried to explain the error, and the person had shown him which line to sign. He wanted to know if this meant he would have to give the money back. Elise said she'd write down what he'd told her. If anyone wanted him to change it, she wanted him to ring her.

On the way outside, he asked Hex if they were all right. He still seemed to be speaking about something that had passed between the two of them. Hex said he meant to come by the garage again if there was time, because somebody should put the proper wheel wrench back where he'd found it. The man laughed in relief. If you'd been watching from the folding table, you would have seen exactly two people briefly discuss a wheel wrench.

Hex and Elise walked back to the café. She ordered a cup of tea and put both payments into a little table on her page, in tidy blue ink. Above them, on the previous page, she had written a list of groceries. He asked her if she really kept all of it in one place. She said if another person in the world wanted both the invoice number and three lemons, they were welcome to a look.

A while after that, Hex began helping her collect answers. He drove her to a takeaway after closing. The cook came out wearing a stained apron and found them a bench where they could all sit down. He waited two minutes for Elise to ask him what had happened, and then said he had already been through it with somebody on the previous Tuesday. Elise asked if anyone had written it down. The cook said the man had brought a pen and borrowed a receipt.

There were three or four conversations like it. Common words acquired a second meaning for Hex. An account had been corrected. A driver had been sent home. The details had been checked. He had to listen for the answer to the question behind the one Elise had asked. Which driver? Whose home? How much did it cost anyone to tell you?

He still did jobs for Carver. That was the part he would have found hardest to explain to somebody watching all of this from the outside. One evening he came in and found his kitchen shelf leaning abruptly forward. A jar had slid down it and landed by the sink intact. He fixed the shelf, made himself some toast and took a call about driving a car across town. There was time to say he'd given up the work. The man at the other end asked if he was listening, and Hex said he could be there for nine.

It helped to be with Elise again, working through one answer at a time. She had a way of saying when something upset her without asking anyone else to decide whether it ought to. Once after a man stopped in the middle of a sentence and walked away from the two of them, she said she needed ten minutes. They went into a betting shop because it was raining and stood by the quiet end of a counter, watching a race neither of them understood. Afterwards she said the ten minutes had worked.

She told him a little more about her sister on the way to the car. Ninja had once come to get her in heavy rain and spent half the walk pretending she had only happened to be going that way. Elise knew perfectly well that she'd been in the middle of dinner. She let it last until Ninja started accusing her of having left the good umbrella behind on purpose. Then she thanked her. That was a conversation they could both understand.

Hex remembered the shape of the story long after he'd lost most of the details. A person cared enough to show up. The other person gave her enough room to complain about the rain first. There were few relationships in his life where anybody could afford to spend the time that way.

At the café the next morning, the woman who brought the tea said a man had asked for him earlier. Hex checked his phone. There hadn't been a message. She was wiping down the table as she spoke, and by the time he got her to look up, she'd already told him the part she thought he needed to hear. Somebody wanted to know what time he usually came in.

He ate the toast before he answered Carver's next call.

* * *

A few weeks later, a man named Carver put a folder on Hex's café table and sat down across from him. Hex knew Carver's car by the smell of the peppermint sweets he ate when he was waiting for someone to answer a question.

“The club by the river,” Carver said. “Thursday, eight thirty. You’re picking up an envelope.”

“From who?”

“Elise. You met her.”

Hex turned the pages. There was an address he already knew and instructions on where to sit. At the back, a photograph had been clipped upside down.

He turned it around. A woman was standing outside a corner shop laughing at something beyond the frame. There was a bag under her arm and a bus moving out of view behind her. He could make out a streak of rain near the edge where the lens had caught it. Her name was typed beneath the image. Ninja. The instruction below said to call immediately if she showed up.

“Elise's sister?” Hex asked.

Carver nodded.

“She's bringing something as well?”

“No. She's got her own job. Thursday nights she gets off at eight.”

Hex looked up. “How long have we been watching her?”

Carver slid the folder back across the table until the photograph was out of sight. “I’m asking you to tell me if you see her. You don't have to do anything else.”

Carver's phone rang. He went out the back, leaving the folder on the table. Hex waited until the door shut and took the photograph off its clip. All the pages had been numbered, except the photograph. He put it in the inside pocket of his coat and closed the folder.

At the time, Hex told himself he'd give it to Elise. She would know how to talk to her sister about it. Then he thought about Thursday and what it meant to put a photograph of somebody's sister in the briefing for a job involving Elise. He couldn't get Ninja's expression out of his head. She looked completely at ease. The man who'd taken it knew what time she went home.

Hex left his tea unfinished and went back to his flat. He took the picture out and stood in the kitchen while a little water ran from the jug he'd just refilled. He could hear it dripping off the counter onto the floor. He put a towel under it and kept thinking.

* * *

The afternoon before the handoff, Hex phoned Elise from his flat. She picked up after two rings. In the background he could hear a till opening and someone asking if she wanted a bag.

“I heard you're meeting me tomorrow,” he said.

“I'm hoping that's the extent of my evening.”

“Is it normal for them to make this much fuss over their accounts?”

“No. And I don't think it's the accounts they're worried about.”

He asked her what she meant. She waited to answer until she was outside the shop. He heard a loose wheel rattling on what might have been a shopping trolley.

She said she'd been keeping short notes about jobs where the name of the person signing off didn't match the name of the person giving the order. At first it was because she was tired of being called in to correct the records after hours. Then a woman came to ask what had happened to her brother. Elise remembered entering the cost of a job that matched the day he'd disappeared. There were two numbers against it. One was for a car. She'd spent all evening trying to work out what the other one bought.

“Maybe don't tell me that on the phone,” Hex said.

“I've been trying to figure out where I can say it out loud.”

He could have told her about the photograph straight away. He thought about it while she took a few steps along the pavement. He didn't want to send her running to her sister's flat if somebody was watching to see where she'd go. He wished afterwards that he'd said exactly that and let her decide.

“Do you need to go anywhere before the club tomorrow?” he asked.

“Why?”

“I can give you a lift.”

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, “Let's see how it goes. I can get myself to the club.”

“Alright.”

“And if you get there first, there's a kettle. Let it run for a bit before you fill it. The pipes make the water taste like a spoon.”

He laughed. When he called the number again the following evening, it rang out.

* * *

The social club hadn't opened to members for months. On Thursday evening the river was high and there was a light on upstairs.

Elise was waiting in a room with a stack of folded tables against one wall. Hex had thought she'd be angry. The first thing he noticed was how carefully she put down her cup, as if she had to watch her own hand to get it to the table.

“Did you bring the envelope?” he asked.

“I brought it this morning. I changed my mind about leaving it.”

“Where is it now?”

She looked at the stairs. “Somewhere they've got no reason to go. They rang me after I'd got home and read out my sister's address.”

Hex reached into his coat. He put the photograph face up on the table.

Elise recognised the moment it was taken. “I borrowed her umbrella.”

Hex took the chair on the other side of the table. There was a small clock on the wall behind her. The second hand stopped each time it got to the top. He could hear it trying to go past.

“I can take you home,” he said. “We can talk to her together.”

Elise put a palm flat on the photograph. For a moment it looked like a picture she'd brought herself. Then she pushed it toward him again.

“If I go home, somebody will be waiting there. That's what they wanted me to understand when they called.”

“Somewhere else, then. You can choose.”

She looked at him for a long second. Then she got to her feet.

They heard the front door below. An instant later two sets of footsteps started up the stairs. Hex put the photograph back in his coat. He'd seen a second door on the landing coming in and had been trying to work out whether it led down to the river when Carver came into the room.

“How was the drive?” Carver asked.

“Fine.”

“The car’s still blocking the road. Move it round the back.”

“The road's empty.”

“Then it won't take you long.”

The man who'd come up behind him stayed on the landing. He'd been there the first time Hex had been told to stop asking questions. Hex looked at Elise. She had both hands on the back of the chair. He'd had a few seconds to do something different. He might spend the next five years working through the things he could have done with them.

He walked out. He went down the first flight and got partway down the second. A chair scraped hard across the floor above him.

He turned around.

On the landing, he hit the light switch. It took a second for the light to come on. The room door had closed and there was nobody standing on the landing anymore. He said Elise's name through the door and heard nothing. By the time he got it open, he was too late to help her. Two other people had died that evening. There were sounds downstairs again before he could do anything for any of them.

Hex left through the door he'd noticed before. It opened onto a passage with a window at the end. By the time he got outside, the rain was light enough that he could see the building in the water.

He stood where he was for a few seconds. In the other pocket of his coat were keys to a flat with a jug of flowers on the kitchen counter. He thought about going back for something and couldn't name what it was. Then he heard the front door and started walking.

* * *

By morning Hex was at a railway station. A television over the coffee counter showed part of the road by the club. The news said the police were contacting families. Hex walked toward the coffee counter and then back to the bench. He repeated that twice before he understood that he was waiting for the screen to tell him what to do.

Carver rang. Hex let the first two calls go. On the third he answered.

“Did you speak to anybody?” Carver asked.

“Who?”

“The police.”

The train to the city came in. Hex watched its windows pass.

“Come home,” Carver said. He said it with the easy certainty of a man who knew exactly which door Hex had to walk through to get home.

“My phone's going to die,” Hex said.

He ended the call, switched the phone off and bought a train ticket for a place where he'd never taken a job. He went through there and changed again. By the time he stepped down in Bellwether, his shoulders hurt from keeping them still.

The bar was open. Hex sat near the counter and asked the woman pouring somebody else's drink if she knew of any rooms. She had one upstairs that she used for visiting relatives when she could persuade them to visit. She asked his name. He'd spent half an hour on the train coming up with one simple enough to say while somebody was waiting.

“Elias Ward,” he said.

“Ada.” She handed over a key on a blue cord. “You'll hear the ferry early. Go back to sleep if it wakes you.”

He slept until noon. By then Ada had news for him. There hadn't been a morning ferry in eleven years.

“People lie about worse things,” she said, setting a cup of tea in front of him.

Hex thought he might be able to stay there a few days.

* * *

Ada put Hex to work before he got as far as the garage. On his fourth morning in Bellwether she dragged a small table out of an upstairs room, pulled it halfway across the carpet and asked him if he knew any reason a round leg ought to have four holes in it.

He said he could think of several. He got down on the floor to find out how many were relevant. Someone had fixed the leg once before. They had used a piece of wood from something with blue paint and pushed it into the loosest side of the joint. He made a new wedge from wood Ada had put aside for the stove. She told him there was soup downstairs when he had finished.

After he carried the table back, he did the thing he'd done all week. He went to the station to look at the timetable and stood there until the next time he could leave was well fixed in his mind. There were only four trains a day. If he missed the last one, he knew how long it took to walk to a place with more.

An old man came and sat on the bench under the timetable with a newspaper rolled into a tube. He watched the row of trees behind the tracks for several minutes. Hex stood at the opposite end of the shelter reading every note in the smallest print. Finally he heard the man breathe out through his nose.

He thought something was funny. It turned out he was looking at a crow which had just picked up the string around a broken bundle of apples. The bird was trying to get a whole apple by pulling the string. Hex had watched it make exactly that mistake twice without noticing.

The man's name was Len. He hadn't met anybody from the village at that point who could stand still for that long and had no idea where they were going. He told Hex a bus came through the market on Tuesdays if he'd rather go somewhere by road. Then he told him which way the sub-postmaster walked home in case the station toilet was shut and he needed to borrow a key.

Hex asked if there was anything in Bellwether people didn't have to borrow a key for. Len suggested the river. He could walk down to it from the market in about twenty minutes, although it might take longer coming back.

Hex went down that evening. There was enough light to see water moving quickly around the first bend. A fishing platform leaned into it on two wooden legs. The rail had gone smooth from people resting a wrist on it. Hex stood there a little way back, where he could still see the path between the trees.

He didn't know how to answer the question of what he planned to do now. Sometimes it came down to things he could finish in an hour. He had found somewhere to sleep. He had found a toilet with a lock that worked. He'd fixed a table leg. Ada had told him how much it would cost to stay a week. Somewhere after those answers was a question about what he intended to do with the photograph hidden at the bottom of his bag, and what he would say about Elise when he had to tell anyone who had known her.

He took a stone off the railing and threw it into the river. It was meant to skim. It entered the water straight down and went out of sight. He tried again with another stone and did very slightly better.

Later he paid Ada for the week. He went upstairs with the key in his pocket, noticed the blue cord had come untied at one end, and tightened the knot. That was the first night he left his bag unpacked on the chair instead of leaving it at the foot of the bed, where he could catch hold of it in one movement.

By the next morning somebody on the market knew he'd mended the table. A woman brought him a broken hinge, with the cupboard door still attached. She said Ada had assured her he could have a look. He worked out she had no screwdriver at home and nothing wrong with the hinge except one screw that had loosened. He found the right tool and put it back together while she told him about a bad patch in the road she always forgot about until she was standing in it.

The woman told two customers who passed them that Elias could fix hinges. Hex thought this sounded a little premature. By then she was explaining where he probably lived. He waited for the tight feeling in his chest to pass. There were years in which a stranger being able to tell another stranger his name and where he slept would have been reason enough to leave town. Listening to this woman, he realised she was going to stop talking as soon as she found someone who wanted to hear about the road.

In the afternoon he used Len's directions and found the way to the train station from the river without going through the market. Then he came back the other way. On his way into the pub Ada was writing the lunch prices on a small board and blowing the chalk dust off them as she went. He held the door for somebody on the way in and stayed to eat.

In a few days he would see Len again, bent over a white car with a line of rain going down his back. He'd recognise the roll of newspaper on the passenger seat before he got a look at the man's face. Len would remember him, too. It was a small village. By that point Hex was beginning to understand how much of a comfort there could be in that.

* * *

The garage sat at the far end of the market, where the road dropped away toward the station. There was a little white car parked outside it the first time Hex walked past. The man beside it had the bonnet up and a line of rain down his back. Hex stopped and asked what was wrong.

The man said, “It was fine ten minutes ago.”

Hex looked inside. “And the ten minutes before that?”

“It was also fine.”

“Brilliant. Has it always made that noise in the rain?”

There was a pause. Then the man said, “How much of your day do you have?”

“Most of it.”

It was Len, the man from the station. He'd run the garage for thirty-two years and had been trying to convince his daughter he could do it for another thirty-two. By the end of the afternoon Hex had helped find what was wrong with the white car and moved a stack of winter tyres away from the leaking roof. Len asked him to come back on Monday.

On his first paid morning Hex found out the garage radio only played if there was something heavy on the plug. He asked whether Len would mind if he repaired it. Len said people had tried. Hex fixed it while his tea was brewing, and the first thing it played was the middle of a very long song about a dog. Len said he preferred it broken. Hex caught him humming the song the next day.

Work at the garage was the first thing in Bellwether that started and finished at reliable times. Customers wanted to know whether they could take their cars home. Len had no objection to Hex asking why a job needed doing. If somebody arrived just as they were closing, they looked at each other and settled it with a shrug.

After a few months Hex could hear a bike coming up outside and tell which postman was on the hill. He could sometimes get through lunch without checking the glass over the garage door for a face from the city. When one did look familiar, he'd set his tea down and keep his hands busy until the customer came close enough to turn into somebody else.

One January, two years after Hex arrived, a woman called the garage as they were turning the lights off. Her car had stopped beyond the last farm. Len was already reaching for the heavy coat he wore when the temperature dropped. Hex offered to go.

Out on the lane there was a thin hard layer of snow over the road. The woman's daughter was waiting in the back of the car with an open schoolbag and a single orange glove. Hex asked her to hold a torch while he took a look. She spent a thoughtful minute trying to keep the beam still, then asked him what he'd do if he had an orange glove for one hand and a blue one for the other.

“Wear them both,” he said.

“That's what I said.”

The mother laughed. Hex did what he could by the roadside and drove with them back to the garage. Len had left the lights on. While they waited for the woman's brother to come and get them, they made tea and hung the orange glove over the radiator with a clothes peg. The woman left with the keys to Len's spare car. Her daughter collected her glove and thanked Hex with a little salute.

On his way home he thought about the first garage and the woman with the flowers. So much had changed that he'd started counting his life from the day he took the train. It felt good to recognise a part of himself that had already been there.

Len eventually cut back to mornings. He left Hex in charge after lunch. In the winter, they kept a spare pair of boots near the heater for whoever decided to cross the market without looking at the sky first. People in Bellwether began sending friends to the garage and telling them to ask for Elias. Each time it happened, Hex answered a little sooner.

In the drawer upstairs at the bar, under a key that no longer opened anything, there was still a photograph. He took it out once to write a letter to Ninja. It began with 'I was there.' He put the pen down after that. He could imagine it dropping through the slot at her home. He could imagine her having to read it standing in the hall without even knowing who had sent it. He folded the sheet and kept it with the photo.

Some nights, long after he stopped reaching for the drawer every day, he could see her face clearly enough without opening it. He could almost hear the laugh. That was the part his memory always added, because the paper gave him no way of knowing what it actually sounded like.

* * *

In his third year at the garage, Hex made a mistake with a customer's van.

It was the blue delivery van he'd heard coming down the hill every Thursday morning. One day he worked on a little rattle in the door and told the driver he'd found it. Two streets away the driver found the rattle again. There was nothing dangerous about it. The man was going to be in the van for six hours and had paid Hex to give him an afternoon without that particular sound.

A younger Hex might have put it down to the age of the van and waited to see if anybody came back. This time he heard the van starting up across the market, caught the driver before he pulled out, and asked him to come round the block once with him in the passenger seat. The man looked at the clock. Hex offered to help him carry the first three boxes if it made the time up.

They drove round Bellwether until the rattle came back. They went over the same bump three times. On the fourth, the man pressed a hand to the paper map shoved into the pocket on his side of the door and held it still. The sound stopped. Beneath the map was a loose piece of trim Hex hadn't even looked at.

They went back to the garage. He took it apart and fixed it. The man sat in the office and filled in his next receipt. When they were done, Hex gave him the money he'd paid that morning. The customer said he could keep it for staying about to fix the problem. They went back and forth until Len, who was ready for lunch, suggested they put some of it toward sandwiches.

The driver left with a sandwich for the road. A week later he was back. The door was quiet. What he wanted now was for Hex to look at a scrape in the rear corner so he could tell his wife whether it would come out.

That evening Hex thought about the number of times he'd been told to make something look as though it had been done right. Usually the person asking could have had the real thing put right, if they'd wanted to give anybody the time. It had taken him an hour to find a rattle and several years to feel at home saying he'd missed it.

He shut down the garage, rinsed three tea mugs and left the smallest one on the counter for the following morning. A girl had stood in the doorway that week and asked if there was anywhere she could go to watch a tyre being put back together. Her bus was due in ten minutes. Hex had said he was going to start at eight the next day and she was welcome to come and see. At a quarter to eight he was in the garage with the light on. The girl arrived with her father and a sandwich to share.

* * *

On an afternoon toward the end of Hex's fourth year in Bellwether, a grey car pulled up by the garage with a number plate he knew.

He could see it through the window from the workbench. It came to a stop a little too far from the kerb, with one of its brake lights out. He'd changed a different bulb in that car a few months before he left the city. He remembered the driver opening a packet of peppermints with both hands and asking him which part of it was supposed to be easy.

Hex took one step toward the back door. He stopped when he reached Len's mug. It was still steaming. Len was out on a call and had told him a customer might come about a headlight. There was a number on the sheet where the office girl had put out the other jobs for the afternoon. Hex checked it. That wasn't the number.

A woman got out of the grey car. She had a coat over one arm and a key ring with a hard yellow ball attached. A man about her age opened the passenger door and wanted to know if she was going to be long, because he still had a parcel to collect somewhere up the road.

Hex waited behind the counter until she came in. She said she was from just over the hills, and somebody at the station had sent her. She'd bought the car three weeks ago. The other brake light had worked when they tested it then. Did he know if that meant the light was under warranty?

He asked where she'd bought it. It was a used car lot a couple of streets from the warehouse. There was nothing unusual about that. It had a paved forecourt and a glass office, and people who couldn't get the cars they wanted anywhere else thought it was worth going. Most weeks at least one car from that part of the city found its way through Bellwether.

Hex said he could check the bulb and let her know. She handed over the keys.

He went out and opened the boot. There was a clean bag of shopping inside and a child's scooter with a scuffed purple wheel. Whoever had cleaned it for sale had left two little rectangles of adhesive where something had been taken off the lining. Hex looked at those for longer than he needed to. Then he lifted out the groceries to keep them away from the work and changed the bulb.

It took ten minutes. When she tried to pay him, he had to remember how much they normally charged. The man with the parcel came back carrying a long flat package in brown paper. He held it up and asked her to guess what was in it. She said if it was another shelf, she'd make him hang this one himself. They stood there for a moment adjusting who would get into the car first. Then they pulled out and left.

Hex watched them down the market as far as the bend. Both brake lights came on at the turn.

At four thirty somebody came with a headlight that really did match the job sheet. Hex replaced that bulb as well. He took a little time putting away the tools afterwards. The number plate was still in his head, with the date he'd last seen it and the old precise feeling that he had about half an hour to work out what he needed before he went.

When Len got back, Hex said the afternoon had been quiet. Len took a sip from the mug he'd left on the bench and pulled a face. Hex got them both fresh tea. They sat in the small office looking through the day's paperwork. The rain had started again, quietly, against the window above Len's head.

Hex thought about telling him. He got as far as explaining that one of the cars had come from an old part of town he used to know. Len asked if it had been a long time since he'd been there. Hex said it had. Len waited, with one finger resting on a place in the invoice he meant to come back to. Hex had the feeling there would have been room for anything he chose to say.

He said a friend of his had died there. It came out with neither name attached. Len put the invoice down and said he was sorry.

There were details Hex didn't know about Len, either. He knew which size torch Len took to work and how long he went on looking for it when he'd lent it to somebody else. He knew that once a year Len got three days together and went up north to see his sister. He knew Len's daughter rang the garage when she wanted to be sure he'd taken his lunch, and that one afternoon Len had sat with a stranger an hour past closing because she didn't think she was ready to drive home.

They finished their tea. There was only one job left to do by then. Len went to close the shutter and Hex started one last look through the garage for things left where somebody could trip over them the next morning.

Back upstairs, he opened the drawer. He hadn't seen the name printed under the photo in months, because he'd always held it by the same end when he took it out. That evening he looked at the name. He wondered if there was any place in the town where Ninja could talk to somebody about Elise without having to decide what she was ready to tell them.

The letter still said that he'd been there. He wrote a second line. Your sister tried to do something when it would have been easier to go home.

He read it back. The sentence was small enough to hold. He left it there, folded the sheet and put it under the photograph. He had arrived in Bellwether with one bag. Over the last year he'd acquired three boxes of tools and a pair of winter boots that fit, and found where to buy bread from the baker when the shop shut early. If he had been in a hurry, most of it could have stayed. He had made a habit of being ready to leave. He was finding out what he wanted more than that.

He turned off the light and put his keys down on the chair. The one for the garage was unmistakable in the dark. Len had bent a bit of wire through the hole, so he'd always find it first. In the morning it would be right where he had put it.

* * *

Five years after he brought his bag upstairs, Hex closed the garage one evening and put Len's mug where he liked to find it. He'd spent the last half hour persuading a bolt out of a wheel. It had taken three minutes less than he thought it would. He stepped out with a little time left over and stood under the eaves to enjoy it.

Across the market, a traveller came out of the hotel and turned down her collar just long enough to check which way the light was coming from. There was rain between them and a shopkeeper bringing in his last crate. Hex took the shorter way to the Crooked Lantern, past the baker's window. By the time he went in, the traveller was still standing outside the hotel.

Ada put a beer on the counter for him. “Len was in earlier. He says if I hear the garage radio playing after seven, I'm to call and get you to go home.”

“He still thinks I live somewhere else?”

“He's holding out for somewhere farther away.”

Hex laughed, took his beer and crossed to his usual table. He still chose it because he could see the door, and still found his hand reaching for the back of the same chair each time he got there. This time he took the chair on the other side. He could see a strip of the bar in the glass of the cupboard. If Ada moved two steps to the left, he could see her shoulder. That would do.

He took his first sip. The paper beermat stuck to the glass and then let go. He had just thought he might manage an entire drink like this when there was a shove at the door.

The door always stuck in the rain. Nobody who lived in Bellwether had to push it twice.

“Oh, come on,” a woman said.

The latch gave. Hex set down his glass, and turned his head.
