# Elsewhere: The Hidden Valley
## Episode 2 — The Name on the Garage

Previously on Elsewhere, Ninja lost her sister Elise, and spent five years learning to get through the days without her. Now she's taking a few days away. On her first evening in Bellwether, she pushed open the door of the Crooked Lantern.

Five years earlier, Hex was lying on the wet floor of a garage trying to persuade a bolt out of a wheel.

His uncle used to say that when a bolt refused to turn, you should put the wrench down and work out what was holding it before you took it personally. Hex always took it personally for a minute first. It was good to get that out of the way.

The van belonged to a woman who sold flowers from a stall near the station. The other garage had told her she'd have to leave it until Monday. Monday would have been three days too late. Hex had heard her on the phone while he was waiting for a cup of tea at the café behind the warehouse. He'd asked what kind of van it was and then spent an evening in the florist's brother's garage with a light balanced on the edge of a bucket.

He turned the wrench. The bolt came loose.

When he finished, the florist asked him how much he wanted. He gave her a number. She gave him that, plus a bunch of flowers she'd taken home from the stall because one stem had broken.

“There's nothing wrong with the others,” she said.

At home he cut them down to fit in a jug. They brightened up a kitchen he had barely used that month. He had enough money to pay his rent now. He also knew that at some point the man who'd provided most of it would ring him and tell him where to be. Sometimes the flowers on the kitchen counter and the phone next to them felt like things belonging to two different people.

He'd got into the work through the warehouse. One evening he'd been asked to drive a car from one place to another, drop off the keys and come back by train. It paid four times what he'd made that week. The second time, he'd asked what was in the boot. The man paying him said, “A good reason to keep your radio on.”

Hex ought to have put his keys down then. Instead, he started getting good at working out which questions a man expected him to ask and which ones it was safer to save for later. The crew liked him best when he looked as if nothing could surprise him. Eventually he'd learned to wear that expression into the supermarket. Once a little girl said hello to him in the cereal aisle and he had to think quite hard about how to make his face answer her.

On the way into the café one Tuesday, he held the door for a woman whose paper carrier bag had split. A lemon got halfway to the road before Hex caught it under his shoe.

“Very dignified,” she said.

“You've got three more in the gutter.”

“Then this one gets to tell the others what happened.”

He brought the bag in and the woman behind the counter found an empty bread wrapper to put the lemons in. Hex took his tea to his usual table. The woman he'd helped took a seat two tables away. He would see her at the café again. He remembered her because she thanked him for catching the lemon and then paid for his toast without mentioning it.

A week later, somebody from the crew handed him a set of car keys and a note. He was to pick up a clerk named Elise and drive her to a building across town. When he pulled in at the address, the woman with the lemons opened the passenger door.

“Fancy seeing you here,” she said.

He asked her if she knew whom she was working for.

“I have a fairly good idea.”

“What do you do?”

“Paperwork. What do you do?”

“At the moment, I'm driving you across town.”

“Excellent. Let me know if that changes.”

By the end of the drive, he knew she had a younger sister who kept lending her things and claiming she would never do it again. He knew because Elise had been planning to buy an umbrella on the way back, and when he asked why she wasn't carrying one, she'd said she was already on her sister's third.

“We get on very well, as long as it's dry,” she said.

He liked her. She made him feel like somebody offering a lift. It had been a long time since anybody from work had done that.

* * *

A few weeks later, a man named Carver put a folder on Hex's café table and sat down across from him. Hex knew Carver's car by the smell of the peppermint sweets he ate when he was waiting for someone to answer a question.

“The club by the river,” Carver said. “Thursday, eight thirty. You’re picking up an envelope.”

“From who?”

“Elise. You met her.”

Hex turned the pages. There was an address he already knew and instructions on where to sit. At the back, a photograph had been clipped upside down.

He turned it around. A woman was standing outside a corner shop laughing at something beyond the frame. There was a bag under her arm and a bus moving out of view behind her. He could make out a streak of rain near the edge where the lens had caught it. Her name was typed beneath the image. Ninja. The instruction below said to call immediately if she showed up.

“Elise's sister?” Hex asked.

Carver nodded.

“She's bringing something as well?”

“No. She's got her own job. Thursday nights she gets off at eight.”

Hex looked up. “How long have we been watching her?”

Carver slid the folder back across the table until the photograph was out of sight. “I’m asking you to tell me if you see her. You don't have to do anything else.”

Carver's phone rang. He went out the back, leaving the folder on the table. Hex waited until the door shut and took the photograph off its clip. All the pages had been numbered, except the photograph. He put it in the inside pocket of his coat and closed the folder.

At the time, Hex told himself he'd give it to Elise. She would know how to talk to her sister about it. Then he thought about Thursday and what it meant to put a photograph of somebody's sister in the briefing for a job involving Elise. He couldn't get Ninja's expression out of his head. She looked completely at ease. The man who'd taken it knew what time she went home.

Hex left his tea unfinished and went back to his flat. He took the picture out and stood in the kitchen while a little water ran from the jug he'd just refilled. He could hear it dripping off the counter onto the floor. He put a towel under it and kept thinking.

* * *

The afternoon before the handoff, Hex phoned Elise from his flat. She picked up after two rings. In the background he could hear a till opening and someone asking if she wanted a bag.

“I heard you're meeting me tomorrow,” he said.

“I'm hoping that's the extent of my evening.”

“Is it normal for them to make this much fuss over their accounts?”

“No. And I don't think it's the accounts they're worried about.”

He asked her what she meant. She waited to answer until she was outside the shop. He heard a loose wheel rattling on what might have been a shopping trolley.

She said she'd been keeping short notes about jobs where the name of the person signing off didn't match the name of the person giving the order. At first it was because she was tired of being called in to correct the records after hours. Then a woman came to ask what had happened to her brother. Elise remembered entering the cost of a job that matched the day he'd disappeared. There were two numbers against it. One was for a car. She'd spent all evening trying to work out what the other one bought.

“Maybe don't tell me that on the phone,” Hex said.

“I've been trying to figure out where I can say it out loud.”

He could have told her about the photograph straight away. He thought about it while she took a few steps along the pavement. He didn't want to send her running to her sister's flat if somebody was watching to see where she'd go. He wished afterwards that he'd said exactly that and let her decide.

“Do you need to go anywhere before the club tomorrow?” he asked.

“Why?”

“I can give you a lift.”

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, “Let's see how it goes. I can get myself to the club.”

“Alright.”

“And if you get there first, there's a kettle. Let it run for a bit before you fill it. The pipes make the water taste like a spoon.”

He laughed. When he called the number again the following evening, it rang out.

* * *

The social club hadn't opened to members for months. On Thursday evening the river was high and there was a light on upstairs.

Elise was waiting in a room with a stack of folded tables against one wall. Hex had thought she'd be angry. The first thing he noticed was how carefully she put down her cup, as if she had to watch her own hand to get it to the table.

“Did you bring the envelope?” he asked.

“I brought it this morning. I changed my mind about leaving it.”

“Where is it now?”

She looked at the stairs. “Somewhere they've got no reason to go. They rang me after I'd got home and read out my sister's address.”

Hex reached into his coat. He put the photograph face up on the table.

Elise recognised the moment it was taken. “I borrowed her umbrella.”

Hex took the chair on the other side of the table. There was a small clock on the wall behind her. The second hand stopped each time it got to the top. He could hear it trying to go past.

“I can take you home,” he said. “We can talk to her together.”

Elise put a palm flat on the photograph. For a moment it looked like a picture she'd brought herself. Then she pushed it toward him again.

“If I go home, somebody will be waiting there. That's what they wanted me to understand when they called.”

“Somewhere else, then. You can choose.”

She looked at him for a long second. Then she got to her feet.

They heard the front door below. An instant later two sets of footsteps started up the stairs. Hex put the photograph back in his coat. He'd seen a second door on the landing coming in and had been trying to work out whether it led down to the river when Carver came into the room.

“How was the drive?” Carver asked.

“Fine.”

“The car’s still blocking the road. Move it round the back.”

“The road's empty.”

“Then it won't take you long.”

The man who'd come up behind him stayed on the landing. He'd been there the first time Hex had been told to stop asking questions. Hex looked at Elise. She had both hands on the back of the chair. He'd had a few seconds to do something different. He might spend the next five years working through the things he could have done with them.

He walked out. He went down the first flight and got partway down the second. A chair scraped hard across the floor above him.

He turned around.

On the landing, he hit the light switch. It took a second for the light to come on. The room door had closed and there was nobody standing on the landing anymore. He said Elise's name through the door and heard nothing. By the time he got it open, he was too late to help her. Two other people had died that evening. There were sounds downstairs again before he could do anything for any of them.

Hex left through the door he'd noticed before. It opened onto a passage with a window at the end. By the time he got outside, the rain was light enough that he could see the building in the water.

He stood where he was for a few seconds. In the other pocket of his coat were keys to a flat with a jug of flowers on the kitchen counter. He thought about going back for something and couldn't name what it was. Then he heard the front door and started walking.

* * *

By morning Hex was at a railway station. A television over the coffee counter showed part of the road by the club. The news said the police were contacting families. Hex walked toward the coffee counter and then back to the bench. He repeated that twice before he understood that he was waiting for the screen to tell him what to do.

Carver rang. Hex let the first two calls go. On the third he answered.

“Did you speak to anybody?” Carver asked.

“Who?”

“The police.”

The train to the city came in. Hex watched its windows pass.

“Come home,” Carver said. He said it with the easy certainty of a man who knew exactly which door Hex had to walk through to get home.

“My phone's going to die,” Hex said.

He ended the call, switched the phone off and bought a train ticket for a place where he'd never taken a job. He went through there and changed again. By the time he stepped down in Bellwether, his shoulders hurt from keeping them still.

The bar was open. Hex sat near the counter and asked the woman pouring somebody else's drink if she knew of any rooms. She had one upstairs that she used for visiting relatives when she could persuade them to visit. She asked his name. He'd spent half an hour on the train coming up with one simple enough to say while somebody was waiting.

“Elias Ward,” he said.

“Ada.” She handed over a key on a blue cord. “You'll hear the ferry early. Go back to sleep if it wakes you.”

He slept until noon. By then Ada had news for him. There hadn't been a morning ferry in eleven years.

“People lie about worse things,” she said, setting a cup of tea in front of him.

Hex thought he might be able to stay there a few days.

* * *

The garage sat at the far end of the market, where the road dropped away toward the station. There was a little white car parked outside it the first time Hex walked past. The man beside it had the bonnet up and a line of rain down his back. Hex stopped and asked what was wrong.

The man said, “It was fine ten minutes ago.”

Hex looked inside. “And the ten minutes before that?”

“It was also fine.”

“Brilliant. Has it always made that noise in the rain?”

There was a pause. Then the man said, “How much of your day do you have?”

“Most of it.”

His name was Len. He'd run the garage for thirty-two years and had been trying to convince his daughter he could do it for another thirty-two. By the end of the afternoon Hex had helped find what was wrong with the white car and moved a stack of winter tyres away from the leaking roof. Len asked him to come back on Monday.

On his first paid morning Hex found out the garage radio only played if there was something heavy on the plug. He asked whether Len would mind if he repaired it. Len said people had tried. Hex fixed it while his tea was brewing, and the first thing it played was the middle of a very long song about a dog. Len said he preferred it broken. Hex caught him humming the song the next day.

Work at the garage was the first thing in Bellwether that started and finished at reliable times. Customers wanted to know whether they could take their cars home. Len had no objection to Hex asking why a job needed doing. If somebody arrived just as they were closing, they looked at each other and settled it with a shrug.

After a few months Hex could hear a bike coming up outside and tell which postman was on the hill. He could sometimes get through lunch without checking the glass over the garage door for a face from the city. When one did look familiar, he'd set his tea down and keep his hands busy until the customer came close enough to turn into somebody else.

One January, two years after Hex arrived, a woman called the garage as they were turning the lights off. Her car had stopped beyond the last farm. Len was already reaching for the heavy coat he wore when the temperature dropped. Hex offered to go.

Out on the lane there was a thin hard layer of snow over the road. The woman's daughter was waiting in the back of the car with an open schoolbag and a single orange glove. Hex asked her to hold a torch while he took a look. She spent a thoughtful minute trying to keep the beam still, then asked him what he'd do if he had an orange glove for one hand and a blue one for the other.

“Wear them both,” he said.

“That's what I said.”

The mother laughed. Hex did what he could by the roadside and drove with them back to the garage. Len had left the lights on. While they waited for the woman's brother to come and get them, they made tea and hung the orange glove over the radiator with a clothes peg. The woman left with the keys to Len's spare car. Her daughter collected her glove and thanked Hex with a little salute.

On his way home he thought about the first garage and the woman with the flowers. So much had changed that he'd started counting his life from the day he took the train. It felt good to recognise a part of himself that had already been there.

Len eventually cut back to mornings. He left Hex in charge after lunch. In the winter, they kept a spare pair of boots near the heater for whoever decided to cross the market without looking at the sky first. People in Bellwether began sending friends to the garage and telling them to ask for Elias. Each time it happened, Hex answered a little sooner.

In the drawer upstairs at the bar, under a key that no longer opened anything, there was still a photograph. He took it out once to write a letter to Ninja. It began with 'I was there.' He put the pen down after that. He could imagine it dropping through the slot at her home. He could imagine her having to read it standing in the hall without even knowing who had sent it. He folded the sheet and kept it with the photo.

Some nights, long after he stopped reaching for the drawer every day, he could see her face clearly enough without opening it. He could almost hear the laugh. That was the part his memory always added, because the paper gave him no way of knowing what it actually sounded like.

* * *

Five years after he brought his bag upstairs, Hex closed the garage one evening and put Len's mug where he liked to find it. He'd spent the last half hour persuading a bolt out of a wheel. It had taken three minutes less than he thought it would. He stepped out with a little time left over and stood under the eaves to enjoy it.

Across the market, a traveller came out of the hotel and turned down her collar just long enough to check which way the light was coming from. There was rain between them and a shopkeeper bringing in his last crate. Hex took the shorter way to the Crooked Lantern, past the baker's window. By the time he went in, the traveller was still standing outside the hotel.

Ada put a beer on the counter for him. “Len was in earlier. He says if I hear the garage radio playing after seven, I'm to call and get you to go home.”

“He still thinks I live somewhere else?”

“He's holding out for somewhere farther away.”

Hex laughed, took his beer and crossed to his usual table. He still chose it because he could see the door, and still found his hand reaching for the back of the same chair each time he got there. This time he took the chair on the other side. He could see a strip of the bar in the glass of the cupboard. If Ada moved two steps to the left, he could see her shoulder. That would do.

He took his first sip. The paper beermat stuck to the glass and then let go. He had just thought he might manage an entire drink like this when there was a shove at the door.

The door always stuck in the rain. Nobody who lived in Bellwether had to push it twice.

“Oh, come on,” a woman said.

The latch gave. Hex set down his glass, and turned his head.
