# Elsewhere — Episode 12: A Place at the Table

### Previously on Elsewhere

Iona gave the officer a record of a meeting Pike held after the fire. It confirmed he had sent two versions of the warehouse register to different offices. Iona also told Ninja about the room above the print shop. Elise had planned a taller chair for Ninja and a third chair for anyone who needed a place to talk.

Ninja told Iona she was still angry. Then she asked her to show her where the afternoon light fell.

### Chapter One — A Seat on Borrowed Time

Ada’s noticeboard said that the community hall needed extra chairs on Thursday. Someone had drawn a little chair in the corner, which Ninja thought was sensible for anyone who got that far and still didn’t know what a chair was.

She had offered to help lay the tables for the soup lunch. By the time she arrived, Hex had already been asked to fix a folding chair, arrange the other chairs, and find out where all the teaspoons had gone. He had done the first two. The teaspoons plainly wished to be left alone.

“There are six in the windowsill.”

He counted them. “Why are they there?”

“I never said we’d find out why.”

The hall had tall, modern windows and old hooks with places for names beneath them. Ada remembered when they’d still been sharp enough to tear a coat. She tested one with her finger and seemed mildly disappointed that nobody was in danger.

Ninja put a jug of water on each table. On the last one she set three glasses because she had three left in her hands. She’d only just put the last glass down when a man in a raincoat walked through the open door.

He stood just inside and asked whether anyone could lend him a phone charger.

Ada said she had one in her bag. She asked him what socket it took.

The man smiled. He was going to say something else when Hex came out from behind the nearest table. Ninja watched the small fact of recognition pass between them. The man looked at Hex’s work jacket, then at the box of teaspoons, and finally at her.

“Martin Pike,” he said, as though she had asked.

Years later, when Hex thought about Pike walking into the village hall, he would remember the charger before he remembered the face. Pike used to keep an extra jacket at work in case he had to send one to be cleaned. The cleaner sometimes pinned the receipts to the lining. When they did, Pike would stand by the desks and ask people about their weekends until one of them offered to cut the receipts off for him. At that age Hex had thought all men with actual offices knew how to borrow a pair of scissors without speaking to three people.

Ada opened her bag. It had four pockets and she checked them from left to right, as if there were some order in which a charger could reasonably turn up. Behind the big zip she found a screwdriver she hadn’t been able to find when the lock in the ladies’ toilet came loose. She put it on the counter as a small reminder that the day had contained a piece of good news.

Hex picked up the screwdriver and asked if she wanted him to see to the lock. Ada said she’d need the door open for the first few minutes after lunch started, because the sign that said the lock was broken had gone missing. They decided he would leave it on the counter so she could find him later if she had a moment. Then Ada went back to the bag.

Ninja watched Hex put the screwdriver down. He’d said he could fix it as though he still expected to have an afternoon after they talked to Pike. She knew what it could take to remember that. Last night at Briar House, when she put her phone on the table beside the bed, she’d found herself moving it closer to her pillow three times. She had finally plugged it in on the other side of the room. If it rang, it would be a thing she had to get up to deal with.

A woman approached Ninja from the other end of the table. She had a heavy coat over her arm and wanted to know whether people always sat in the same places. Her mother had come once and somebody had taken her jacket off the back of a seat because the chair belonged to a man called Tommy. The woman was eighty. She’d left without sitting down.

“You can sit where you like today. If Tommy comes in and says that’s his chair, send him over to me.”

The woman put the coat on a chair by the window and went to get her mother. There was a little pile of fabric where it sagged through the back. Ninja tucked it up so it wouldn’t drag on the floor. When she looked round, someone had opened the kitchen hatch and she could smell the first pot of soup.

Ada found the charger. It was tied in a neat loop with the kind of wire people throw away when they take bread out of the bag. She gave Hex a quick look before she unfastened it. He nodded at the table near the door. There was room for Pike there without anyone having to lift somebody else’s coat.

### Chapter Two — While It Charges

Ada had already got the charger out. She gave it to the man and pointed him to a socket by the door. Hex said nothing until Pike had connected his phone and set it on the table with the screen down.

“Who told you I’d be here?”

“No one. I came to the workshop and your friend said you’d gone to help with lunch.”

Ninja looked at Hex. She hadn’t known he could go so still while remaining quite plainly in the room with her. He let the teaspoon box down on the nearest table.

“I’d like five minutes with you,” Pike said to him. “There’s a question I could answer before anyone else gets dragged into it.”

“You can tell the officer.”

Pike looked at her again. “I suppose you have a number for her.”

“I do. Do you want me to give it to you?”

He took a chair by the charger, took his raincoat off, and folded it on his knees. Under it he was wearing a plain grey jumper. He looked like someone who had arrived early on purpose because he thought the room would belong to him until everybody else got there.

“I knew your sister,” he said.

Ninja picked up the jug. She remembered the sound it made when she put it back on the table. She would not have thought a jug could be set down so carefully that it made a small noise anyway.

“I know you did.”

The phone screen lit up. Pike turned it over just far enough to check the battery. He said he had a note Elise had written to the office. According to him, the note would explain why she was at the warehouse. He spoke about it as though they were discussing a letter sent to a landlord.

“Did she write it before Iona let her in?” Hex asked.

Pike glanced up at him. “That’s why I wanted five minutes.”

“You can give the note to the officer too.”

Hex pulled out a chair for himself and sat down. He didn’t sit alongside Pike. He sat at the opposite end of the table with his hands where she could see them.

“She’s right,” he said. “You can.”

Outside, a delivery van pulled up and reversed much more neatly than anyone had expected. The driver came round the back, took out two blue crates, and brought the first to the door. She asked who’d signed for the produce last week because there was one crate that hadn’t come back. Ninja said she had only come to help with lunch. She took the crate from the driver while Ada went to check the other deliveries.

Inside it were several loaves in tall paper bags. Ninja took one bag out and smelled the heel of the loaf through the fold. She put it with the others, then returned to the table. Pike had moved his phone about six inches closer to his elbow. The charger pulled at the plug. He moved the phone back again when it buzzed.

He told her, quite softly, that he remembered Elise’s handwriting. She liked to underline the words she thought someone might miss. He was fairly certain the person who received the note would have remembered it. Ninja could have asked him who that person was. She could feel the question behind her teeth.

“You can tell the officer what you remember about her handwriting too.”

Pike picked up a paper napkin and pulled it straight against the side of his leg. “I wasn’t sure if it was what you wanted,” he said.

She didn’t need to choose a thing he could offer her. There were things she wanted that even the nicest version of this morning couldn’t supply. She asked him to move his glass while she finished setting the table. He did it without complaint. She heard the metal tip of the charger touch the leg of the chair as he shifted.

The old lady from the door came through with her daughter. She had very bright shoes and a wooden stick which she hooked on the back of her chosen chair. Her daughter went to fetch a napkin and the old lady looked Pike up and down, then asked if he was expecting anyone in the seat opposite. Pike said he only needed the room beside the socket. The old lady nodded, content to leave him there. She moved her stick a little closer to her own chair.

Hex was at the serving end of the room, picking up a serving spoon for Ada. He’d put his jacket on the back of the quiet chair to mark the chair as repaired, and a woman had already asked him if he was selling the jacket. He set the serving spoon down, poured a glass of water, and brought it over to Ninja.

“That’s yours,” he said, when she reached as if she might have to put it somewhere.

“Could you stay in the room until I go outside?”

“Of course.”

She took a sip of water. Hex went back to help Ada with the ladle. She watched him walk away and knew exactly where to look for him when she needed to. On the other side of the door she could hear the woman from the van stack the empty crates. Then somebody started to carry soup out to the tables, and the room grew warmer all at once.

### Chapter Three — One Bowl

Ninja had expected to feel much better as soon as she decided what to say. She found that making a decision and being able to breathe easily were separate things. There were two windows on the other side of the room, and she tried looking at one without checking the other.

Pike took the charger out when the screen showed enough battery. He wrapped the cable loosely and put it on the table.

“Do you still have the note?” Hex asked.

“I have a copy.”

“And where’s the original?”

“I couldn’t tell you that.”

There were a dozen possible questions in the room then. Ninja could think of three before she remembered she didn’t have to ask any of them herself. Ada had begun filling soup bowls at the counter. She set one on the closest table, then looked at Pike in case he wanted to wait.

He asked how much the lunch cost.

“Whatever you put in the tin,” Ada said.

Pike looked at Ninja. “I won’t stay if it makes things difficult.”

“The soup’s Ada’s. I’m going outside. If you have something to say about Elise, I’ve told you who to say it to.”

She went out to the step. The sky had cleared enough to make a line of light under the hall door. She rang the officer from the path and described the meeting. The officer asked if she wanted to wait outside while she talked to Pike by phone. Ninja said yes. She also asked if someone would write down that there might be an original note. She didn’t want that question to disappear because she’d been too busy making sure another person had heard her say no.

By the time the call ended, Hex was outside beside her. A bowl in each hand. He had remembered to bring two spoons.

“Where did you find the rest?”

“Somebody put them in a vase.”

“Well, of course they did. Where else would you put teaspoons?”

She took her bowl and followed him to the low wall outside the hall. The soup was hot enough to take time over. She sat with her hands round the bowl and let the heat be one simple thing she could pay attention to.

Ada brought out the second pot and set it down beside them on the low wall. There was a tea towel over the lid. She said if either of them wanted more, they could serve themselves until she came back with the bowls. She’d found a jar of pepper with the lid on crooked and no one knew who to blame for putting it away.

Ninja lifted her own bowl and held it against the sleeve of her coat. When she moved her thumb she found a little warm patch of skin. She told Hex she couldn’t tell whether she wanted Pike to finish talking to the officer quickly or to have to keep talking until he answered everything.

Hex wiped a spot of soup from the side of his finger. He said that if Pike were still talking now, he’d be able to hear what the officer asked. Then he’d have to work out what he was going to say, knowing his answer would go somewhere he couldn’t reach when they hung up. Years ago Hex would have been very pleased just to see him try that.

He remembered being asked to come into Pike’s office after closing time. Pike had a drawer that used to catch on his desk when it was very full. He’d pull at it with both hands and talk into the little gap at the same time. On the day Hex was thinking of, nothing in the drawer belonged to Hex, but it took almost ten minutes of watching before he realised he was allowed to ask what the meeting was about.

“Why did you stay?”

Hex thought about it. He said that after a certain time at the Directorate, everyone still in the building was just a person you went home behind. You heard them scraping the ice off their cars. You said good night to them in the bus shelter. It was easier to imagine staying for those people than for any office programme or printed promise. On that evening, he’d known he had to be at work the next day and wanted tomorrow Hex to have an easier trip in. He’d forgotten to ask what the man at the desk wanted tomorrow Hex to be carrying.

Ninja held the folded tea towel away from the pot for a second. The steam came out into the air and disappeared almost at once.

“Tomorrow Hex seems quite capable of telling me if something’s going badly. D’you think he will?”

“I think he could give it a go.”

She shifted on the wall until their shoulders touched. Hex looked down and brushed a crumb from the top of his shoe with his other foot. She watched him succeed on the third try. The woman in the bright shoes came out to tell someone she hadn’t yet got cold and didn’t know what all the fuss was about. She went back inside ten seconds later. When the door opened again, it was for Ada, coming for her pot.

Hex and Ninja stayed on the wall as the soup cooled. It was thick enough that keeping it on the spoon became an interesting problem. He asked if she wanted bread. She said she’d had enough bread yesterday for a person who was trying to make one decision about the future.

“You decided about a roll,” he said.

“It took me all day.”

They breathed out at almost the same time. Hex had his jacket zipped up only as far as the top of the bowl. On the other side of the path a row of little flowers had come up inside the edge of the tarmac. Ninja recognised their leaves. They were the ones Ada had called volunteers, which she had taken to be a polite way to say weeds.

She asked what he thought Pike had expected from him. Hex said he probably thought they would both want to remember a time when they knew what to say to each other. Once you could make somebody remember that feeling, it was easier to get them to agree to a small thing. There had usually been another small thing on the other side of the first one.

Ninja told him she’d heard him say she was right. He looked down at his spoon.

“I did think you were right.”

“I know. I liked hearing it at the table.”

They ate a little longer. There was a difference between having someone agree with you because they were on your side, and having them help you carry the side you had chosen. Ninja was beginning to know that difference when she heard it.

After a while they went inside with the bowls. There was one spoon left on the wall. It took them both until they reached the door to remember it. Hex went back for it and held it up like a prize. He told Ada he thought they were making progress with the missing cutlery. She counted the spoons in his hand and said she supposed it was possible.

Ada had put a second tin by the hatch. This one was for a woman who’d put her bus fare in the soup tin by mistake. People were passing it down the table and then talking over each other to explain what it was. By the time it got to Hex, he had been told to take out exactly seventy pence, put in enough for another bowl, and find out where the bus stopped since they’d moved the roadworks.

“I’ll come with you and show you,” he told the woman, who had got her coat on already. She laughed and told him she wasn't leaving until she'd had the pudding. Hex put her bus fare beside her pudding spoon. Then he came back to the hatch and asked Ninja if she’d seen Ada’s screwdriver. She pointed it out. Somebody had almost put it away with the bread knives.

Ada had taken the sign off the ladies’ door. When Hex opened the door a little, the catch slid down inside the plate and rattled. He asked Ninja to hold the door steady. She thought he meant near the handle, but he moved her hand about a foot higher so the hinges would be easier to see. He was going to see whether it would close with the plate turned round.

The conversation around the tables went on carrying past them. A man wanted to know if the cup in front of him was his. A woman said it was if he could remember how much tea he’d put in it. In the draft from the hallway, Ninja heard one bit of a different conversation. Someone asked who the man in the raincoat was. Someone else said he was friends with the man who mended the chairs. She felt herself tense at the exact instant Hex pressed the screwdriver against the plate. He stopped turning it.

“It was loose before I got there,” he said. “I might need another screw.”

She loosened her grip on the door. When he took the old screw out she saw it was a little shorter than her smallest fingernail. He put it on the windowsill. She told him what she’d heard someone say. He stood away from the door and listened to the people at the tables for a minute. Then he said next time somebody asked he could tell them himself. He used to work with Pike. They’d known each other well enough to say what they needed to say, and still managed to say very little of it.

Ninja looked towards the hatch. Ada was smiling at something from the far end of the room, out of their sight. She thought it would probably be someone asking if they’d be allowed seconds. Ada had been letting people take leftovers home one container at a time because there were barely any clean lids left. There might be even fewer by closing.

Hex found a screw that reached as far as the timber. When he put it in, the latch held in place. He opened the door twice to see if anyone would have to lean on it to get out. Then he asked Ninja to try it on her own. She stepped inside, closed the door, and turned the lock. It was almost silent. From inside she could see that someone had taken the broken lock sign and put it face down on the windowsill. She brought it out with her so Ada could use the other side.

They went back to the tables. There was enough pudding left for half a bowl each. Hex went to fetch a clean spoon and came back with six, which was just how many there had been on the windowsill before lunch. Ninja said she thought they could stop counting now. Hex said it was a sensible time to stop. He was fairly sure another one had already gone missing.

### Chapter Four — The Hall Afterward

Pike left without speaking to them again. He set the borrowed charger beside Ada’s bag. When Ada went to count the money in the tin, she found a folded note beneath the coins. It paid for several bowls of soup and explained nothing at all. She went back to counting.

Ninja and Hex stayed to put the tables away. At the end there was one chair nobody wanted to fold because the frame made a noise like a goose. Hex tipped it over and looked at the hinge. Ninja held the seat still while he moved a small pipe pin into place with his thumb. Afterward it folded without a sound.

Rose came in to collect a tray. She noticed the chair by the wall and said she was fairly sure it had come from the upstairs room years ago. Elise had tried it, folded it flat, and returned it to the hall because it didn’t fold flat enough. Rose looked at Hex. “How flat does it go now?”

He showed her. Rose said that was good enough and set it by the front door to take home.

After they put the tables away, Ada went through the hall with a dustpan. No one ever needed a dustpan in the middle of a table, she said. You needed one under the chair where somebody had spent a whole lunch pretending to eat the crust off their bread.

In the kitchenette, Ninja found the charger by Ada’s handbag. Pike had wound it in the same loop Ada used and left the wire from the bread bag beside it. She could remember him saying Elise’s name in a voice that sounded practised. She tried to remember what else he’d sounded like. He’d had to clear his throat before asking about the lunch. It was possible that he really had been hungry. She hoped he’d given the officer something she could use. There was space for both things by the time she picked up the charger.

Ada came in with the dustpan. She poked her fingers into her bag for the pocket where the charger went. Then she told Ninja about agreeing, years ago, to look after a box of belongings for a woman from the village. For a long time no one had known the woman meant to come back for it. They kept wanting to ask Ada what was in it. She knew it contained one saucepan because the handle came through a hole in the side. People would bring her that detail later as if she’d told them the box contained a whole kitchen.

“Did she come back?”

“She rang me about the saucepan. By then she wanted the rest to go to the charity shop. She’d got herself an address where she didn’t need most of it.”

Ada wanted to know if Ninja would like to be at Briar House when the officer called again. She asked as though she could just as easily have offered to keep a bowl of soup in the fridge. Ninja thought for a little while. Then she said she wanted a key to the front door. She might want to walk somewhere after the call and come in again on her own.

Ada reached for the hook under the kitchen noticeboard. A small key was hanging under a handwritten label that said big cupboard. She said that was wrong. The cupboard had never had a lock. It was the key to Briar House, and she’d moved it onto the hall board the morning after Ninja first stayed there.

“You’ve been waiting for me to ask?”

“I knew you’d tell me when you wanted to be able to come back by yourself.”

Ninja put the key on a ring with the other ones so she would be able to find it with one hand. Through the open kitchen door she could see Rose carrying the folding chair past the tall windows. Hex had one hand on the bottom of it to help her get round the frame. The street lights were coming on outside. Ninja put her coat on and went to hold the door for them.

Ninja’s phone buzzed as they came out of the hall. The officer had reached Pike. He had said the note was written at the station, which gave her something to ask about. More important, the archive had found a stock envelope with Elise’s reference number and three unsigned receipts tucked inside. They were preparing a copy of each.

She stopped under the street light to read the message through again. The last line asked whether she could come to the print shop tomorrow. It said there was something on the back of one receipt that was easier to show.

“I thought I’d be waiting weeks.”

“You might still be waiting weeks for the rest.”

“I’m taking tomorrow for now.”

She put the phone away. Hex held the hall door while she went back to check that they’d turned the light out. There was a wide space where the tables had been. Tomorrow there would be somebody else’s chairs and somebody else’s lunch.

When she came outside again, he had waited on the step. The night felt ordinary enough that she could hear a television through an upstairs window. She was grateful for the sound. It suggested somebody had reached the part of their evening where nothing else was required of them.
