# Elsewhere — Episode 6: The Door at Bellwether Station

### Previously on Elsewhere

Someone followed Ninja to the old bridge and sent Hex a photograph. Hex told her about the message. He also told her his real name, and that he was there the night her sister Elise died. It was the first time anyone had told Ninja that the fire was planned.

Ninja asked him to leave her the photograph and give her a little time. She wrote down the question she wanted to ask him next. What was Elise bringing her that Sunday?

At the workshop, an envelope arrived. Inside was a photograph of Elise at a station, with a woman in a cream coat at the edge of the frame. Hex knew the woman. He knew the handwriting on the back, too.

Ask me who opened the door.

### Chapter One — A Place to Wait

The first train through Bellwether ran at six forty-two. Hex knew this because he had heard it from his kitchen every morning for five years, with the exception of one Tuesday when it was diverted and he spent half an hour wondering what he had forgotten.

That morning he was awake before it. He watched the light come through the gap in the curtains and waited for the sound.

It had been a long time since he had said Iona Vale’s name. He had practised it once the previous evening, standing alone in the cottage with the photograph on the counter. The name sounded so ordinary in his own kitchen that he tried it again.

They had worked together for four years. Iona used to arrive early to a meeting so she could work out which radiator was on and sit beside it. She made a habit of remembering what people owed one another, from expense claims to favours nobody had asked for out loud. She had been the person Hex called after the fire because he thought that whatever else happened, she would answer him.

At seven eleven his phone lit up.

The message gave him a time. Nine ten. It gave him a place. Bellwether Station, inside the old waiting room. And it offered one assurance.

I’ll be on the public side.

He almost wrote back to ask what that was supposed to mean. Then he pictured the waiting room as he had last seen it, with a broken vending machine, windows onto the platform and a corridor at the back leading to the service door. A person could enter and leave in full view of the ticket kiosk. Iona had chosen it so that he could walk away.

Or so that she could.

Hex made coffee. He poured himself a second cup when he discovered the first sitting untouched by the sink. At twenty past seven he wrote a message to Ninja. He looked at it for a long time before sending it.

A former colleague wants to meet at the station. I think she knows something about Elise. Nine ten. I’ll tell you what she says if you want me to.

He put the phone down. There was no quick reply, and he found himself grateful for the quiet. It gave him enough time to think of all the reasons why Ninja might decide that she wanted a day of Bellwether with a book and a walk and no more visitors at her door.

He hoped she would have a day like that again. He was starting to understand it couldn’t come from his keeping things out of sight.

Hex went to the workshop to find Tomas before going to the station. The door was unlocked. Inside, someone had moved the generator far enough from the wall to leave a strip of floor much cleaner than the rest.

Tomas was drinking tea at the workbench, turning a nut into an interesting place to balance another nut. Hex told him who had sent the envelope.

“Iona,” Tomas said. “Did you ever speak to me about her?”

“No. I don’t think I ever said her name in Bellwether.”

Tomas lifted the nut on top and put it down again. “Is that what you want to do now?”

Hex looked at the window. The florist was standing in the street with a bunch of green leaves resting against one shoulder while she tried to get her key into the lock. He watched until the door opened.

“I want someone to know where I’m going,” he said. “In case either of us comes out too scared to say what happened.”

Tomas took his phone out of his pocket. The case was cracked along the top, and had been for as long as Hex had known him. He wrote down the time of the meeting, then set the phone over by his tea.

“Should I come?”

“I don’t think so. Iona picked a place that’s easy to leave. If you come along, I might spend the whole time trying to tell from your face whether what I’m hearing is as bad as I think.”

“That sounds like a good reason to go by yourself.” Tomas picked up the tea. “Obviously I’m available if you want to do a less sensible thing.”

Hex laid his hand briefly on the workbench. It was cool through the tips of his fingers.

“At nine forty,” he said, “if I haven’t rung.”

“I’ll ring you.”

Hex nodded. A little of the tension went out of him. Tomas had made a plan he could rely on in fewer words than it had taken to ask.

On the way to the station, he bought coffee because he had a hand free and didn’t know what to do with it. The woman behind the bakery counter put it in a red paper cup, and gave him one of the little cardboard sleeves because the cup would be too hot to hold. He took the cup and remembered to thank her. There was something comforting about a stranger making sure he didn’t burn his hand.

### Chapter Two — The Coat on the Peg

Ninja woke to rain ticking against the windowsill. For a few seconds she could smell toast and hear Ada in the kitchen and had the luxury of being nobody in particular.

Then she saw Hex’s message.

She read it twice. She took the folded scrap with the robin on it from her cardigan pocket and set it by the phone. There was only one question inside its pencilled square.

Downstairs, Ada was standing in front of the coat pegs, trying to persuade a scarf to stay on its hook.

“You’re up early.”

“Someone who knew Elise is meeting Hex at the station.”

Ada gave up on the scarf and handed it to her instead.

“Then you can borrow this.”

“I haven’t even said I’m going.”

“You looked for your coat while you were telling me.”

Ninja took the scarf. It was a deep yellow wool and smelled faintly of the hall cupboard.

“I want to hear it from her, if she’s willing to tell me. I don’t want to spend the morning waiting for someone else to decide what I’m allowed to hear.”

“Have something to eat first,” Ada said. “You’re allowed to start there.”

The toast had gone cold, so Ada put it back under the grill. A little marmalade had run into the gutter of one slice. Ninja watched the toast emerge darker, and this time she ate it while it was hot.

She told Hex she was coming. Then she told the officer from yesterday where she was going. The officer asked whether she was meeting in public, and Ninja said she was. After the call she sent Ada the time and location even though they were both still in the same room.

Ada read the message through her glasses and sent back a single yellow heart. Ninja sat there grinning at her phone for a second. It had always seemed impressive to her that an older person could make a tiny digital picture feel exactly like having a blanket put round your shoulders.

At the gate, she pulled the borrowed scarf up over her chin and set off. There were two routes to the station. One went over the old bridge. The other passed the bakery and the chemist, where there were people opening shutters and getting on with their day. She took the second one.

### Chapter Three — Nine Ten

Bellwether Station had a ticket machine that apologised in three languages and a clock above the canopy that ran four minutes fast. Ninja arrived by the clock at nine nine. Hex was on a bench outside the waiting room, beside a red paper cup he appeared to have forgotten.

He stood when he saw her.

“Did you sleep?”

“Long enough to find out none of it was a dream.”

He nodded. Then he moved his cup to give her the option of sitting down. She sat. The bench was damp enough to come through her jeans immediately.

“This bench is horrible.”

“It always is. Even in July.”

They got up again and stood next to the timetable. Hex nearly smiled. So did she.

“What’s her name?”

“Iona Vale.”

“Did Elise trust her?”

“I thought she did. I don’t know when that stopped.”

Ninja tucked her hands beneath her elbows. They watched an actual passenger consult the ticket machine, give it a look of mild disappointment, and go over to ask the kiosk attendant for help. Ninja envied the simplicity of his trouble.

At nine ten by her phone, a woman came under the canopy from the footbridge. The coat was still cream, though it was modern and good against the rain. She was older than Ninja had expected. The hair at her temples had gone silver and she was holding a folded newspaper to keep her glasses dry.

Iona looked first at Hex, then at Ninja.

“Are you Elise’s sister?”

“Yes.”

“I’m glad you came. I’d imagined having to do this through him.” She took a breath. “That would have been easier for me.”

“Do you mind if I stay by the window?”

“Please do. You can see the entrance from there.”

They went in. There were no tables, just three rows of blue seats and a stand of old leaflets on places to visit. Ninja took the seat nearest the window. Hex remained on his feet until Iona sat, then picked the chair farthest from the back corridor.

The woman placed the folded newspaper on her lap. She went to speak. Then she noticed a pair of small hands pressed against the waiting-room glass from the outside. A child was looking through to find out whether there were sweets in the vending machine.

The machine was dark. The child ran off at once.

Iona watched until the glimpse of a bright red coat had gone.

“Your sister used to carry apricots,” she said.

Ninja looked at Hex. He was looking down at his hands.

“I know. He told me.”

Iona kept her eyes on the empty patch of window where the little red coat had been.

“Elise used to meet me here sometimes,” she said. “The first time, she was late and she walked into the waiting room looking so pleased with herself that I knew she’d have some ridiculous excuse.”

Hex looked at Ninja. She was listening very still.

“She said she’d started out early. Then she went to put a letter through the wrong door and had to spend fifteen minutes convincing a man called Gerald that she didn’t live there. Gerald thought she’d moved in next door. He offered her a chair and a bowl of pears and she was too polite to leave before she’d had one.”

A passenger had come into the room and sat on the far side. He took out a book. Iona waited until he had found his place before she lowered her voice and continued.

“The letter was for Ruth. Elise left it with me instead so I could give it to her. I spent the whole trip home with it in my bag. That was before I understood why she wouldn’t put certain things in the office post.”

Hex remembered the sort tray by the office door. Once a week a woman gathered everything in it and took it to a room whose handle turned only from the inside. He had watched her do this a hundred times without asking what was in the bags.

A train outside brought a quick change of air through the window frame. Ninja pulled the borrowed scarf nearer to her mouth. Iona folded her newspaper more tightly across her knees. Hex had seen her do the same thing with a sheaf of office papers years ago, hanging on to the straightness of an edge while she chose her words.

“I thought there would always be time to ask Elise what she was doing,” Iona said. “She used to tell me if I wanted a straight answer I should ask a straight question. Usually right after I’d spent five minutes coming around the side of one.”

Ninja looked from her to Hex, who lowered his eyes. On the nearer seat, the small envelope was still hidden inside the newspaper. Ninja hadn’t seen it yet. She was fixing one corner of the scarf, undoing a fold that kept catching against her face.

Then she sat back, and in the few seconds before she spoke, nobody in the room tried to rescue her from the silence.

### Chapter Four — What She Took With Her

Iona opened the newspaper. Between the pages was an envelope much smaller than the one sent to the workshop. Ninja could see her sister’s initials on it, blue ink, with one letter slightly leaning onto the other.

“Before we start…”

Ninja stopped, because she had wanted to say that differently.

“I have a question I need answered first. Elise was going to bring me something on Sunday. She never said what it was. Did she tell you?”

The woman touched the corner of the envelope. It was the kind of movement that made Ninja think she had asked the right person.

“She was going to bring you a recording. She made it while she was going through an incident log the Directorate wanted corrected.”

“Corrected,” Hex repeated.

“That was the word they used. Elise copied part of the log to a small digital recorder and spoke over it to explain what the figures meant. A record that told you, in her own voice, which names had been removed and why they mattered.”

“Why was she bringing that to me?”

“She trusted three people to recognise her voice. One was me. One was a clerk called Ruth Avery, who’d helped her make the copies. And the third was you.” Iona drew the envelope closer. “She was going to ask if she could leave it with you. I think she wanted to ask you at the kitchen table with a pot of tea. That was how she did things when they mattered.”

Ninja could see the cake tin again. She could see the cupboard with one sticky hinge, and Elise sitting at the table without taking off her coat because she was going to be in a hurry to say what she had come to say. The kitchen was exactly where she had imagined the visit for five years. She had never imagined what would have been in Elise’s hands.

“Do you have the recorder?”

“I have something that will help you find it.”

Iona slid the envelope across the empty seat between them. Ninja took it. Inside was a small nickel key with a round red tag. Written on the tag in black ink was the number twelve.

“It came from the station,” Iona said. “An old left-luggage locker that hasn’t been there for years. Elise liked to keep familiar objects. She kept using that key long after the locker was taken out.”

“What does it open now?”

“A metal cashbox. She left it with Ruth.”

The morning train to the coast pulled in outside the window. The same passenger who’d fought with the machine stood by the doors, holding a cup of tea. Ninja watched him wait for the people getting off. The whole platform seemed to breathe in and out as the doors opened.

She turned the key over in her fingers. There was a little speck of dried blue paint on one of the grooves. What had Elise painted, she wondered, that had left that small a mark? If her sister had been there, she would have asked her. She had to keep getting used to having questions that stayed with her.

“I want to hear her. I can’t remember what she sounded like when she really needed me to pay attention.”

“I think you’ll remember when you hear it,” Iona said.

### Chapter Five — The Door

Hex had let the conversation belong to Ninja until then. He had thought he could wait as long as she needed. Then he saw her glance at him and understood that she was leaving him an opening.

“Who opened the door?” he asked.

Iona settled her hands on her knees. Then she moved them, as if there were no good place to put them.

“I opened it.”

The train outside began to move. Through the window the seats seemed to slide past, bright rectangles with people already looking at the rest of their day.

“The photograph came from here,” Iona said. “Elise was waiting for me. I told her there was a clerk at the warehouse who could help her leave the city, along with the records. There was no clerk coming that night.”

Hex sat down.

“Who told you to send her there?”

“Pike. He said she was in danger and we had to move her before morning. We had used the same door on the south side before. It looked like a fire exit. Opening it from the outside took a service key. I let Elise in and then I went back to the car.”

“Did you know there would be a fire?”

“No. I knew that some part of it felt wrong. I asked Pike about it, heard the answer I needed, and left her there.” Iona turned toward Ninja. “I didn’t go back when I should have.”

Ninja pressed the little red tag into her palm. Hex wanted to ask Iona why she had kept the photograph, and why she had let five years go by when a family was still waiting for answers. He also wanted to ask those questions of himself.

“Who was Pike?”

“The man who signed off on the operation,” Hex said. “Martin Pike. He’s still there.”

Iona nodded. “Ruth vanished from her old address three weeks ago. I went to find her because someone had asked about Bellwether. They had an address for Hex and a name for you. I recognised the name. I couldn’t find Ruth, and the person who’d come asking knew she had kept a box for Elise.”

“Were you the one who took the photo at the bridge?”

“No. I was across the river that morning, though. I saw the car. I got a look at the driver. I can give that to the police if you have a number.”

Ninja reached for her phone. She thought of the afternoon in Ada’s kitchen, how strange it had felt to write down an incident number while there was still toast on the counter. She found the number and showed Iona. Iona read it slowly and repeated it back.

No one spoke for a little while.

Then Hex asked, “Why didn’t you pick up?”

Iona looked at him then.

“Because Pike was in the car,” she said, “and he already knew it was you calling.”

Hex put his hands flat on his knees. The fear he’d spent half his adult life learning to hide had never felt as mundane as it did now, in a blue plastic chair at a station that still apologised when a train was late.

For a little while after telling Hex about the unanswered call, Iona watched a loose thread on the knee of her trousers. There was no reason she needed to look at it so closely.

“Pike said you were already on your way out of the city,” she told him. “That you’d understand why I couldn’t talk.”

“What did you believe?”

“I believed you were ringing me from a place you wished you were out of.”

The answer landed slowly. He had made the call from the edge of a kerb, in the dark, with a fire engine idling close enough for the engine sound to drown out half the dial tone. There had been a slip of wet paper under his boot. He’d seen a shoe print in it and wondered, irrationally, whether it was his.

He kept losing parts of that evening in his memory. The parts that stayed were usually small, things nobody would have put in an account of what happened. The call mattered more than most. Some nights he had imagined what Iona would say if he could ask her about it. None of the things he’d prepared to say now seemed like sentences that belonged in a room with three people in it.

“What did you do when he got out of the car?” he asked instead.

“I waited till he’d gone, then I went back to the warehouse. There was a cordon. A fire officer told me she couldn’t let me through.” Iona put her hands together. “After that I went home. I wrote down what I’d done and what I remembered. The statement’s in my bag. I’ve had an updated copy in there for three weeks.”

Hex looked at the bag. It had a long thin strap that kept sliding off the seat. Iona caught it each time.

“Do you know why you went back?” he asked.

“Because I wanted her to walk out and tell me I worried too much.” She gave him a small, miserable smile. “She used to tell me that all the time.”

Ninja was looking at the bag too. It seemed to Hex that they had spent a great deal of the morning looking at small objects. A scarf, a newspaper, a little tin-coloured key. Suddenly he was glad the important things had somewhere to go apart from their faces.

“I’d like the police to have it when you call,” he said.

Iona brought her bag closer to her feet. She nodded.

### Chapter Six — Across the Bridge

A freight train came through without stopping. Iona waited for it to pass before she stood.

“I’m going to the station kiosk,” she said. “The woman behind the counter said I can use her phone. I’ll make the call there.”

“If you find Ruth before we do, tell her I have the key. Let her decide whether she wants to see me.”

“I’ll tell her.”

Iona carried the newspaper with her. She had folded it back into its original shape, with the corners exactly aligned. Ninja watched her go through the door. Through the glass she saw Iona ask the attendant for a phone. The attendant pointed toward the open end of the counter and pushed a handset toward her.

Ninja put on Ada’s scarf.

“Walk with me?”

Hex stood.

The rain had eased to the kind you could decide was mist if you didn’t want to put your hood up. They took the long way out of the station and passed the platform fence. Somebody had tied a child’s single blue glove to the top of a railing. It hung out over the pavement, as if it were making a patient and optimistic attempt to hail a bus.

At the bridge Ninja stopped. She knew the road the car had taken and the spot where the photograph had been taken. What she also knew, quite suddenly, was that the first time she stood here, before she understood what she’d been looking at, she’d wanted to tell Elise about a man who made a joke without smiling.

She wanted to tell Elise so many things.

“If we find it, I’m going to listen to her before we do anything else.”

“Okay.”

“And if she sounds all business and does that thing where she tells you something twice because she assumes you never listen the first time, I’d like to listen to that twice too.”

Hex turned his head toward the river. Ninja heard him breathe out slowly. When he looked back, she saw that he was smiling.

“I remember that,” he said.

Together they walked toward the bakery. They were almost through the door when Ninja’s phone rang. It was Ada.

“There’s a woman at Briar House,” Ada said. “She says her name is Ruth. She’s got a cardboard box she won’t put down.”

Ninja looked at Hex. He stepped aside so she could get back out the door.

“Put the kettle on.”

Then she went home.
