# Elsewhere — Episode 8: The Green Paperclip

### Previously on Elsewhere

At Briar House, Ninja heard Elise’s voice for the first time in five years. Elise had recorded the names of people the Directorate had erased from its records. Ruth described a second copy of the original paperwork in a station repairs ledger, marked with a green paperclip. The police checked the register and found that Martin Pike had collected the ledger that morning.

Ninja remembered the words her sister had actually used. The second copy.

### Chapter One — What a Name Can Do

By breakfast the rain had softened to a sound you could almost confuse with the kettle. Ninja sat with the recorder in front of her and the little red key beside it. She had tried sleeping with the recorder in a drawer. Twice she had reached to make sure the drawer was still shut. Eventually she put it on the bedside table, where the small white scratches around its play button were just visible from the pillow.

Ada came in carrying a clean tea towel over her shoulder. She took one look at the table and set a piece of toast beside the key.

“People think better with toast,” she said.

“Is that an official Bellwether policy?”

“Unwritten. We did have a vote.”

Ninja buttered the toast to the edges. The corner broke off and fell butter-side down. She turned it over and ate it anyway.

Ruth came down just before nine, in the cardigan Ada had lent her. They played the end of the recording again. Elise talked about the green paperclip. Then there was a faint scrape, a click, and the room returned to its ordinary size.

“Did she tell you where she kept the first copy?”

Ruth took a moment with that. “She said she’d left it with someone who would know what a name could do. I thought she meant someone from work. They all used to talk like that when they didn’t trust the walls.”

“Elise used to say things like that even when she trusted the walls. Then she’d get cross if I asked her what she meant before she was done.”

Ruth smiled. She started to fold a paper napkin and stopped halfway.

“There was a card in her coat pocket,” she said. “The corner stuck out when she hung the coat up by my desk. It had a blue swallow on it. She used the back to write down my new phone number.”

Ninja could see the coat, the hanging loop that had always stretched, and Elise emptying the pockets in front of her to prove she had lost nothing important. A card with a blue bird sounded like exactly the thing Elise would remember after forgetting her wallet.

“Could it have been a calling card?”

“It might have been. Thick cream paper. The bird had one of its wings down over a line, like it was about to dive.”

It was a small thing to be told. For the first time since yesterday, Ninja wanted to put her shoes on and go outside.

Before she left, Ninja spread a clean sheet of paper on the table and tried to draw the card from Ruth’s description. The first bird came out with a tail like a teaspoon. Ruth said it had been infinitely more elegant than that. The second bird looked distinctly pleased with itself, which Ruth allowed was closer.

Ada took her glasses off to hold the sheet nearer her face. She recognised nothing about the bird, but remembered that the print shop kept the cards from most local businesses in a drawer. There had once been an entire card for somebody who did nothing but sharpen scissors. You got your scissors back in an envelope with a small comb printed on the flap, which Ada had considered overdoing it.

When Ninja finished the drawing, Ruth showed her where to put the line under the bird. A small level line with a break near the tip of the left wing. Ninja made the break with the side of the eraser. It was the first thing she had deliberately removed from a page all morning. She looked at it with unreasonable satisfaction.

Ruth sat a little longer with the sheet before giving it back. She said Elise used to lay all her receipts out on the counter at work and turn the ones she wanted to deal with so they pointed toward her. The others lay crosswise. Sometimes Ruth came back from lunch and found a biscuit balanced on a particularly uninviting receipt. She had always supposed it was an attempt to make the paperwork happier.

Ninja listened with her pencil held still. There were pictures of Elise that she had looked at so often she could have placed a finger on every fold in the paper. This was an entirely new picture and she didn’t have to hold it in her hand.

She turned the sketch round so it pointed toward her. Then she folded it and stood up.

### Chapter Two — A Bird in Blue Ink

Hex was at the garage door when Ninja found him. Tomas had left little magnets round the inside of the door frame so he could put his keys down without ever knowing where they were. Hex was taking them off, one by one, to count them.

“Are they multiplying?”

“Only when he’s had the drill out.”

She showed him the drawing Ruth had made of the swallow. It had a deep fork in its tail and looked slightly put out. Hex tapped the corner of the paper.

“The print shop used to put a bird on its cards. Down near the river.”

“Used to?”

“Rose retired. Her daughter runs it now. They still do a card for the florist who thinks email is rude.”

They called the officer to pass on what Ruth remembered. She asked them to tell her if they found anyone who’d known Elise, and to leave any papers where they were until she could arrange to collect them. Ninja wrote down what they’d agreed and folded the paper before putting it away.

Outside, the river carried a branch through the shallows with quite a lot of ceremony. Bits of willow brushed the water. Hex walked on the side nearest the road, and Ninja stopped at the crossing to wait for an ordinary white van.

“You know I noticed you doing that yesterday on the bridge.”

He looked where she’d been looking, at the space between himself and the road.

“I know.”

“I appreciate the thought. Sometimes I’d rather know what you’re looking at.”

“All right.”

The van passed. They crossed, and this time Hex told her he was looking at an empty parking space because it was where the hatchback had stopped the previous evening. Today there were tyre marks and a washed-out sweet wrapper. He left it there. It was helpful, Ninja discovered, to be told the small plain thing he knew and allowed to walk on beside him.

At the far end of the crossing a man with a small terrier had got the lead caught in the spokes of a bicycle. He stood quite still while Hex helped him unwind it. The dog was also still, though it gave each of them a look that suggested it knew a superior method. When the lead came free the man thanked Hex by calling him Elias.

Ninja heard it and watched Hex answer as easily as he ever had. It hadn’t occurred to her that hearing someone else use it might help. He was a person who fixed the latches and knew which bin the broken kettle went in. Whatever else he’d done, people had learnt to use that name while asking for very ordinary favours.

They walked on. A row of benches faced the river. The first needed painting. The second had been thoroughly painted in a colour only a person with a great deal of conviction would call green. On the third, someone had put a woollen scarf to dry, folded over the back in the way you might put your own scarf somewhere you could see it from the house.

Hex told her the river used to freeze along the edge. One winter the level dropped while ice was caught in the reeds, and they were left with a transparent shelf above the water. He’d walked down there every morning that week. The ice looked different each day. The quiet of an ordinary place changing without needing him had helped him decide he could stay.

Ninja thought about that while a bicycle came slowly past them. It was the first winter he’d been in Bellwether. He would have arrived around the time she stopped going to the old café where Elise had always asked for the table by the radiator. While he was learning a new route he could walk, she was trying one that didn’t take her past the same window.

At the next corner Hex held back to let her choose which way to reach the shop. She chose the shorter one. There was somebody putting fresh rolls in a window and the whole street smelled briefly of bread.

### Chapter Three — The Print Shop

The print shop had a bell above the door and a small heater somewhere behind the counter. The room smelled of brown paper. Inside the window, where they could be picked up free by anyone who wanted one, were a pair of new bus timetables and a leaflet about a lost tortoise.

A woman in a green jumper came out of the back room carrying a fresh bundle of cream card.

“That’s a swallow, technically,” she said, after looking at the sketch. “My mother would say it was definitely a swallow, because she carved the stamp. She still thinks we should be making our own ink.”

“Her name’s Rose?”

“Rose Fenwick. I’m Mara.” She set the paper down. “If you’re looking for her, she comes in Tuesdays and Thursdays to tell me what I’ve put in the wrong drawer.”

Ninja explained why they had come. She didn’t know how to introduce Elise to a stranger. In the end she said her sister had been in Bellwether five years ago and might have brought something in to be copied.

Mara’s hands moved from the edge of the bundle.

“Was it Elise Varen?”

“Yes.”

“She used to come in with a scarf around her hair when it was raining. Never an umbrella. She said she’d only have to remember where she’d put it.” Mara looked between them once. “I was still in school. I remember her because she brought me a book about drawing hands. I’d shown her something I’d done, and that was the only part I’d left out.”

Ninja wondered how many people had a memory like that. Something neither of them had considered important enough to recount when there was all the time in the world.

Mara turned the sign on the door so it said she would be back at half past. Then she made a call to Rose. She spoke for perhaps a minute, with long stretches of listening. Once she looked at Ninja and said her name, softly enough that it sounded temporary.

When she put the phone down she asked whether they could wait a few minutes.

“We’re quite good at that now.”

Mara pulled a stool out with her foot. “You can do it sitting down here.”

### Chapter Four — Things People Leave

Rose arrived from upstairs in slippers, which suggested that retirement for her meant moving most of the business an inconvenient distance from the tea tray. She listened to Ninja, then found a cardboard folder beneath a pile of print samples. Its outside was faded where somebody used to put a ruler across it.

“Elise brought a packet in,” Rose said. “She asked me to make two plain copies. I kept the first one until she could collect it. Later she asked me to take it to the person whose dates were on the first page.”

“Do you remember her name?”

Rose looked at Hex, then at Mara, then back to Ninja. “She goes by Alice Reed now. She earned the right to tell that story herself. I can leave her a message if you want.”

“Tell her who I am. And that she doesn’t have to meet me to send anything to the police.”

Rose nodded. She put the folder away and brought out a smaller notebook. In the notebook, beneath a page advertising the winter raffle, was a cream envelope with the blue bird printed on the back.

“Elise left you this. Did you know?”

Ninja shook her head.

“It came back in the post,” Rose said. “She gave me an address and the house number was wrong. I put it away until I could ask her what she wanted me to do.”

The front said Ninja Varen. There was an old stamp on the corner and a tired little postmark. Ninja read both of her names more than once. That was her own name in her sister’s handwriting, and she already knew what it would look like when it moved onto the next line.

She opened the envelope. The paper inside had been folded in thirds. The first sentence asked her to buy milk on Saturday because Elise expected to forget.

“She remembers milk in a letter she writes on a Thursday. And then on Saturday she asks me whether we ever discussed it.”

Hex smiled. “I can imagine.”

There was a little more. On Sunday, Elise said, she would have something to leave with Ninja, for a few days if she could. She had a cake tin too. She hadn’t forgotten which of them it belonged to. She was simply planning to argue her case after lunch.

Ninja refolded the letter and tucked it into the envelope. The paper was warm from her fingers. She could feel a laugh and a cry very close together, and she gave them a minute to decide who was going first.

Rose had already moved the print samples back into place. She left the old notebook out because Ninja had asked if she could see the entry for the day the envelope came back.

The address was written carefully. The number was wrong by only one digit. Ninja could remember the flat with that number. A retired teacher had lived there, and late in the afternoon she used to put a saucer of water out for birds that passed through the yard. The saucer was white and very shallow. It used to have a blue flower in the centre.

For a moment Ninja wondered what would have happened if the woman had recognised the surname. She could have put the letter through the right door. Ninja would have opened it in her own kitchen while Elise’s cup was still on a hook. She’d have had five years to read the same sentence about the milk.

Rose moved the cup nearer to her.

“I wish I’d checked it,” she said.

Ninja traced her name on the envelope without touching the wet corner of the table. She could think of ways straight through a lot of things now, when there was no longer anywhere to go. It was possible that opening the letter then would have helped her. What mattered immediately was that Rose had kept it through five years of other jobs, through birthdays and floods and changes of address, without ever seeing a reason to throw it away.

Mara showed her a biscuit tin full of cards and labels her mother had made. Some were plainly mistakes. One said spring fair in two sizes of lettering with the dot left off the little i. Mara kept that one because she’d drawn the flowers on it when she was eleven. Rose said the flowers were the reason they used it anyway.

Ninja put the envelope against the inside of her coat. She imagined Elise watching a person set type by the window, asking how a card was made, and remembering to take one with her. It had taken five years and a sketch of a bird to reach this room. For a moment she let herself be pleased that she had reached it.

### Chapter Five — Across the Counter

Mara came back with three more chairs and two cups because the third was halfway through a soak. After a while Rose told them something about the person she had delivered the packet to. Alice had been a records worker at a small council office. For years she’d taken calls from people who didn’t know which department to ask for. She would sometimes stay late if somebody sounded too embarrassed to ring a second time.

Ninja could hear Elise's first name on the recording. It fit the story perfectly. Alice was one of the people who had been given an ending on a form while she was still alive and answering the phone.

“Do you know what was in the packet?” Hex asked.

“Dates. Copies of instructions. A carbon on very thin paper that you had to put over white to make out.” Rose put her empty cup down. “My eyes were better then. I still had to read some of it twice.”

The bell rang outside. Mara had left the door unlocked. A woman stepped inside with three shopping bags and said she had come for wedding place cards. Mara asked her which wedding. The woman said her own, and they all found this a perfectly satisfactory answer until Mara asked for a name.

For that little interval there was nothing else to do except move their feet to let the woman past.

Ninja took the letter back out. She looked at the milk again. Later she would want to read it until the paper wore thin. For now she just needed to know there was an actual envelope where her sister’s voice had managed to wait.

Rose called Alice from a phone at the back of the shop. She didn’t leave a message. The call must have connected, because after a minute she said, “I’ll tell her,” and reached for a notepad.

When Rose came back, she pulled up her chair more carefully.

“Alice has your name,” she told Ninja. “She wants to meet tomorrow. The old ferry shelter, at eleven. You can bring him along, if he wants to come.”

Hex put his cup down. “Did she ask for me?”

“Actually, yes. She said she recognised your name too.”

### Chapter Six — The Walk Back

The air outside had changed. The clouds were still thick, but a narrow stretch of brightness lay along the water. Someone had bought flowers and was trying to fit them in a bicycle basket without losing the smaller stems.

Ninja phoned the officer. Rose would pass on Alice’s details with her permission, she said. They could take it from there. Ninja had to say the name twice because a van went by the first time. She was glad she had said it aloud to somebody who had a place to put it.

Hex waited by the railings.

“Do you think you ever met her?”

“I don’t know. I’ve been going over it in my head. There were so many forms.”

“A person saw your name and thought you might be worth asking. You’re allowed to have one thought about that that doesn’t turn into a bad one.”

He looked at her. The water went past. It was easy to pretend there was something important to watch in it, and for a while they both did.

At the end of the street Ninja stopped to buy milk. She had no obligation to bring milk home. She wanted to bring some anyway. There was a line at the till, and Hex stood with her while she held the cold bottle against her sleeve.

“If we’re late tomorrow, can we agree it was the clock’s fault?”

“Of course. I’ll let the clock know.”

She laughed more easily than she had expected to. By the time they turned toward Briar House, she was carrying the milk in one hand and the envelope in the other. The pressure of the envelope was slight, but she knew at every step where it was.
