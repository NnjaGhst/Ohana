# Elsewhere — Episode 7: The Voice on the Tape

### Previously on Elsewhere

At Bellwether Station, Ninja and Hex met Iona Vale, the woman in the cream coat. Iona admitted she had let Elise into the warehouse on the night of the fire, following orders from a man called Martin Pike. She had left Elise there without knowing the warehouse would burn.

Elise had planned to bring Ninja a recording. A clerk named Ruth Avery had kept it in a locked cashbox, and Iona gave Ninja the key. It was small and silver, with a red plastic tag.

On their way back from the station, Ninja and Hex stopped outside the bakery. That was when Ada rang.

There’s a woman at Briar House. She says her name is Ruth. She’s got a cardboard box she won’t put down.

### Chapter One — One More Cup

The first thing Ninja saw when she opened the gate was a pair of wet trainers through the sitting-room window. The person wearing them had drawn her feet together, exactly on the little square of rug Ada usually reserved for the mending basket. On the pavement outside, someone had stepped in a puddle and left a wet footprint with a diamond pattern in it.

Hex stopped on the gate’s other side.

“Are you coming in?”

“I can wait out here.”

“I know. I’m inviting you inside.”

He shut the gate. He put his hand on the latch, the way he always did, to prevent it slamming.

They found Ada sitting near the window with the woman in the damp trainers. Ruth Avery was short, with straight greying hair clipped back above one ear and the pink mark of a tightly fitting hat still showing across her forehead. She wore a thin blue waterproof. She had her arms around a supermarket carton which appeared to have carried small tins of beans at some point in its history.

Ada had set out five cups, because she’d seen Hex on the path and because nobody in her house had to commit to tea before being given a cup.

Ruth watched Ninja close the sitting-room door.

“You look like her around the eyes,” Ruth said.

“Sometimes I catch it in the mirror. Usually when I’m annoyed about something.”

Ruth smiled. It came slowly, as if her face had almost forgotten how. Then she looked at Hex.

“I remember you,” she said.

“I remember you too.”

He let the answer rest there. Ninja could feel the little hesitation between the two of them. For herself she had decided that, for the next hour, she could do without anybody who expected forgiveness to arrive on a schedule.

“Can I sit here?”

She pointed to the end of the sofa, a little way from Ruth. Ruth nodded twice.

Ada went to fetch the kettle. They listened to the tap run in the kitchen, to the big rumble of a delivery van going past, to the house making the small sounds an old house makes when the weather is changing.

Ruth put the carton down on the rug at last.

“I had this under my desk a week,” she said, “before I understood what was in it.”

“Do you know if it still works?”

“I know the light still comes on.”

For a few seconds that was enough.

Ruth told them she had got off the bus two stops too early. She had lived near Bellwether once, but this was the first time she had used the route since it changed. She hadn’t known whether to get back on the next bus or keep walking with the box tucked under her jacket.

“The driver thought it was a cat,” she said. “I was holding it so tightly. She said she wouldn’t charge for a cat.”

Ada asked whether Ruth would like one of her old cardigans. Ruth said that she was quite warm, and then blinked a few times and asked that maybe the offer could stay open.

Ninja sat quietly while Ruth gathered the next part of the story. The woman had once worked on the public desk at the station. She knew how to send money and bicycles and a pair of false teeth to lost property without anybody leaving the station embarrassed. The Directorate had an office nearby and it had been suggested she apply there once the public desk was cut to two afternoons a week. She had accepted because she had a daughter at college. At first the work had been mostly about getting numbers to match.

Elise arrived one day with a form Ruth had set aside because it looked wrong. She asked Ruth what she thought had happened. It was the first time anybody in the building had asked her what she thought. The next day Ruth put aside another form.

Hex remembered Ruth behind the office counter, collecting everyone’s empty mugs before she went home. He had thought she was simply a person who liked to keep the counter clear. Listening to her now, he could picture her taking a piece of paper from under the edge of the tray, folding it into quarters, carrying it with her into the lift.

“It used to make me feel sick just to walk past the building,” she said. “Later it was worse when I thought I might forget the things I’d seen if I never spoke about them.”

Ruth turned to Ninja. Her coat was drying enough to show the faint print left by the hem of the carton. “Elise said I’d always know where to find her sister. I asked how, and she gave me your birthday and an address with the best fish and chips on the corner. That was what she knew about your road.”

Ninja looked down at the box. Ada stood to fetch the cardigan anyway and brought it back folded over one forearm.

### Chapter Two — A Box in a Box

The supermarket carton contained old newspapers, a clean tea towel, and a grey cashbox with a damaged corner. A previous owner had painted the handle blue, then stopped halfway through. Ninja could see the same colour caught in a groove on her key.

She had carried it in the palm of her hand all the way back from the station. Now she reached into her coat pocket before remembering she was still holding it.

Ruth watched her fit it into the lock.

“Elise told me you’d be the one with the key,” she said.

“Iona gave it to me this morning. Why did she have it?”

“She’d borrowed it to move something for Elise. I couldn’t tell you what. What I can tell you is that when I saw the two of them together later, Elise made Iona promise to pass it on if she couldn’t.”

Ninja turned the key. The lid lifted a few centimetres and caught.

“Slide it sideways first,” Ruth said. “She did that dropping it down a stairwell. The catch has been like it ever since.”

Ninja slid it sideways. Inside was a pocket-sized digital recorder, a spare battery still in its packet, and a coil of wired earphones with a knot she recognised from every other pair of earphones her sister had ever owned.

Her laugh came out too loudly. She eased the wire between her fingers. Without even thinking about it, she had picked up the loose end the way Elise used to, through the middle and around twice. She would have had the whole thing untied in under a minute.

“There’s an old speaker in the kitchen if you need it,” Ada said, bringing in the tea.

“Can I try it with just this?”

“Of course you can.”

Hex was in the chair by the far side of the fireplace. Ruth took her tea and moved over a little, making room even though there was already room. Ninja understood the gesture. Everyone in the room was trying to go on existing without taking up more space than she could bear to give them.

She found the play button, and stopped with her thumb above it.

“If she sounds frightened, I’m going to need a minute.”

“Take as many as you like,” said Ada. Then she sat down herself.

### Chapter Three — The First Hello

There was a hiss of air. A soft bump. Someone cleared her throat.

“Right. If this thing takes batteries, there should be another one in the tin. And if I’ve put the battery in the wrong way round, Ninja, please enjoy being right for the next thirty years.”

Ninja shut her eyes.

She had thought the first word might hurt because it would sound different. What hurt was how exact it was. Elise left a little space after saying Ninja’s name, as if she expected to be interrupted there. She’d had that habit since they were children.

On the recorder, Elise drew a breath.

“I’m going to say the dates first. Then I’m going to tell you why I have them. If you’re listening to this on Sunday, I’ve probably already told you most of it while you were trying to make tea. Please let the tea go cold. I’ll make you another one.”

Ninja paused it.

Someone had moved the little lamp closer to the window. Sun was trying to come through the rain, and the lampshade was a kind of yellow that looked very bright in the grey room. She sat looking at it until she could hear the sounds of the house again.

“She’d have made me another one with the same teabag.”

Ada had her own cup between both hands. “Sounds economical.”

“She’d have called it vintage.”

Ruth laughed, a little surprised by herself. Hex didn’t say anything. Ninja looked over and found that he was watching the floor beneath the corner of the rug, giving her the dignity of having her face to herself. She waited until he looked up.

“You can listen with me. I want you to hear what she says, too.”

He moved a footstool closer and sat where she could see him. Ninja took out the earphones. The recorder’s speaker was tiny, but for some reason it made having Elise there feel almost ordinary. There was a familiar rasp in the high notes, like a phone call from a supermarket car park.

She pressed play again.

“I’ve put down eleven names,” Elise said. “There were twelve originally. Ruth showed me where to look, and there’s a man called Hex who sent back the transport instructions with a question written on them. I don’t know if he knows any of this yet.”

Hex leaned back a little. The chair creaked.

“He asked why the return journey had been taken off the form. I thought one question would be easy for them to explain. What they did was send out a fresh copy of the form with his question rubbed away. That’s when I started looking for all the other questions I couldn’t find anymore.”

Elise began with a date in March. Then she said the name of a woman who had worked in a council office. Ninja couldn’t see her face, so she gave her one from memory. A woman she used to pass on the stairs, always carrying her own lunch. Already the names were taking up places in the room.

Ruth remembered the day the recorder was delivered.

“It came in such a big box,” she said, “that Elise thought somebody had sent her a toaster.”

Ada seemed to think this was a reasonable assumption. She sat a little further back in her chair, the way she did when she’d found a conversation she would be happy to sit in for a while.

“We got rid of the packaging because we didn’t want anybody searching it for the instructions. She’d ordered it on her own card. Later she practised with it in the little stairwell beside the canteen. It sounded like she was interviewing herself for a radio programme that nobody else was going to listen to.”

Ninja had known Elise to do this with a new phone, or a new vacuum cleaner, or a plug whose instructions had been written with too much confidence. She could hear the mildly accusatory tone her sister would have used to interrogate the machine.

Ruth had moved the supermarket carton clear of the rug and was flattening the corner, one crease at a time.

“She asked me to read the first name back to her. So I did. Then she asked how I’d spell it, and I did that too. She thought it might be the only time anybody spelled the woman’s name correctly in a copy of the record.”

On the pad beside the recorder there was still an open space where the name would go. Ninja picked up the pencil, listened for Elise to return to the first date in March, and wrote it down. The first letter she wrote was small and uncertain. She went over it until it looked the way she wanted.

Hex remembered the technician who used to take in the Directorate vans at night, stopping to dam the tea with his gloves to keep it from running over the work orders. He could no longer call the technician to mind without immediately remembering that he had laughed at something Hex said about the wheel trims. Ordinary funny things would keep coming back to him, he realised, very inconveniently, just when he wanted to see the whole stretch of his life in one colour.

Elise’s voice was warm enough to make it clear that she had expected a person like him to be able to change his mind. It was suddenly difficult to listen to her because of that, and equally difficult to look away.

### Chapter Four — The Other Record

They listened right through the dates.

Elise’s voice was steady, a little impatient when she read figures aloud. There were three cases where dates had moved between records and four where the names had been deleted. She made a point of reading both versions slowly enough for somebody to write them down.

Ninja wrote on the paper with the robin at the top. She started with the council worker. Hex found another pencil and put it next to her hand when the first one went blunt.

Then Elise said that she had left the last name out of the recording on purpose.

“She’s alive,” Elise said. “Her paperwork says she isn’t, which is useful to people who think you can stop somebody asking questions by making up an ending for them. She’s agreed to speak when someone can make sure she gets home afterward. Let her choose when. I mean it.”

The recorder clicked. For a moment Ninja thought she had reached the end. Then there was a cheerful little scratch and Elise spoke again.

“I dropped a mug. That was a mug.”

Ada set her cup down. Ruth put a hand up over her mouth. Ninja laughed, but she had to turn her head away.

The voice continued. “There should be a second copy of the uncorrected paperwork in an old repairs ledger. The clerk marked the page with a green paperclip. I remember green because I spent ten minutes looking for one, and now I have thirty-eight. If someone goes looking for it, tell them not to call ahead.”

The recorder clicked off for good.

Ninja sat very still. There was a small crust of dust in one of the buttons. She rubbed it gently away with the edge of her sleeve.

“There’s no goodbye on it.”

“No,” said Ruth.

That hurt in a different way. It took her a little time to understand why. Elise thought she would see her on Sunday. She hadn’t ended the recording like a person saying goodbye because she expected to come into the kitchen, borrowing a pen and demanding the old cake tin, with the future sitting there as plainly as the furniture.

Ninja stood up and went to the window. The world outside was still going on. A woman was unlocking a bicycle across the street. Ninja watched her put her handbag in the basket. For a few seconds she saw how ordinary her sister’s plans had been, right up until the moment somebody else took them away.

“Where’s the ledger now?”

“I can tell you where it was,” Ruth said. “On a shelf in the maintenance office at Bellwether Station.”

Hex looked up.

“The office behind the waiting-room door,” Ruth added. “I put it there myself.”

“Does anyone else know about the green paperclip?”

“Iona knows. I don’t know who she told.”

Ninja turned away from the window.

“Then the police need to know first.”

### Chapter Five — What the Room Could Hold

Ada brought the cordless phone through. Ninja had her incident number written on the pad, underneath a list of people somebody had once gone to a great deal of trouble to erase.

While she made the call, Hex put the cups on a tray. One of them had a spoon inside. He lifted it out gently so it wouldn’t rattle. Ninja watched him because it was an easier thing to look at than the page.

The officer from the previous day was still on duty. At first she asked the same questions again. Then Ninja told her about the second copy, and she stopped reading from whatever screen was in front of her.

They arranged for somebody to come to the house, take a statement from Ruth, and make a copy of the recording. The officer said she would raise the question of the ledger with a colleague. Ninja asked if the colleague could leave the station staff out of it until they knew who had been asking around.

There was a pause. The officer said she could make a note of that. Then she asked if everyone in the house was safe.

Ninja looked at Ada’s feet tucked under her chair. She looked at Ruth sitting with the cardboard carton beside her, both hands empty for the first time that morning. Hex had gone to put the tray down on the sideboard.

“We’re all indoors. Ada has the chain on the door.”

The officer said she’d be there before two.

Ninja lowered the phone to her lap. None of it had made her suddenly feel brave. She was tired, the way she felt after missing a train and spending the next hour trying to make a new journey out of the pieces. But two o’clock was something definite she could move toward.

“I’d like to hear the first bit again before they come.”

Ada reached for the kettle.

“Tell me when you’re ready.”

They didn’t listen at once. Ruth went into the hall to take off her wet shoes, and Hex grabbed a towel in case she wanted to dry them. Ninja watched them both going about these little jobs. Her whole life, she had thought being looked after must be something you could recognise while it was happening. Today it was one person fetching a towel, another coming back to put the kettle where she could reach it.

After a while she sat at the table, moved the recorder close, and pressed the button.

“Right,” said Elise.

Ninja rested a hand on the table and let her sister finish.

The officer arrived carrying a plain zipped folder and a canvas lunch bag with an apple showing through the top. She introduced herself to everyone, including Ada, whose address she already knew. Ada gave her a cup and moved the sewing basket so she could set the folder down without having to balance it on her knee.

She let Ninja show her the box and listened to the beginning of the recording before she opened the folder. When Ninja reached over to turn the recorder off, she didn't ask her to leave it on. They agreed that Ninja could sit beside her while she copied the files. The tiny bar on the screen advanced as slowly as a thing could advance while somebody was watching it.

On her way out, the officer gave Ruth a card and asked where she could contact her that evening. Ruth looked at Ada, and Ada took out her phone as if the answer had long been obvious.

“You can ring my number if she’s resting,” she said.

Ruth folded the card into her cardigan pocket. She said she could go to her daughter’s in a day or two. Ada said that sounded sensible and that she had an extra toothbrush upstairs all the same. Hex opened the front door for the officer and took half a step outside to close it quietly as she left.

The street smelled of rain and bread. He stood there until the officer’s car turned the corner. A delivery driver waved at him from a van. He waved back, and wondered when the village had started to expect that it would find him here.

Inside, he met Ruth on her way upstairs to put on dry socks. She had to turn sideways to pass him in the narrow hall.

“There’s a bit on the recording about your question,” she said. “When she found it, she came to ask me whether I thought we could have a word with you.”

“She didn’t get the chance.”

“I thought you should know she asked.”

Hex stood against the wall while Ruth went up. The cupboard door beside him was slightly open. A coat sleeve stuck out through the gap, and he tucked it in, gently so the catch wouldn’t snap. When he turned around, Ninja was in the kitchen doorway with both hands in her cardigan, waiting for him to come and sit down.

### Chapter Six — Two Slices

After the officer left, Ada put the bread out. Ninja hadn’t eaten since breakfast. She looked at the bread as if it were going to ask her a question she didn’t have the answer to.

Hex took a plate from the cupboard. When she nodded, he put two slices in the toaster for her. He placed the jar of marmalade beside the plate with its lid loose.

“I really wanted to ask you something before, about the warehouse.”

He reached for the next plate and waited.

“Were you the person who told someone I’d need a way to get home?”

“I told a medic. There wasn’t anyone there with you yet.”

“There was a woman in the corridor who had her car keys in her hand the entire time. I thought she just wanted to leave so she could get home to her own family. Then when I stood up, she asked if I knew where I was going. I didn’t. She drove me all the way home.”

“Did she stay?”

“She made a cup of tea. I never knew why she did that.”

Hex put his hand on the back of the empty chair across from hers.

“I wish I’d done more.”

“I do too.”

He took that in. It wasn’t an invitation to defend himself. He pulled out the chair and waited until she looked up before sitting.

The toaster popped. She was halfway through the first slice when Ada brought over the old paperback from the sitting room.

“Page one hundred and ten,” Ada said. “Your horse woman’s finally gone after him.”

“The horse or the man?”

“One hopes the horse. I didn’t read ahead.”

Hex smiled over his tea. Ninja liked seeing it. She could like that and still be angry. She was going to make room for more than one thing at a time, even if it took practice.

The officer rang again just before five. The maintenance office at the station was being refurbished. The old ledgers had been moved to an archive cabinet in a building beside the river. Someone had looked at the register. The repairs ledger with the right dates had gone out that morning.

The person who signed for it had used a real name.

Martin Pike.

Ninja put the phone down. There was a few seconds of quiet in which nobody could have told, looking in the window, whether anybody in the house had moved.

Then she took the recorder back out of the box. She had been afraid to set it down all day. Now she set it beside the marmalade jar and put her hand over it for a moment.

“He can have one copy.”

Hex looked at her.

“She said it was the second copy. We haven’t even asked where the first one is.”

Outside, the rain had stopped. A drop came loose from the end of the gutter and fell into the grass. Then, for the first time all day, there was light enough to see the other side of the river.
