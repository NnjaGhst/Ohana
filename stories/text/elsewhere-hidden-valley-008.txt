Previously on Elsewhere. Callum showed Ninja the letter that came after his mother Beth went away. Della remembered seeing Beth and Elise together at the old bus stop. Ninja made an appointment to speak to someone about the warehouse fire. Callum agreed to bring the letter, and Hex said he would give his account.

It was brighter inside the county offices than it looked from the street. There was a large rubber plant where the light came through the front doors, and the leaves had the faint print of somebody’s fingers where they’d been dusted. Ninja could see that from the chair by the reception desk. She’d been sitting there long enough to see a woman come through with a pile of folders and return with three of them the other way round.

Callum arrived holding the letter flat inside his coat. He’d brought a small bag in case he needed to carry it home. He sat on the chair nearest the window and looked out, although the rubber plant took up so much of it that there wasn't a great deal of road he could see. Ninja asked if he’d had trouble finding the place. He said he walked past it twice because the red door in his memory was on another part of the building. She hadn't seen any red doors. She said she would look next time she went out.

Hex came in a little behind him. He’d said he would meet them here, and Ninja had let him go his own way to get there. He had something in his hand when he walked through the door. It was a pen with the cap held to the barrel by a strip of tape. He said he remembered picking it up twice before he left. He put it down beside him and didn’t ask her if she’d kept the recording on her phone.

A woman named Sian came out and called Ninja’s name. She wore a long cardigan that didn’t quite cover the sleeves of the shirt underneath. She showed them a room off the corridor and asked if they all wanted to sit down together first. There were just enough chairs. Ninja said she would like to say why she’d come, and then Callum could have the room to himself if he wanted it. She saw him nod.

Sian asked Ninja to begin with what she knew, in the order that was easiest for her to tell. Ninja put Elise's blue page on the table, the copy that Mara had watched her make that morning. Then she took the photograph out of her coat. She’d come back for it before they left. She could already feel herself wanting to hold on to it while she spoke. She put it flat and moved her hand away.

“This is me outside the corner shop. I was with my sister Elise. I remember exactly where she was standing. The man who brought me the photograph says he showed it to Elise to make her go somewhere that night. She went to the warehouse and died there. I can tell you what happened to me recently. He can tell you about the warehouse himself.”

Sian looked at the photograph for a moment and then at Ninja, checking she had finished before she asked a question. She wanted to know when Ninja first saw the picture. Ninja said she’d seen it at the hotel, after a man she didn’t know turned up at the door with Hex’s keys. Sian wrote that down. Then she asked about the keys. It was a small relief to talk for a minute about something that had a colour and a piece of wire on it and an exact place where it used to hang.

When Ninja was done, Sian asked whether she could make a copy of the photograph. Ninja said yes. The woman gathered up the edges carefully and went to a machine out in the corridor. Ninja could see her through a panel in the door, standing with one hand on the lid. There was time for anybody in the room to say something. They sat there together until she came back.

Callum told Sian there was a letter from the time his mother went. He asked whether he could read it aloud himself. Sian said he could. He looked at Ninja, and she could tell he had made up his mind about the rest of them staying. Hex and Ninja went back to the corridor. The pen he’d brought was still on the spare chair, and when they got out he realised he’d left it there. He didn't ask to go back for it.

They stood at the window. Through the glass they could see part of the main street and a bus slowing for the crossing. Callum had told Ninja he used to leave the phone on the table when he was waiting for his mother to ring. She wondered how long the copy of Elise's page had been on Mara’s table on the night she dropped it off. Probably only long enough for the kettle to boil. It had been a different sort of waiting. She could hold the silence in the corridor without trying to make the two the same.

Hex took a breath and said he didn’t expect to see her after he went in. She asked whether he meant he expected to be there a long time. He said no. He meant he thought she’d want to go home as soon as she’d done what she came for. She looked out at the crossing another minute. It was a relief to know that was what he was thinking. She had wondered what he would make of her being quiet. Now she could choose whether she wanted to answer.

“I might go home when it’s done. I’m going to be here when you come out. I can tell you that much right now. It’s what I know.”

Callum came out holding the envelope with both hands. He had a little ink on his lower lip, as if he’d put the end of a pen between his teeth. He told Ninja he had written his own phone number down twice. One of the numbers was on the page he could take with him. He’d given it to the same person twice, and she had taken both copies as though it happened every day. Ninja offered him a tissue. He wiped his mouth, looked at the mark on it, and laughed once in surprise.

Hex went in when his name was called. Ninja could see him looking over his shoulder as the door closed. He was looking at the people in the corridor, the ones he'd come in with and the ones who worked there and happened to be walking past, all mixed together. Then the door shut. On the other side he pulled a chair out. Ninja knew the sound from when she'd done the same thing.

Ninja and Callum went out for air. There was a row of pale steps even though the building itself was made of brick. At the top was a patch that was still wet from where somebody had tipped out a plant saucer. Ninja could see the water working its way down the first step. It got there slowly enough that she could have watched without knowing it was moving.

Callum said he’d started with the milk in the refrigerator. He hadn’t planned to do that. He'd planned to tell her about the bus, because that sounded like something a person might ask questions about. Sian asked what had made him sure of the date and he’d told her about the milk. She’d written it down without smiling. He had thought talking about the letter would make him think mainly about the letter. He found he kept remembering what he’d done with the things in the house after he’d read it.

He’d taken down the curtains, himself. He thought his mother would have wanted him to leave them, because the room was cold with them down. They had stopped being able to sell the house without doing something to the front room. He took the curtains with him and could tell Ninja which box they were still in. She understood why he kept both the curtains and the letter, and why he was glad to be holding only one of them as they sat there.

A bus pulled in on the other side of the street. This one did stop. A woman got off with her nails painted a pale yellow and waited beside it for a little boy to go down the last step without her help. Callum watched them get to the other side. Then he said there was one thing Sian had asked him to let himself think about. If Beth had walked away because she chose to, he still wanted to know she was all right. That feeling could stay true even if he found out the letter was written by somebody else.

Ninja said she thought there was room for that. She had no idea what she’d do if anyone said Elise had chosen to go to the warehouse even after she knew it was dangerous. She suspected that was precisely what she had done. It couldn't be used to explain away the person who asked her to come. It could tell Ninja something about the person she was missing. She didn’t know how to put it all together and was glad she hadn’t tried to tell Callum in a way that made it sound finished.

I made enough toast for two people the first morning I went to my aunt’s after Elise died. I did it every morning I stayed, and by the third one I could tell she was eating both pieces because I hadn't learned what to do with the spare. There’s a lot of room between being told someone’s gone and having a day that makes sense.

Callum got a message from the man he worked with. He looked at the time and stood, brushed a little grit off the back of his coat, and said he should go and tell the man he was going to be late. He didn't feel like taking the bus after all. Ninja asked if he'd ring when he got in. He said he could send her a message. She said that was fine. He put his phone away and went down the steps. At the bottom he walked past the place where he could have caught the bus and carried on a little farther without turning round.

When Ninja went inside again there was nobody in the chair Hex had left. She found a different one near enough to see the interview room. She had the feeling that if she sat just right, with a foot against each leg of the chair, she could stay there as long as she liked. After a while she heard somebody in the room say the name he’d given her at the bar. She lowered her eyes. The voice went on for a long time. She couldn't make out the words and was glad she could hear the difference between wanting to know and having the right to hear them as he said them.

Inside the room, Hex had found the pen. Sian showed him where he had signed the end of the first page by mistake, and let him turn it over so he could see that nobody had written anything on the back. He gave the first name he used as a boy, the name people in town knew, and the name Carver and the others called him. He waited while she wrote them down. When she asked about the photograph, he began with Carver setting it on the corner of a desk, close to enough other papers for him to have left it there if he'd chosen.

He said what he told Elise. There was a bit after that where he had to stop. Sian asked if he wanted a glass of water. He did. While she got it, he looked down at the notes he’d brought and saw one line that said he’d tried to get back inside after the door shut. The notes didn’t say how far he’d got to the first time he walked out. He wrote that in the margin while he waited. He could still see the chair stacked at the edge of the light. If he could tell her exactly where he was, perhaps there would be enough room in what he said for somebody else to think about the person who had been left there.

Hex’s thoughts
There’s nobody here who knows how I used to get people to listen to me. If I take another minute, she won’t fill it in for me. It turns out a room can go very quiet and still be waiting for an answer.

When he came out, Ninja was on her feet. She didn't ask him whether they believed him. She asked whether he wanted to go back out to the street before he told her anything. He said yes. They went through the doors together and stood at the top of the steps. The pavement was a different colour where the rain had stopped. It looked as though it mattered which places had had time to dry.

There was a little cafe across from the crossing. A woman turned the sign round in the window while they were standing outside. They must have passed it earlier while the place was serving lunch. Hex asked if they could go there anyway. It had booths against the wall and a handle on the door you couldn’t turn without knowing somebody had just cleaned it.

They sat at the table with a view of the crossing. Ninja ordered toast. She hadn’t thought about it until the woman asked what she wanted. Hex ordered the same thing, and she asked him if he was doing it because of what they said at the bar. He admitted he was. She told him he’d better order a second plate if he was actually hungry. He did, once she stopped smiling just long enough for him to tell how serious she was.

For a little while they listened to the coffee machine. It made a noise like water going down a pipe and then stopped. The woman who ran the cafe brought two mugs and said the plates would come out one at a time because the toaster was being temperamental. Hex asked what sort of temper it was in. The woman said it was clever enough to make a person think it was going to work perfectly until they turned their back.

Ninja remembered another conversation about bread and thought she was going to be all right with hearing him laugh. When he did, he had turned his face toward the window. She only saw part of it. That was enough to bring back how strange and good it had been to hear him start laughing the first time, when she'd known nothing about what that voice had already been used for.

He said Sian had asked him to come back when someone else had read the account. He said he would. He couldn’t tell how soon they expected him or how long it would take his friend at the garage to get used to staying away from work. He thought he ought to get in touch tonight and explain why he'd asked him to take the day off. Ninja said he could do it from there if he wanted. He looked at the phone and said he wanted to say it to the man in person when there was time for both of them to have a reaction.

She thought about the man in the market and the speed with which Hex knew what to say to him. The same man was now leaving the phone in his pocket because there could be two reactions to a piece of news and he wanted to be there for the second one. She wanted to be able to see both those things. The toast arrived while she was thinking about it. She buttered a corner and waited until the butter melted.

“I don’t know what it would look like for me to forgive you. I know I can be glad I met you and still want answers from you. I think we can eat something and let those be the things that are true this afternoon.”

Hex said he thought they could. He moved the jam far enough toward her that she could reach it but left the little wrappers alone. She tore one open and put it on her own plate. Then he began to tell her about the time he left a bag of groceries on the roof of his car and drove off to the second set of lights before remembering it. It had been fairly late, and he had gone back along the route on foot to see if he could find any apples. He found three apples, one orange that wasn't his, and somebody who wanted to know what he thought he was doing. He had made up a better reason than the real one before he remembered that there was nothing wrong with the real one.

When she laughed this time she was looking straight at him. He kept his eyes on her. Then he looked down to see if he’d spilled any coffee. She could see him trying to give her the space to stop before he said anything. It pleased her that she could go on smiling without having to begin another conversation just to keep him there.

Hex’s thoughts
I wanted breakfast because I thought there’d be time to tell her things about myself she’d love. It turns out there’s time. Some of it is going to be quiet because she’ll be deciding what to do with what she heard. I want to come back tomorrow and see if she wants jam again. I don’t want to ask her in a way that makes her say it now.

Two plates came close together near the edge of the table. They had each run out of toast when the woman put a third plate down and said it was the only one that had come up without anybody watching it. Hex asked Ninja whether she wanted half. She did. He arranged the two pieces so she could pick either one. She took the smaller. Halfway through it she thought better of what she'd done and asked if she could have a corner from his. He slid the whole plate across.

She received a message from Callum. He said he was home and the letter was in a different drawer. He'd kept both the old envelope and the new one. She told him about the plant in the lobby and said she'd meet him there any time he needed somebody to sit with. He wrote back that he'd found the red door. It was near the place buses backed up before they started a different route. He’d spent one winter going in and out of it. He said he would show her the next time she was there.

Hex was watching someone outside fold up a table. Ninja could tell from where she was that he was ready to go when she was. She checked the time and decided they could stay until the woman in the cafe needed the cups. They both drank their coffee very slowly after that.

By the time the cups were empty, somebody had put a radio on in the kitchen. They could hear the rise and fall of a voice they didn't recognise. Hex went to pay. He left the pen on the table, and when Ninja picked it up she saw that the cap Sian had found was a slightly different blue. She put it beside his cup instead of reaching over to put it in his pocket.

The woman came back for the plates. She wanted to know if the toast had been all right. Ninja said it had been very good. The woman said that if she came in on Friday she could try something with cinnamon in it, because she was planning to be in trouble with the person who ordered the bread that week. Ninja said she'd have to see where she was on Friday. She meant it. It was the first time she'd thought about where she might be three days from now without the warehouse deciding which room she had to picture.

Hex came back and got his coat from the hook. He offered to find hers. She pointed to the one he was standing beside and he stepped sideways to let her get it herself. It was nearly dry except around the cuffs. He had paid for the whole table before she could tell him she wanted to pay her own. She said he could let her buy a cup of coffee some other time. She heard how his breath changed when she said it. He didn't reach for an answer before he understood what she'd offered.

The cafe woman put the cinnamon toast on a new card beside the till. They stopped to read it on their way out, because it felt fair to give her the benefit of somebody noticing. Then Ninja opened the door. Outside it was cool enough to make her grateful she'd found the right coat. She waited half a second on the pavement, and Hex came through beside her.

They went as far as Hex’s garage before they went anywhere else. He had been meaning to see it from the street. He didn't plan to open on the way past, and she could see that he wanted to be able to say where he'd been that day if the man he worked with asked. It was still light enough to see the blue of the shutters. Under the rain they had looked black in the picture his friend sent when he was wondering if they were going to need another coat of paint.

Hex stopped at the opposite kerb. There was one corner of a blue sign moving a little in the breeze. He looked at the place where it had come loose, then over at the shutter across the doorway, as if he was adding up the work he would find inside when he finally opened it. Ninja saw his hand go to his pocket and come out empty. He didn't have the keys he’d usually have used. She thought she knew exactly what memory had just come back to him.

She asked if he wanted to cross the road. He thought a minute and said he’d rather stay where they were. He could see enough from there. His friend had texted that somebody came up to the door looking for a tyre change and left when he saw the opening hours. Nothing in that message stood out. Hex had spent years getting used to having days nothing stood out in. He explained how it felt to read the words twice and know he was going to keep reading them until he knew what they meant.

They walked past the garage without crossing. At the far end of the road there was a tree growing out of a little square of soil that used to have a fence round it. Ninja could see one bolt sticking out of the pavement. It was light enough to see which part of the bolt would hold if somebody tried to put the fence back. When she turned round, Hex was looking at the same place. She didn't ask him why he'd stopped.

He said he was going to ring his friend. He thought they could meet at a cafe in the morning and talk there. Ninja said that sounded all right. She asked if he wanted her to wait while he made the call. He said if she wanted to. They found a place where the pavement widened, and while he called she took an old receipt out of her pocket and worked out when the last bus ran. He arranged to meet the other man and finished the call without trying to explain it on the pavement.

She put the bus number in her phone. Hex asked if she wanted company to the stop. She considered that for a moment. Earlier in the day she had wanted to be able to leave the room if she had to. Now she wanted to walk the last bit of the road with someone who knew why she’d spent an entire afternoon going as far as she had. That wasn't enough to decide where she’d want him tomorrow. She had no need to decide it before they got to the stop.

“Come as far as the bus with me. When I get home I’ll make sure you know I got there. If you want to tell me how it goes in the morning, ring me after breakfast.”

He said he would. They started down the road. At the next crossing a bicycle came up behind them so quietly that they both turned when the bell went. The person on the bicycle said thank you to both of them. Ninja watched the back light go on and waited for it to disappear around the corner.

At the bottom of the road, the little shelter faced a dark window with a lamp just out of sight behind it. Ninja could see the light by what it did to the edge of the table. She and Hex had stood under another shelter that morning, and he had watched the road while she tried to say enough to her aunt to make what she was doing feel real. Now he stood beside her and looked at the place where somebody had written the number of the bus in felt pen. She could see the rain had lifted a little of the ink and left a mark beneath it.

It would be nine minutes, according to the phone. He asked if she wanted him to go before it came. He had already done what she asked and walked as far as the bus stop with her, she realised. He'd asked because there was a second way for the evening to end, and either would have been an answer to what she'd said. She told him nine minutes was long enough for him to stay. He put one shoulder against the wall and looked down the road again.

Ninja put her hands in her pockets. There was an old ticket folded up in one of them. She couldn’t remember what she'd been on when she got it. She pulled it out, looked at it, and read the date. It was from a trip to see her aunt at the beginning of the month. For whatever reason, she’d kept it through washing the coat and through buying a different coat and deciding the first one was warmer. She thought there might be a photographic record of every trip she had taken that had meant something to her. She wouldn't be able to tell anyone what the important thing had been just by showing them where she’d got off.

She turned the ticket over. There was enough room on the back to write down the number of the bus that would get her home, although nothing to write with. She thought of the pen Hex had brought in the morning and asked what happened to it. He said Sian had found him a new cap from one that had run out of ink. He'd left it in the side of his coat this time. He gave it to her, and she wrote the number on the back of the ticket. Then she wrote her own number underneath, handed him both, and told him she hadn't always been sure he really knew it.

The bus came a minute before the phone said it would. Ninja got up with time to see the driver look toward the stop. Hex shifted to let her go through the opening first, then stood at the edge of the pavement as she found a place by the window. He looked down when he noticed the ticket still in his hand. At the first corner he looked up again. She thought he could see her through the glass even after it began to catch the light from the houses. She stayed turned that way until she could no longer see his face.
