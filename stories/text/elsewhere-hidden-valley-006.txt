Previously on Elsewhere. Ninja and Hex crossed the footbridge on the far side of the ridge. From a bus shelter, Ninja called her aunt and asked for the number of Mara, a friend of Elise’s. Mara picked them up outside a laundrette. Ninja told Hex he could come, and that she wanted him to wait downstairs when they arrived.

The flower shop had a green awning with one bad corner. It sagged even with the rain running clear of it. Mara pulled up close to the door and got everybody out before she moved the van. Ninja could see the lights on behind the glass. There were cut stems in a white enamel bucket. On the counter, one flower had come loose from a bundle with an elastic band around it. It was the sort of place she could have walked past every day and thought she knew, right up until she needed to come inside.

Mara took them through the shop. Upstairs was her kitchen. Straight ahead, through a door with dirty handprints at different heights, was a little room where she wrapped orders. She told Hex he could wait there, if he didn’t mind a draught when they opened the side door. He said he didn’t mind. Ninja saw that he’d noticed the handprints too. The smallest one was only as wide as two of his fingers. He let the door swing in without putting his hand over it.

Mara put the kettle on upstairs and asked Ninja to turn the latch if she wanted it turned. The stairs were under the window at the other end of the landing. Ninja left the door open a few inches. It gave her a view of the top step. She didn’t think she’d have been able to listen properly if she couldn’t see it.

The kitchen held the warmth of another room. There was a rack of wet cups on the draining board, a folded cardigan by the bread bin, and two pieces of toast on a plate with a knife set between them. Mara asked if Ninja wanted one. Ninja said she could probably manage half. Mara cut the bigger piece in half and gave her the whole of the smaller one as well. When she set the plate down she looked Ninja in the face properly for the first time since they got out of the van.

She said Ninja had run past her once as a girl without a coat on. Elise came along two minutes later holding the coat by the hood, with the cuffs knocking against her boots. She hadn’t been going very fast. She told Mara that if she carried the coat long enough, the person who needed it would eventually turn round. They’d stood there under an awning till she did.

Ninja had no memory of the coat. She remembered a day she’d reached into the cupboard and found a pair of gloves Elise had left to dry. That could have been the same day. She told Mara that in case it helped her remember something else. Mara smiled, pushed two mugs toward the dry end of the table, and said you never got to be sure with that sort of memory. You got the piece you got.

“I have a piece from the night she died now. Someone took a photograph of me outside the corner shop. Hex got it from the man he was working for, and he showed it to Elise. He told her they knew where I lived. I’ve been trying all morning to put that beside the things I remember about her. Did she ever tell you anybody was following her?”

Mara waited until Ninja had finished. She said Elise had asked if she could leave something in the flat one evening. She’d said she was making a copy of some notes and didn’t want the copy and the original in the same place. Mara didn’t ask what the notes were for. Elise said she’d collect them herself, and that if she didn’t, somebody would eventually know to ask. She hadn't left a name. Mara had taken that to mean somebody else knew what Elise was doing. She said she’d had five years to think about that conclusion, and it seemed less clever every time she went over it.

Ninja’s hands were burning against the mug. She set it down. For a minute she looked at the steam and thought she could still choose to ask Mara about the coat. Then she asked whether Mara still had the copy.

Mara said she’d put it back where Elise left it after she heard the news. Nobody ever came to ask. She should have told somebody about it sooner. She said the words from where she was standing, with a hand wrapped around the strip of fabric on the back of her chair. Ninja could tell it mattered to Mara that she be able to hear the answer without having to look up.

“Let me see it. We can talk about what you should have done after I know what you had in front of you.”

Mara went into the room next door and came back with a narrow tin that had once held biscuits. She set it on the table and took the lid off. There were thread ends, a baby tooth inside a tiny bag, and two spare picture hooks with the little nails still through them. Under those lay a piece of blue paper folded into thirds.

Elise had used a blue duplicate pad at work. She’d tested the first one she bought on the kitchen table to see whether you could make a readable second copy without writing hard enough to mark the wood. Ninja remembered the joke she made about the strength of her arm and how she kept talking while she searched for a thinner board to go under it. Now she looked at the folds in the paper and could guess where Elise would have pressed them flat with a fingernail.

Mara opened the sheet. There was nothing on the back. On the front were five names and a small number of plainly written sentences. Dates stood beside the names. At the bottom was one sentence that didn’t match any entry. “I heard the doors close and thought it meant they’d gone.” Ninja read that sentence once quickly and again more slowly. She couldn’t tell what Elise meant by it. She knew the shape the second reading would leave in her head.

Three of the names were unfamiliar. A fourth belonged to somebody she remembered hearing about from Elise, though she couldn't place the conversation. The fifth she knew the minute she saw it. A woman named Beth had lived two doors down from their aunt during the winter when the kitchen flooded. Beth had lent them an electric heater and told them to leave it switched off if nobody was home. The following summer she went away. Ninja had heard the house was going to be sold. That was the last thing she knew.

She wondered briefly if there was another Beth with the same surname. She found the date and the little remark beside it. It said, “Last shift. Walked to the bus with me.” She could remember Elise saying she’d seen Beth at the bus stop. It had been the sort of detail that meant somebody had a little company on the way back, and Ninja could remember agreeing that it was nice. Nothing else about the call had stayed around to tell her why she hadn’t asked a second question.

Mara pushed the chair opposite her back with her foot and sat down. She remembered Beth borrowing buckets from the shop for the flooded kitchen. One had still been outside the back door when the house sold. Mara had gone to fetch it herself. As far as she knew, Beth was already away by then. She could tell Ninja what she’d seen. She drew a line with her finger beside two names the way a person might put a finger in a book while she looked up a word.

Ninja’s thoughts
I have passed that house a hundred times since we got the heater back to her. I always look at the window with the red curtains. I don’t think I ever made myself say why. I want to know what happened to Beth before a man tells me what the date means.

Ninja asked Mara whether Beth still had family nearby. Mara knew she’d had a son but couldn’t say where he was. Better to find out before anybody assumed he wanted a stranger to bring up what happened. Ninja looked up at that. She appreciated being reminded that knowing one side of a name didn’t give you the whole person.

Mara went to look for an ordinary sheet of paper. She said Ninja could make her own notes and leave the blue one as it was for now. They didn’t need to do anything to the copy before they understood the original markings. When she came back, she had a blank sheet and the pen she used to write the prices on the flower cards. It wrote in a very dark green. Ninja put the date on the clean sheet. Beside it she wrote down what she herself remembered about Beth. It felt important to write it before she heard anybody else’s account.

Ninja took the photograph out of her coat. She turned it face up beside the blue sheet. For the first time there was something in the room she knew had been in Carver’s hands and something she knew had been in Elise’s. There wasn’t any reason to make them touch. She left a space between them, big enough for Mara to set her mug down.

The person who took the photograph had been on the other side of the road. Ninja told Mara what she remembered about Elise and the umbrella. Mara didn’t recognise the street from the little bit of shopfront she could see. She knew the umbrella, though. It folded up into a plastic tube with a cap that came off in your bag. Elise used to take it out without looking and leave the cap at the bottom until she had to dig for her keys. Mara had gone to the trouble of finding her another cap once. The new one lasted a week.

Ninja laughed. She could tell she’d surprised both of them. The laugh didn’t last any longer than it had to. When it was over she could still see the paper on the table. She found she could look at it for a moment longer now that she’d been allowed to remember the umbrella.

Ninja went back to the entry for Beth. Above it Elise had written another date and crossed it out. It was the only place on the page where she'd gone back to correct something. Ninja bent to see it properly, then stopped herself from trying to read through the ink. The last time she’d seen Elise writing out a date that carefully, they’d been filling in a form at their aunt’s. Their aunt knew the year she’d moved but had to work out the month from what was growing in the garden. Elise had asked about the washing line, and between them they’d got as far as June.

She wanted, sharply and quite uselessly, to be able to ring her sister and ask how she’d settled on the second date. She could picture getting a long answer with two or three better pieces of information folded into it. For years she’d thought she knew which things she missed. The things that still caught her tended to be jobs Elise would have been good at.

Mara came back from the sink carrying a towel. When she saw the way Ninja was bent over the paper, she set the towel down and waited.

Ninja asked Mara to tell her the evening again, beginning a little earlier. Mara said there had been a delivery at the shop that ran past closing. Elise stayed back and helped carry the empty buckets into the yard. She’d had a book with her. Mara didn’t know what she was reading but remembered putting the book on the dry end of the counter. Later, when they went upstairs, Elise took the book and the pad with her. She asked for a few minutes on her own at the kitchen table. Mara went downstairs to finish something. By the time she came back, the blue sheet was already folded. Elise held it out by one end.

Neither of them could remember what Elise did with her evenings on any reliable basis. She would turn up with two rolls wrapped in a paper bag or call from halfway down the street with a question about a key. Mara had thought there’d be a next time she saw her with the blue pad. She said this plainly and turned the towel over in her hands until the folded edges came together.

Ninja asked if Elise had seemed frightened on the evening she left it. Mara thought about it long enough that Ninja knew she was trying to remember a person and not answer a question. Elise had checked the time twice, she said. She had eaten one slice of toast and left the second. She was able to laugh at something Mara said about next door. Mara didn’t know what those things added up to. There were evenings she couldn’t have told you what she herself felt as she got to the other side of them.

Ninja thought of how easily she could have given somebody an account of last night from the way Hex handled his glass. The account would have had true things in it. If she happened to give it while looking at the photograph, it would have had different true things in it. She put a hand on the edge of the table and let it stay still while Mara went on.

Mara said she still had a calendar from that year downstairs in a box. She might be able to find the delivery date on it. They agreed she would look when she had time. Ninja added “delivery, calendar” to her own sheet and drew a space underneath. She wanted there to be room for Mara to remember that it happened on a Tuesday, or that it had happened an entirely different week from the one she thought it had. That would be useful to know too.

She asked for a glass of water. Mara filled it at the tap and set it down on the folded towel. Ninja drank almost all of it. She could see a bit of flour in the grain of the table from when somebody had rolled something out there. The rim of her glass cast a bright patch over the blue paper. She shifted it a little so she could see the page without the light on it. The writing came back as ordinary handwriting. The clearest thing in it was where Elise had put a full stop after Beth’s name and then decided she had something else to say.

Ninja said she wanted to read the page to someone else, very carefully, without filling in anything she thought Elise would have put if she’d had more room. Mara asked whether she meant Hex. Ninja said she meant someone who knew what to do with the page. For now, what she wanted from Hex was a memory that belonged to him.

Mara asked if she should go downstairs to tell Hex they needed a little more time. Ninja told her she’d do it herself. She opened the door a little wider and called down. The answer came from much closer than she expected. Hex was at the foot of the stairs. He’d heard the chair move and come as far as the first step. He asked if she wanted him to come up. He waited while she considered it.

“Yes. There’s a copy of a page Elise kept here. I’ve had a chance to read it for myself. You can come up and tell me if you recognise anything I read from it. Leave the paper on the table, please. I want to be the person who decides when the page moves.”

He came up slowly like somebody arriving at a house whose steps he didn’t know. He stood in the doorway until Mara pointed to an empty chair. Before he sat he looked through the kitchen window. Below it, down the back lane, somebody was turning a car round. For a second Ninja saw the stoppage in his face that she’d noticed in the market. The driver finished turning and went away. Hex followed the car with his eyes until he couldn’t see it. Then he thanked Mara for the chair.

Ninja read the surnames and kept the sheet in front of her. Hex listened to all five before he answered. The fifth wasn’t the one he’d heard Carver say, as far as he remembered. He knew the second. The man had been at the club once, and Hex had known him by his first name. He remembered that he’d been carried out to a taxi by two people who were trying to keep him from stumbling. He couldn’t say they’d meant him any harm. He could say they’d asked Hex to bring his coat, and that there was a tear in the lining where something used to be pinned. For a moment he could see that coat so clearly he kept looking at the back of the kitchen chair instead of at Ninja.

Mara asked whether it would help to reread the dates. Hex said he was almost sure it had been colder than the date beside the name suggested, but every time he put a month to what he remembered he thought about the coat again. He was willing to write down the smallest thing he knew. Ninja gave him another sheet. She left both her own notes and Elise’s paper where they were.

Hex wrote the name and the words “coat taken to taxi” and put the pen down. There was room to write a great deal more. He picked it up again and added that he hadn’t seen where the taxi went. Then he pushed the sheet across to Ninja so she could see where he had stopped.

She asked if that was everything he wanted to say about the man. He said he remembered one of the people who helped him out. There had been a time when that person could call Hex on a day off and expect him to come. That was all he could add without telling her things about the club that might look like answers but didn’t say what happened to this man.

Hex’s thoughts
I used to talk until a person heard the part I wanted them to hear. If I could keep going until I sounded certain, I could leave a lot of things out. That’s what my mouth wants to do right now. Write the part I can stand behind. Let her see where it ends.

Ninja got up to look out the window. The lane was empty. At the far end a man was opening the gate of a little yard. A white cat stood where the gate would swing and ignored him until he asked it politely to move. She watched it walk off, tail in the air. She thought how easy it had been for a car outside a window to become the only thing she could see.

Mara asked if the people looking for them knew the shop. Ninja said she didn’t know. Hex looked at the paper in front of him and said he’d never been here before and hadn’t known Mara’s name until the laundrette. Mara said she thought it was best to tell the people who lived next door that she’d had unexpected company and ask them to ring if anybody wanted her address. She went down to do that and left the door partway open. Ninja could hear it close again at the bottom of the stairs.

For a while neither of them said anything. Ninja could see Hex’s reflection in the window without turning. He’d put both hands flat on the table. There was something he wanted to ask, but she could tell he was going to sit there until she looked at him.

“I’m going to find somebody who can hear this properly and tell me what I can do with it. I’ll start with what Elise left and what Mara saw. If somebody asks you about the warehouse, are you willing to tell them the same things you told me on the hill? That includes the photograph, and what you said to get Elise to go.”

He said yes. It was quick enough that she knew he’d been thinking about the question before she asked it. She turned round. He had pushed the chair back a few inches. He stayed sitting because he could see her looking at him and was waiting for her next sentence.

She asked whether he’d say it if she wasn’t there to listen. For that one he took a little longer. Then he said he would.

She asked Hex to wait a minute while she found the part of her notebook where she’d started writing before he came upstairs. The notebook was really two sheets of paper. One had Elise’s notes copied in green and the other had an account of what Ninja had seen herself. She’d left a line for the call from the bus shelter and the name of the woman they’d spoken to. She filled that in now. She remembered only the first half of what she had told her aunt, so she wrote that much and drew a short line after it. There would be room to add the rest if she thought of it later.

Mara said they could clear the table and put the blue sheet in a new envelope. Ninja asked if they could leave it where it was while they talked. She found the crease in the photograph on her lap, ran one fingertip along it, and asked Mara whether she wanted to hear Hex speak. Mara said she would if Ninja asked her to. Ninja thought about how she’d asked him to wait downstairs. That had made it possible to tell Mara the first part without taking care of anybody else’s face. She wanted the same room for the next part.

Mara went downstairs to see whether she still had any of the good tea. As she went out, she said out loud that she could come back if they wanted her. The door at the foot of the stairs made a different sound from the one at the top. Hex seemed to listen for both before he spoke.

He said he had thought about going to see somebody after the warehouse. He didn’t say that so Ninja would hear that he had nearly done a good thing. He said he’d put his coat on and gone as far as the road. He was trying to remember precisely how long he’d stood there when it occurred to him that the time might be different now with five years between him and the road. He could tell her that he’d gone back inside, taken the coat off, and never made the call.

Ninja asked what he would have said. He thought he would have tried to tell it starting with the door shutting. That was the first thing he’d remembered whenever he’d tried to picture that night. The first words the person listening would have heard from him would have been about a fire and somebody he couldn’t reach.

Ninja turned over the sheet she’d written on. She told him they could start with who had the photograph and who put it in his hand. That was the part she needed to hear stated clearly, and the part she wanted somebody to hear first if there came a day when she let them listen. Hex took a breath and said he could start there. It was the first thing he’d said since coming upstairs that sounded as though he’d had to choose it.

She set her phone down on the dry patch by the end of the table. The glass was streaked where she’d wiped it with the inside of her coat. She could see the circle at the bottom of the screen that started a voice note. She told him she wanted to make a record of what he remembered today, while they could both still tell what he’d said himself and what they’d learned afterward. He could stop if he needed to, and when he was done she’d let him hear it back.

He carried the empty mugs to the counter. He came back with nothing in his hands and asked her if she wanted him to say the name he gave her at the bar. She did. She wanted to know that he could begin with it and keep speaking.

Ninja started the recording. Hex said his name, said where they were, and said what he had been asked to do with the picture. He stopped on the word “Elise” and then said the rest of her name. That was the only part he went back over. She could hear the breath he took to get through what he said to her. The distance from one side of the table to the other felt larger while he was speaking, as if for once there was room for both of them to sit there with the knowledge of it.

When he was done she let the recording run long enough to catch a sound from downstairs. Mara had come back in, put a box down, and called up that the neighbours had said hello. The clock clicked over while the phone was still running. Ninja stopped it and wrote the time on the page with her own account.

Hex’s thoughts
Last night all I could think about was getting her to stay until morning. Now I’m sitting here hoping she writes down exactly what I said, even the parts she might never want to hear again. I don’t want to ask her to stay before she’s had a chance to decide.

They listened back to the beginning. Hex didn’t comment on the sound of his voice. There was a place where he took a breath before saying what Carver had told him. Ninja had heard that breath from the other side of the table. Out of the phone it sounded like a man who had been asked a hard question and had decided to answer it. She let the note play to the end before she touched the screen.

Mara brought up three clean cups. Ninja said she wanted to ring her aunt again before they did anything else. She had said she would ring twice. Hex stood to give her the kitchen, and she shook her head. He waited. She took the phone to the window and called from there.

Ninja’s thoughts
I don’t know what I’m going to want tomorrow. Today I know where the page is, and I know where my own words are. I know I can ring home. I can let him sit at that end of the table without deciding what it means. I can start with hello.

Her aunt answered. Ninja said hello. Then she heard the familiar sound of a cupboard closing on the other end and leaned her head lightly against the cold glass. Below her the wet street was beginning to dry in patches. One narrow strip beyond the flower shop had already turned the colour of ordinary pavement.
