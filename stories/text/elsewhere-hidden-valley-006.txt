# Elsewhere: The Hidden Valley
## Episode 6 — The Blue Page

Previously in Elsewhere. A letter from her sister sent Ninja out of Bellwether with Hex and Daniel. They crossed a swollen river on a stone footbridge. At the old post office in the valley, Ruth told Ninja how Elise came there to deliver mail after a storm. Then she laid out an envelope that Elise had left behind. It had been waiting in a drawer for five years.

Ruth found a knife with a round end in the drawer by the sink. The envelope had already been opened. She had to use the knife because somebody had left it under a heavy book long enough for the damp to make the flap stick all over again. She worked at one corner until it gave. Then she opened the rest of it with her little finger.

Ninja hadn't realised she'd been waiting for a noise until she heard the paper let go. There was a faint rasp, followed by Ruth putting the knife down. It was the sound of somebody doing something carefully that would have been easier if they'd had no reason to care.

Inside was a piece of school exercise paper, taken cleanly out of a book along a ruled edge. There were two folds in it and a patch where the blue colour was lighter. Ruth spread it out right side up. The others could see it from their chairs if they leant forward. Daniel was the first to sit back. He had spent five years trying not to write down where his own copy came from, and apparently even somebody else's creases could make him feel as though he were reading something over another person's shoulder.

Ninja took in a list of names and a short set of instructions. She could hear them in her sister's voice, right down to the change of speed before Elise got to the part that needed the listener to pay attention. It said that nobody should copy an entry onto a new piece of paper until they checked whether the name beside it belonged to the person who was there that day. Under that, three names were written in pairs. Each person had two places they were said to have been. Elise had circled one date in the right-hand column.

Ninja had bought a pair of boots that day. She remembered how much they had cost, because she had left the price on one of them all through a shift and only found it when she changed to go home. She had called Elise afterward to tell her. They'd talked about soup. She had thought whatever Elise was doing at the other end of the call would always be there on its own, separate from the boots and the soup.

“Tell me why you kept it here.”

Ruth turned the blue page so that the two names on the bottom lay closest to her. She said Elise had written an instruction on the back of the envelope to leave it alone until she came again in person. When she heard that Elise had died, Ruth took the paper out. She had understood where to look for most of the places. She hadn't understood which person she could trust to tell her about them. She told herself she was keeping the promise as far as she still could. Then she told herself that for so long that she could do it while she was putting a second cup of tea in front of a man she knew had been on one of those roads that day.

The man had a key to the old post office. His name was Arthur Bell. He lived in a little timber house behind the east orchard. For years he had run an unofficial collection service when people couldn't get down the hill. He still did it once a week for two families. Ruth could remember who had made him a key. She couldn't remember the last time she'd noticed whether he carried it.

Daniel moved toward the paper and stopped with his hand in the air. Ruth slid it to him. He looked at the date again. In his copy, there was an entry with a time on it, a departure at twelve minutes past six. Nothing on his sheet gave any other meaning to a name. He didn't have a way of saying which hour the men in the office had been thinking about when they drew up an entry. He'd always been interested in that particular time because it was too awkward to be an estimate.

Hex reached across the table and turned his own mug until the handle pointed at the wall. He asked whether either sheet named a vehicle. Daniel's didn't. The blue page had a small mark Ruth had taken for an underlined letter. Hex asked to see it in better light. He carried the paper to the window with enough space under his hand that nothing brushed the sill. After a few seconds he said it looked like the first part of a registration. Two letters and a figure. Not enough to find a car on its own, but enough to check a car somebody knew.

He put the paper back without looking again. Ninja could see him deciding whether to tell her anything else. She saw him take a breath and realised that the decision was easy to mistake for the moment in which someone was about to tell the truth. Still she waited. He had been to Arthur's place for work once, he said. There was a car under a cover behind the timber house, and he remembered Arthur pushing a board up against its rear wheel so they could bring a handcart through. He couldn't remember the registration. He remembered thinking that he'd seen the car somewhere before.

Ruth poured more tea. She put the kettle down on a piece of folded cloth. The next sensible thing to do, she said, was ask Arthur about the blue page. He'd be at his place until midday, and then he'd usually walk the little way past the orchard to see the stream. Everybody looked at Ninja. She let them for a few seconds. She wanted to know whether they could leave the page on the table and talk through what they would actually be asking him before they went.

* * *

At the old counter, not very far from the map, Ruth kept a long ledger bound in blue cloth. The binding had come partly away from the pages. When she carried it out of the room she put a tray underneath so nobody had to hold it up with both hands all the way to the table.

She had started using the book during the years in which people began ringing the county office to find out when anything was going to happen. A person could call a number and be told a van had gone through at six in the evening. She would write down who had actually got something from the van and at what time, because customers wanted a chance to be angry with somebody who was real. It mattered if it was nearly half past by the time the last part of the way got cleared. It also mattered if you were waiting for a bottle of insulin, which had happened once. After that Ruth put a star beside every date she knew she would remember. The date Elise had circled was one of the stars.

Halfway down the page there was a signed entry that said Arthur collected an undelivered sack at quarter past six. This was the first time Ninja had been able to picture Arthur in the valley that evening. If he'd been out in the rain with a sack of mail, he might have been wearing some sort of cap. He might have hung it on the iron hook by the counter and left water all over the flagstone.

She asked Ruth whether she had seen him collect it. Ruth pointed to a narrow line of writing in the margin. That had been added the following morning. It said she'd been in the back of the house with the relief driver and found the sack gone when she came out. The driver had signed the main note. Nobody had made a habit of doing it one way round or the other as long as there was a name to write down. She remembered she had been annoyed about it at the time because the relief driver took one of her good pens out to the van. Later she found it in a coat pocket that had holes in both corners and didn't know how anyone had managed to get it there.

On the table, Daniel put his own copy beside the ledger. The entry he had been thinking of said a driver left the social club at twelve minutes past six. The initials were A. B. Arthur could have driven to the old post office from there while keeping considerably better time than any driver Ruth remembered. But another name, in another hand, gave the club time as two and a half hours later. It was beside the same receipt number. Daniel had always thought that one of the two entries was a correction made at the end of the night.

Hex asked to see the column beside it. There was a line like a little curved path between the two. It went from the top number to the bottom name. He remembered Carver taking care to keep him out of the back office when anybody went in to change a receipt. He had thought the man who came with the money wanted to count it in quiet. They might have been using a name that already belonged to a real errand. He could understand how much harder it would have been for Elise to question it if every name led back to an ordinary job someone really had to do.

They put the sheets down. Outside the window a thrush got to the edge of the cold frame and perched there, wondering whether to go where any of them could see it more clearly. It waited half a minute. Then it went around to the other side. Ninja followed it over the top of the cold frame until it dropped out of sight.

“I want to go through what we know before we ask him anything.”

She took a pen from near the kettle. She wrote the date, both times, the initials and Arthur's name on the back of a used envelope. Then she changed the line beside the name to say the book said Arthur. It was a different sort of sentence. A person could tell you if the book said something. They couldn't tell you whether Arthur was there just by reading it again.

Daniel slid his copy away from her a little. She thought he might be thinking about taking it home. After a moment he put both hands on the table and said he had told a lawyer about one of the entries once, a long time before he came to Bellwether. He gave her a photocopy and didn't give the exact address it came from. He could remember putting it in the front pocket of a bag on his way to see her. He also remembered carrying the bag back again afterward and feeling embarrassed by how he kept touching the pocket to check it was empty. When she wrote back to him, she said she needed to know whether somebody else could say where it had come from. He hadn't had anybody he could ask then.

Ninja wrote lawyer on the envelope without a date. She could ask for the date later, when it would help more than it hurt. Then she gave Daniel back his copy and asked if he would keep it until they got back. It meant something to him to be asked. He put it in the same place he'd brought it from, and looked down at the place before pressing the fold flat.

Before they went, Ruth put the kettle in its old place close to the stove. She left the blue sheet in the ledger and then took it out again. Ninja asked whether she'd be happier if they took just the envelope with the notes, and kept the original here. Ruth was. She watched Ninja put it in her pocket and found a thicker coat for herself. It was dry on the outside and still a little damp around the cuffs. They had been there long enough by then that none of them minded.

* * *

Behind the orchard, the bench faced the wrong way if you wanted to look at the valley. It faced the bank, where wet grass grew darker around the mouths of old rabbit holes. Ruth told Ninja it had been put there when Arthur was repairing the wall so he could sit down and see how far he'd got. Nobody had turned it afterward. A person could walk all that way to get a view and still end up looking at a job someone had left for themselves.

The sun made a brief appearance while they were going up toward it. It shone through a break in the cloud one field wide. A wet line along the top of the wall lit up slowly enough that Ninja could watch the bright bit move. Then the cloud closed and the wall was the same colour again. She stopped when she reached the bench, partly because she'd got there first, and partly because she wanted to wait until she'd heard the other footsteps come to an end before she knocked on Arthur's door.

Hex sat beside her on the dry end. The damp half had kept the print of somebody's coat. There was a discarded glove underneath it which must have been out there a while. He looked at the glove and moved it onto the stone where a person could see it. Then he asked whether she wanted him in the room while she spoke to Arthur. It was difficult to tell how hard he'd had to work to ask the question. He was looking at the hill the whole time.

“Come in with me. I don't want you answering for me.”

He said he could do that. She'd meant the second part. She found she also meant the first, and it was an odd thing to have to get used to in the middle of everything else.

Arthur was in a lean-to at the end of his house, filing the edge of a piece of wood. They could hear it before they turned the corner. A dog came round to meet them, looked at all the people in the lane, and took the dog-sized space under the bench as though it had been built there for him. He was still there when they turned to look for him a minute later.

Ruth called Arthur's name. He put the file down. He wore a pair of loose gloves with the middle finger cut off one of them. His hand had a slight tremor. Ninja had the thought she often had at work when somebody showed her his hands before he told her what was wrong. She wanted first to give him a place to put whatever he was carrying. He had already put the wood down. She could see a line of pencil close to the edge.

In the little kitchen he pulled out a chair for Ruth and asked whether they had come about the high water. She said everything was all right at the bridge, last they looked. She introduced Ninja. Arthur knew which sister she was. He took off the gloves while he told her how sorry he had been when the news came, and set them one on top of the other as though they were pages he wanted to keep in order.

Ninja got the envelope out of her pocket. She asked what he remembered about the day on it. He said his daughter had been visiting. They'd gone somewhere to eat after a funeral. He knew who the funeral had been for. Apart from the name, the first detail that came to him was how the place they'd eaten at had been about to shut when they arrived. The cook had found two bowls of soup for them anyway.

She asked if he had been at the post office that afternoon. He said he hadn't. Ruth explained what the book recorded. Arthur drew his hand back from the table. After a minute he asked to see the writing on the envelope. He could read it in the window, but only if he moved it back and forth once. When he gave it back, he set a finger on the date before taking his hand away.

It turned out the key that opened the little door wasn't the only one she'd had made. He'd lent it to a relief driver at the end of the previous winter. He did it at the counter because the driver said Ruth had gone out through the main house and he didn't want to walk all the way round to catch her. He had asked for it back twice. The second time he'd been given a new key. It still worked, so he had carried it home. He hadn't thought about the old one again until this morning.

Ninja asked who the driver was. Arthur couldn't remember his last name. He had gone by Pete. He had a habit of folding a piece of loose change into a receipt when he handed it over instead of giving it to you separately. He did the same thing with the dry cleaning tab he'd once been given to take back. Arthur remembered because he'd swept the floor for a copper coin before realizing it was still in the paper.

Hex shifted in his chair. Ninja could hear him about to take a breath. He waited until she turned to him. Carver's first name was Peter. Hex hadn't heard him use it himself. He'd seen it on the inside of a book that Carver put back in his office before leaving on a Friday. He'd wondered if Arthur's driver and Carver could have been the same man, when Ruth showed him the names. Until now he'd had no reason good enough to put it into a question.

Arthur took the two gloves off the table and set them on the back of his chair. He said he could look for a receipt from the book Pete brought back once, or the note he left at the house when he went out with the key. He had a drawer with that sort of thing in it. The drawer would take him some time. Ninja understood he needed them to go before he began to look. They got up. Ruth thanked him and waited outside while he found his glasses for the second time that afternoon.

* * *

The light in the upper room came on a little before four. Ruth hadn't meant to turn it on so early. The garden had gone blue around the edges and there were two letters she still needed to finish, which was how she usually judged the time at which the little lamp ought to come on. She brought the two letters up, forgot why she was carrying them and left them on the edge of the chair.

Ninja had never been in the room before. It had a slope in the ceiling and a section of wallpaper behind the wardrobe that nobody had got round to finishing. If she put her back against the wall, the warmth of the chimney came through the plaster. She stayed there long enough to finish taking the water out of her hair with a dry towel. It was the first time that day she had been upstairs somewhere and could see the people she needed to find from the same window. Hex was at the bottom of the garden with Ruth, checking whether a piece of clear plastic could be made to cover the broken bit of the cold frame. Daniel was taking a phone call under the back porch. She could see the hand that held the phone. The rest of him had gone into his coat.

Ruth had brought up a thin card with the date from Arthur's spare key written on it. The driver's first name was visible at the bottom. He had written it in pencil. Ruth found it tucked at the back of the drawer with the pens just after the others came back. They had one piece more than before. Ninja felt more relieved about having a new person she could ask than she felt triumphant about finding part of a name.

There was a round tin on the small shelf above the bed. Ninja asked Ruth about it when she went by with the towel. Ruth brought the tin down and showed her what was inside. Buttons, two coins that were no longer used and a small steel key with an oval hole at the top. It had belonged to a bicycle carrier. Ruth remembered Elise putting it there on her first visit, because she couldn't find the right pocket in the coat she'd borrowed. The carrier went back on Ruth's van the following week. She had never got round to doing anything with the key.

Ninja picked it up. It was a very plain key and she didn't want to make Ruth explain why it was still there. There were a lot of things of Elise's that she had spent five years trying not to lose, and a lot of things she would probably never know about. She set the key down beside the coins and slid the tin back into the right place. Ruth asked whether she would like it. Ninja thought she would like to know there was one thing here she would see if she ever came back. She left it on the shelf.

Ruth pushed the empty chair away from the door. From the corridor Ninja could see the other side of the wallpaper, where somebody had made a fresh pattern with the join in the paper. She asked Ruth who used to sleep there. The post office had owned the room, Ruth said, if it was possible for a post office to own a room through somebody putting a single bed in it. Any driver could use it if the river got too high. Elise had slept there once and put the pillow at the foot of the bed so she could look down the valley and see if it was still raining when she woke up. Ruth pulled the chair a little nearer the window before she left. It gave Ninja somewhere to sit while she made her call.

She called her aunt. Her aunt asked if she'd had a nice rest, then told her she had accidentally bought twice as many oranges as she wanted and was prepared to leave some by the door when she got back. The ordinary conversation turned out to be hard to keep up once she knew she was going to change it. She asked how her aunt remembered Elise riding the bike. The answer went on for a while. Her aunt had heard that Elise took the hill without putting a foot down the first week she lived at the flat. There were two people who claimed they'd seen it. They disagreed about whether she kept the back light on in the day.

Then Ninja told her where she was and what they had found. She stopped after a minute or two to let her aunt ask anything she wanted. What her aunt asked was whether there was another person there who knew she'd gone upstairs. Ninja said there were three of them, and she could see them from the window. Her aunt took a breath. She wanted to know if she could call again later. Ninja said she could. By the time they hung up, she knew how many oranges there really were. There were seven. It was a generous way to describe twice as many as you'd meant to buy.

For a moment she stood with the phone in one hand and the towel in the other. The glass had fogged while she was talking. She cleared a place the size of a hand, and saw Hex shutting the frame with his boot. He passed Ruth the last of the plastic so she could use the scissors herself. Ninja remembered the way he had let her find where to hold the light when Len's car was stuck, the first night. She hadn't needed five more things to happen to know what she liked about him. She had needed a bit longer to find out what that meant while both of them had things they had to do.

When he came upstairs, he knocked once with his knuckle on the open door. He wanted to know if she was all right, and didn't ask it until he was close enough to hear the answer. She made space on the window seat and he sat beside her. For a time they looked at the lights near Arthur's house. His dog had come out into the lane and was sniffing a low piece of wall in the exact same place it had been sniffing when they walked away from it.

“I can be angry and still want you here.”

Hex looked at her. He said he understood that. There was a silence, long enough for her to work out how much she wanted to do with it. She put the towel around both of their hands, because his were cold and she could. He sat without moving. By the time she needed to look out the window again, he'd already turned so she could see past his shoulder.

At the far side of the orchard, two lights went up on the road. They were just headlamps. They would go out of sight in a moment if the car was following the road toward Bellwether. Instead they rolled back to the turn by the stone bridge and stopped. One of them went out first. The other remained in the trees for a second, then went out as well.

Ninja and Hex stayed at the window. They could make out the low white wall below the bridge, and farther over the small house where Arthur would be sitting, likely with his drawer open. Ruth came up behind them to collect the two unfinished letters. Ninja told her about the lights, and Ruth came as far as the window to look for herself. She knew how a driver normally turned round on that road. This one hadn't.

In the next episode, somebody knocks on the old counter while Ruth is putting the spare kettle on. Ninja can see a small square of paper among the change on the table. She remembers what Arthur told her about the man who used to put his coins inside a receipt.
