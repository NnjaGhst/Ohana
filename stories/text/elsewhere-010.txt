# Elsewhere — Episode 10: The Room Upstairs

### Previously on Elsewhere

Ninja and Hex met Alice Reed under the old ferry shelter. Alice gave the police the first copy Elise had made. Among the papers was an order with Hex’s name signed by someone else. Alice asked him to tell the police about the first version of the order too, the one he really did sign.

On the way back, Hex gave Ninja a photograph of Elise standing beside a bicycle, with a bottle of milk in the basket. He said he would finish his own statement.

### Chapter One — The Date at the Top

Hex went to the workshop early. Tomas had put a mug beside the clock and left a little note underneath it that said the tea was from yesterday. An arrow beneath those words pointed to a second mug, with a tea bag sitting dry in the bottom.

He took the old statement out of the desk. It was dated six days after the fire. The sentences were neat, written in the style people learned when they expected somebody else to remove anything personal. It began with a description of his duties and used the phrase an error of judgement before anybody did any actual judging.

He could remember writing it. He’d been in a borrowed flat where the only door anybody used was at the back. There was a scratched glass top on the table. Every time he finished a page he moved it carefully to the left to avoid losing a corner in the edge of the glass. He had been so meticulous with the paper. There were whole hours from the fire that he had left out.

He reached for a fresh sheet.

Tomas appeared at the door and asked whether he should come back later.

“I don’t think so,” Hex said. “I want to remember there’ll be somebody here when I’m done.”

Tomas took a minute to consider that. Then he put an old radio on the far bench, turned it off when it made a crackle, and left it unplugged. At the other end of the room, he took a small clock apart. He laid each piece on a cloth in the order he removed it. Hex could hear the occasional tap of metal and, beyond that, the rain on the roller door.

The new statement began with the first version of the form. He described the arrow he had drawn, the missing return journey, and the person who took the form back from him. Then he wrote what happened after. He signed a replacement copy of a related transfer instruction after he was told someone else would handle the return arrangement. He hadn’t asked to see it. It had been possible to ask. He put that in too.

At eleven he rang the officer. They arranged to meet after lunch. He gave her the old draft as well as the new one. When he came out, it was still raining, but somebody at the café had put the chairs back under the awning. He stood looking at the spare ones for a moment.

Then he sent Ninja a message. It said he had given a statement. The rest he could tell her himself if she wanted to hear it.

Tomas had warned him before he left that he might come back wanting something simple to finish. The clock was still laid out on the workbench. When Hex returned after seeing the officer, there was a new cloth beside it. A small round hole in the clock face had once held a screw that never wanted to sit flat. Hex chose the second-smallest screwdriver and spent ten minutes getting the screw to catch.

“Done?” Tomas asked.

“The clock, nearly.”

“I was thinking of the other thing.”

Hex set the screwdriver down. He described the chair he’d sat on in the interview room and the way it had moved when he rested his elbows on his knees. There was a jug of water on the table. He had poured some into a paper cup and then waited with the cup almost full, because he knew he would have to put it down before he could start.

The officer had asked why he’d kept the earliest version of his statement. He’d said there was a date at the top. It was a date he was still able to explain, in his own handwriting, from a week when anyone who asked what he meant tended to get a rehearsed answer.

Tomas asked whether he had wanted to take any of it back afterward.

“Of course,” Hex said.

Tomas nodded. He had managed to make the question sound harmless enough that Hex knew his answer could stay where it was.

They fitted the clock hands. Tomas held the face still while Hex tightened the nut. They agreed on the time within a minute and put it back on the wall. Hex stood underneath until he heard it tick.

He could do many things, it turned out, when he only tried to do one at a time. There was bread in the shop, a text still waiting from Ninja, and a screwdriver to take back to the drawer before Tomas invented another story about it.

### Chapter Two — The Key on the Hook

Ada found Ninja on the staircase with her phone in her hand. Ninja had been listening to the last bit of Elise’s recording again, then putting the phone away without actually putting it away.

“There’s something Rose wanted me to give you,” Ada said. “She passed it over this morning while you were asleep.”

She held a key with a flat white tag. On it someone had written upstairs, with the second half of the word much smaller than the first.

“What does it open?”

“A room over the print shop. Rose had a tenant who did late shifts up there years ago. On the way out yesterday, you mentioned the photograph with the bicycle. Rose remembered who used to park it round the back. She went through the old rental book.”

Ninja held the key out flat on her palm.

“Why would Elise have a room here?”

“She didn’t have to tell me that to have one,” Ada said gently. “Rose says there’s a chair by the window and two boxes under a sheet. She said to take your time.”

It suited Ada to make the whole conversation take place on the stairs, where there was no reason for anybody to insist it was an important moment. Ninja let the key rest in her hand. From where she stood she could see the guesthouse front window and a car parked on the street. It was a pale blue one she knew belonged to the woman who had trouble reversing near the gate. She hadn’t been looking at every passing car that morning. She was relieved to catch herself knowing one.

“Can I leave the recorder here while I go?”

“Of course.”

Ninja climbed a couple of steps and came back down. Then she did something which would have been surprisingly difficult to describe to a person who had never lost someone. She went into her room, put the recorder back in the drawer, and pressed the drawer closed without checking it a second time.

### Chapter Three — The Stairs Above the Swallow

At the print shop, Rose showed them to the entrance at the back. The staircase had its own light switch about three inches from the ceiling. Mara claimed that it had been fitted by a very tall electrician having a very good day. Ninja wondered what he might have done with the rest of the wiring.

Hex waited on the bottom step.

“I want to go in first. Will you come up if I ask?”

“Any time.”

She went up. At the top was a white door and a strip of clean paint where an old brass number had been taken off. The key turned with a soft scrape. The latch caught on the frame until she leaned into it. Everything she had done to open it was so ordinary that for a few seconds she didn’t know what she would do if there was anything inside.

It was a small room. The floor sloped very slightly from the doorway to the window. Someone had left two pencils and a chipped green dish on a windowsill. There was a framed print of a pear. One pane of glass gave back a faint reflection of the street, and the other showed the river between the roofs.

Ninja closed the door behind her. She waited for something to happen to her. Eventually she unbuttoned her coat and sat in the chair by the window.

The first thing she noticed was the height of the chair. It was a little too low. Elise would have liked that. She used to sit right down in anything before deciding whether it was ridiculous, and sometimes decided it was both ridiculous and comfortable.

On the ledge under the chair was a piece of cardboard, folded twice to stop the shortest leg from rocking. When Ninja put one foot down on it, the chair became beautifully steady. Whoever had wedged it had known exactly how thick to fold the piece.

She stayed at the window for a little while longer. In the street a woman in a raincoat was hurrying toward the bus stop. She reached it in time and still ran another little step after the bus had opened its doors. There was no reason to remember her, and Ninja thought she might always remember her.

On the landing she could hear the print shop underneath her. A machine ran for about ten seconds. Then it stopped and somebody walked across a different part of the floor. The building had sounds she would get to know if she came often enough.

Rose had set a postcard on the little table by the door. Ninja had walked past it on her way to the window. She picked it up now. It advertised a jumble sale in a church hall with the address written too small to read comfortably. Across the back Elise had written bring a tape measure. There was a faint sliver of graphite under that sentence where somebody had rubbed out a calculation.

Ninja thought of Elise going to the jumble sale. She could imagine the storm of suggestions Elise would make as soon as anybody invited an opinion. A little table had to be a little table you could walk around. A cushion needed to be one you were prepared to spill tea on. If somebody tried to sell her a vase with a hairline crack, she’d ask whether they also had a jar with a lid that would fit inside it.

After their last phone call Ninja had spent weeks going through details like these and expecting all of them to feel the same. A particular voice would come back, and then a sentence, and she would be furious to find it there without the person. The note about the tape measure felt a little different. It belonged to a woman who had stood in this room looking at a space along the wall and imagining where a table could go.

Ninja looked at the space herself. The wall was bare except for a scuff along the skirting board. She moved the postcard a little farther onto the table so it would not slip off.

There was still no reason to look through every single thing before leaving. She could come back. The room made this suggestion so quietly that at first she didn’t catch it. Then she heard one of Rose’s steps on the floor below and knew how to make her own next step. She went to the door and called for Hex.

### Chapter Four — The Boxes

Ninja called down the stairs. Hex came up and stopped on the landing until she opened the door all the way for him. He looked at the pear print first.

“Does that look like a good pear to you?”

“It looks patient.”

“That’s a very generous thing to say about it.”

He offered to help move the boxes. They put the first one on the table. The sheet over the second slid down onto the floor by itself. Ninja left it there. She had been made to feel she ought to be careful of so many things lately. A sheet could have its afternoon wherever it fell.

The first box contained pencils, a winter hat, some folded paper, and a thick yellow notebook. There was a small map tucked inside. It showed the ferry crossing when it was still running. Elise had marked circles at the bakery, the print shop, and a bench halfway down a street whose name had been covered by a brown mark from a ring of tea.

Ninja very slowly turned a few pages. Elise had written a price for a second-hand table. There was a list of things she needed for the room. Among them was a photograph of Ninja, with a note in brackets that said one she likes.

“She never thought I’d like the ones she took.”

“Did you?”

“Sometimes, after I stopped looking for reasons not to.”

Hex went to the window. He kept his hands low, out of the way, as Ninja picked up the book. There was a loose sheet at the back with a sketch of the room. Elise had drawn a second chair by the window. It was a little taller than the one that was there. Underneath she had written check which side gets the afternoon.

In the second box was a cake tin of a kind Ninja hadn’t seen for years, cream on the outside, with yellow pears painted round it. She recognised one dent by the catch. It had come from their kitchen cupboard. Closing that cupboard too hard had once sent the tin down onto a tile. Both of them had been there, and neither had ever admitted which one touched the door.

Ninja opened the tin. It held a folded estimate for a new chair, a loose peppermint, and a spare key. On the estimate, Elise had made another note.

You can come here and do absolutely nothing.

Ninja read the words again. She sat down in the chair with the notebook on her lap. The little folded cardboard stayed obediently where it was. From where he stood, Hex could hear her turn the note over. There was nothing on the back. He said nothing, because there was nothing she needed from him for that particular moment, and because he had time now to let her have all of it.

### Chapter Five — Two People and a Window

After a while Ninja asked Hex to sit. There wasn’t any second chair, so he found a low wooden box. He checked whether it was likely to give way and made the mistake of checking a second time after sitting down.

“If it breaks, I’m telling Tomas it came at you unprovoked.”

“I’d appreciate that.”

They looked out at the river. In a minute a train would go across the far bridge. They knew because the light at the crossing changed and a few small cars stopped.

“Did you send her things or tell her things?”

He thought about what she might be asking.

“Both, sometimes. I lent her a torch once and she returned it with batteries. She made a point of telling me she’d put them in so I wouldn’t spend a week believing I had good luck.”

Ninja smiled.

“Did you know she was planning to keep this room?”

“No.”

“I keep thinking I ought to know the shape of her life. And then there’s a chair I’ve never seen.”

“There were whole streets you never saw,” he said. “She talked about you as if you’d have liked to walk down them when there was time.”

Ninja moved the notebook over so he could look at the drawing. Outside, the train made a low sound and went through the space between two buildings, gone before they could count the carriages.

“Tell me what you wrote today. Start where you want. If I need you to stop, I’ll say so.”

He started with the return journey. He used the words he had put down on the page and then changed one because it sounded too easy. He told her where he had been and what he could have done earlier. She asked two questions. One took him some time. He answered both.

When he had finished, Ninja went to the windowsill and picked up the green dish. There was a thin chip in the glazed edge, which she covered with her thumb.

“I’m glad you told the officer. I’ll need time with the rest.”

“I know.”

They stayed in the room a little longer. He didn’t offer to take her anywhere. When she stood up, he stood too.

When the train had gone, Hex got up and set the little box back against the wall. Without thinking he turned it so the loose nail faced away from the room. Ninja watched him in the reflection in the window. He was still good at small useful things, even when he’d had to sit for a long while with some much harder ones.

He asked whether she wanted a moment alone. She said she was ready to go, but stayed at the window for one last look. He had already put his hand on the latch without opening the door. She liked it that way. The next sound in the room would happen when she decided to make it.

There had been an awful lot of people carrying things over thresholds. Iona with her newspaper. Ruth with the box. Alice with the cake after she gave away the papers she had kept for five years. Ninja held a tin that might have belonged to her from the beginning and tried to remember the last time she’d baked enough to put anything in it.

It was a long time ago, probably. She had lent a cake lifter to somebody and never asked for it back, so it could still be sitting in someone’s kitchen, waiting for her to turn up on an ordinary afternoon. The thought was better than finding it neatly put away at home.

Hex looked at the tin. He refused to ask if she wanted him to carry it. She was glad he didn’t ask, and then surprised herself by handing it to him while she did up her coat. He held it with two hands until she wanted it back.

They turned the light off. The pear print vanished last, because the window was opposite it. Then they stood in the doorway long enough to let their eyes adjust, and headed toward Rose’s voice at the bottom of the stairs.

### Chapter Six — Leaving a Key

Rose was sitting at the counter downstairs, smoothing out a delivery label over and over with the side of her hand. She told Ninja there was no hurry to decide what to do with the boxes. The room hadn’t been on the rent list for some time. Unless the electrician turned up and wanted to charge her for a replacement switch, there was no reason for it to go anywhere this week.

Ninja took the cake tin with her. She put the long key back on Rose’s hook and asked if she might borrow it again tomorrow. Rose said she could keep it until Sunday. Her arm went toward the hook and then paused for Ninja to take the key herself.

Outside it had stopped raining. Hex and Ninja took the street to the bakery. The door was open, which let the warmth out as far as the pavement. Ninja bought two rolls. She told the woman behind the counter she’d like the second one wrapped separately. Later, at Briar House, Ada would ask why.

She would say she was saving a roll for tomorrow, because she wanted to go back to the room and watch where the afternoon light fell. If the chair was on the wrong side, she would move it.

Her phone buzzed as they turned into the lane. It was a message from the officer. Iona was willing to give more details about a meeting she’d attended after the fire. The officer would pass on what she could, when she could.

Ninja put the phone back in her pocket. There would be more questions. For the first time in days she knew exactly where she would be when she decided which to ask next.

At Ada’s gate, Hex held it open. She stepped through, then stopped and looked back into the street.

“There’s an extra roll if you don’t have plans for lunch.”

He looked down at the bag, which very clearly contained two separate parcels.

“Tomorrow can look after its own roll.”

He came through the gate. She waited while he put a hand on the latch and closed it softly behind him.
