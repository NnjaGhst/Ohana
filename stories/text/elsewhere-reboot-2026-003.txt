Elsewhere
Episode 3 — The Night Between Them

Previously on Elsewhere

Five years after the death of her brother Cassian, Ninja came to Bellwether hoping to become somebody nobody knew. Hex had spent those same years running from the night that had taken Cassian's life. In the Crooked Lantern, he recognized the woman from a photograph he had never been able to forget. Against every instinct that had kept him alive, he sent her a drink.

Neither of them knew what that small decision was about to set in motion.

Five years earlier

The night air tasted like metal and rain that hadn't started yet.

Hex moved through the building with the rest of the team, breath steady, heartbeat locked down where it couldn't interfere. The intel had been clean. Elias in a second-floor office, minimal security, one exit that mattered. In and out before the quiet neighborhood even knew something had shifted.

Then the variables started multiplying.

Voices where there shouldn't have been voices. A door that should have been locked standing half open. The sharp, wrong sound of someone who wasn't part of the plan moving through the dark.

Hex's shield slammed fully into place. The caring man who still existed underneath it was pushed so far down he barely registered as a pulse. What remained was efficient, cold, and already calculating angles.

He had been doing this long enough to recognize the particular discomfort of a briefing that had ceased to resemble the world outside the briefing room.

There had been a time when he would have asked who was supposed to be in the building. Who might live nearby. Who was being asked to bear the consequences of a decision made by somebody who would never come here.

He still asked himself those questions.

He had learned what happened to men who asked them aloud.

For a fleeting moment he remembered the photograph in the file. Pink hair. A stubborn expression. A name that had seemed so incongruous among the sterile little entries about liabilities and exposure.

Ninja.

He pushed the memory aside.

HEX — SURVIVAL THOUGHT: Not now.

They cleared the main room. Elias was there, older than the most recent photos, eyes already understanding what the night had become. He didn't beg. He tried to bargain with information, with names, with the quiet threat of files that would surface if he disappeared. The team didn't slow.

There was something almost insulting about how ordinary he looked.

Hex had spent years watching the consequences of this man's rise. He had read the report about his first assignment, understood at last whose interests he had helped advance, and imagined that finding himself in the same room as Elias Voss would feel like reaching the end of a long, terrible explanation.

It didn't.

There was only a man with a face, a voice, and the sudden discovery that the people he had made himself indispensable to had stopped needing him alive.

ELIAS: You don't know what they're asking you to throw away.

Hex didn't answer.

ELIAS: You think this ends with me?

The question stayed in the room.

Hex remembered it later, although he would have preferred not to.

That was when the other man appeared in the hallway.

Not armed in any way that mattered. Not a professional. Just a man who had chosen the wrong night to still be tied to Elias Voss. Hex saw the moment the stranger realized the shape of what he had walked into. Saw the instinctive step backward. Saw the way the situation collapsed into noise and muzzle flash and the sudden, sick finality of a body hitting the floor.

Cassian stopped moving.

For one fractured second the real Hex surfaced hard enough to hurt, the part of him that still felt the weight of every line crossed. Then the shield crushed it back down. There was still a primary target. There was still extraction. There was still the rule that hesitation got people killed.

They finished the job.

The room was never going to become ordinary again, whatever the people writing the report decided to call it.

Hex knew that much.

The others spoke to him. He answered when necessary. He heard his own voice sounding remarkably like the voice of a man who had not just watched something irreparable happen.

He hated that voice.

He needed it.

Somewhere in the confusion, he caught himself looking at Cassian's hands. They were hands that belonged to somebody who had expected to be alive tomorrow. There were small, insignificant details about them that the official version of the night would never contain.

A person could leave behind an entire universe of unfinished things.

Someone would still expect a message. Someone would make coffee for two without thinking. Someone would have a question they would never get to ask.

The network would reduce him to a word.

Incidental.

HEX — HUMAN THOUGHT: Don't call him that.

Nobody heard him.

In the messy containment afterward, while the team worked to make the scene look like something the official world could swallow, Hex's mind snagged on a detail from the file he had almost managed to treat as irrelevant. The photograph. The young woman listed as a possible connected person. The one face in the entire packet that had looked too alive for the world he operated in.

He knew her name from the file. He did not know her in any way that mattered. He only knew that somewhere out there a person was about to lose someone, and that he had been in the room when it happened.

The shield held.

Barely.

The knock

The knock came on an ordinary evening.

Ninja had been rinsing a mug. Sunlight lay across the counter, and her phone was on the table. Three evenings had passed since Cassian had smiled at her from the doorway and told her to lock up behind him.

He had promised to call.

She remembered thinking, as she turned off the tap, that if it was him he was going to receive a thoroughly unnecessary lecture about the existence of phones.

Ninja opened the door to two people with careful voices and practiced sympathy. They told her Cassian had been killed in what appeared to have been a robbery. Wrong place. Wrong time. They were very sorry for her loss.

She stood in the doorway and listened to the words arrange themselves into a story that already felt too smooth. She asked the questions that were expected. They gave answers that closed more doors than they opened. When they left, the apartment was too quiet. The shape of her brother was suddenly everywhere and nowhere at once.

She did not cry where anyone could see.

For a while she stood exactly where they had left her.

She could see the mug in the sink from the hallway. Its handle pointed toward the wall, and she had the ridiculous urge to go back and finish washing it. Something about leaving it half done felt wrong.

Everything else was wrong too.

She picked up her phone.

There was still a message from Cassian about something trivial. She read it. Read it again. The letters remained stubbornly identical, refusing to turn into an explanation.

The screen offered her the little symbol that meant she could call him.

So she did.

And when the familiar recorded greeting answered, she called again.

The sound of his voice was both the only thing she wanted and more than she knew how to bear.

NINJA — THOUGHT: You were supposed to call me. You can't just leave a conversation in the middle like this.

She didn't know who she was talking to.

She didn't know what she expected to happen.

In the days that followed she learned how to nod when people said they were there for her. She learned how to leave conversations before the pity could settle. The official story sat in her chest like something swallowed wrong. She had no proof. Only the quiet, persistent sense that the truth had been edited for her comfort, and that comfort had never been something she trusted.

One afternoon somebody told her that answers wouldn't bring him back.

She remembered looking at the person for a long time before replying.

NINJA: I know.

It was all she said.

It was not all she thought.

Answers wouldn't bring him back. They would, however, let her know which parts of the world she had been wrong about. They would let her stop wondering whether he had been afraid, whether he had needed somebody, whether she had missed something important during their last conversation.

They would let her remember her brother without having to interrogate every memory of him.

But the answers didn't come.

And eventually the people around her started expecting something else.

Progress.

A return to ordinary life.

She became very good at presenting them with the parts of herself that appeared to be moving forward.

The rest she kept.

Five years later, in a village where the rain seemed to have entered into some kind of permanent agreement with the mountains, she found a bar where nobody knew any of that.

She hadn't come there to meet anyone.

Bellwether — the present

In the remote village bar that smelled of wood and cheap whiskey, the past walked through the door wearing her own face.

Hex sat frozen in his corner while the woman from the photograph ordered a drink like the world had never once tried to erase people she loved. The war inside him had already started, the cold voice ordering him to leave, the older, stubborn part of him refusing to look away from the first real thing he had wanted in five years.

He sent the whiskey across the room before the shield could stop him.

When she looked up and their eyes met, something in the air between them pulled taut.

Ninja felt the weight of his gaze like a physical thing. She had come to this place to be lonely on purpose, to sit among strangers who carried no history of her brother's laugh or the hole he left behind. The last thing she needed was a quiet man in the corner looking at her like she had stepped out of a memory he hadn't asked for.

And still she didn't look away.

Curiosity rose despite her best efforts, reluctant, almost annoyed at itself. There was something in the way he held himself, the careful stillness, the sense that he could vanish if he decided to. It made her wonder, against her will, what someone like that was doing alone in a bar like this.

She lifted the glass he had sent and took a slow sip without breaking eye contact. A small, deliberate acceptance that was not the same thing as invitation, but was no longer refusal either.

Hex felt the shift like a door unlocking.

He stood. Crossed the room with the controlled gait of a man who had spent years making sure his body never betrayed urgency. When he stopped a careful distance from her stool, the air between them felt charged enough to taste.

HEX: Mind if I sit?

His voice was low, roughened by disuse in social spaces.

Ninja studied him for a long second. Up close the impression was stronger, the controlled surface, the something heavier moving underneath it. She should have said no. The independent, self-contained version of her had the refusal ready.

Instead she angled her body a fraction, making space.

NINJA: You're already standing there like you will anyway.

The edge in her voice was familiar armor, but it lacked real heat.

HEX: Not if you tell me not to.

She considered that answer. It was inconveniently reasonable.

NINJA: Sit, then.

He sat.

For a moment neither of them spoke. The bar continued its low murmur around them. Somewhere a glass clinked. The ordinary world kept existing, unaware that two people who should never have been in the same room were measuring the distance between them in heartbeats.

NINJA: I'm not in the habit of accepting drinks from strangers.

HEX: I'm not in the habit of sending them.

The honesty in it landed strangely well. She turned the glass slowly between her fingers.

NINJA: Then why tonight?

He could have lied. The shield was excellent at clean, useful lies. Instead he met her eyes and let a fraction of the truth surface.

HEX: Because you walked in and I forgot how to leave.

Something flickered across her face, surprise, then the reluctant spark of interest she hadn't wanted to feel. The loneliness she had been cultivating like a discipline shifted, just slightly, as if it had encountered an answering frequency.

NINJA: Do you always say things like that to people?

HEX: Apparently not. You'd think I'd have practiced by now.

NINJA: You certainly could have.

He looked down at his glass.

HEX: Fair.

She almost smiled.

Almost.

That bothered her more than the drink.

The art of saying nothing

For the next few minutes they discussed absolutely nothing of consequence.

The weather. The village. The road that led through the mountains and seemed to have been designed by somebody who resented the existence of straight lines.

NINJA: I thought the navigation had stopped working. It kept telling me to turn into places that didn't look like roads.

HEX: That's because the local definition of a road is generous.

NINJA: I nearly introduced my car to a tree.

HEX: Did the tree seem interested?

NINJA: Not particularly. Very judgmental, actually.

This time she did smile.

He hadn't anticipated the effect it would have on him.

The woman in the file had never moved. She had never made a joke, never held a glass, never looked at him as though he had said something profoundly stupid. He had been carrying an image of her for years and somehow hadn't understood how little of her that image contained.

Now she was here.

HEX — SURVIVAL THOUGHT: You need to end this.

HEX — HUMAN THOUGHT: In a minute.

He had said that to himself so often in other circumstances that it made him uneasy.

Ninja glanced at the hand resting beside his glass. There was a shallow scratch across one knuckle and the dark residue of something that hadn't quite washed away.

NINJA: What do you do when you're not being mysterious in pubs?

HEX: Repair things.

NINJA: That's vague.

HEX: Things are a broad category.

NINJA: A very impressive job description.

HEX: Motors. Appliances. Whatever Tomas hasn't already declared beyond saving.

NINJA: And are you any good?

HEX: Depends who you ask. The kettles have lodged several formal complaints.

She laughed into her glass.

The sound was unexpected enough that she looked almost offended by it.

Hex had to remind himself not to stare.

NINJA: So you live here?

HEX: Three years.

NINJA: Voluntarily?

HEX: More or less.

She looked at him.

NINJA: That's an interesting answer to a very simple question.

HEX: It was a very interesting three years.

NINJA: You're quite good at not answering things.

HEX: I get a lot of practice.

She gave him a look that made it clear she knew exactly what he was doing.

It should have alarmed him.

Instead he found himself enjoying it.

HEX: What about you?

NINJA: What about me?

HEX: What brings someone here who seems to have such strong opinions about the road?

She traced a drop of water down the side of her glass.

For a moment he thought she would give him the practiced answer. A change of scenery. A new job. The mountains.

NINJA: I wanted somewhere I could be left alone.

He waited.

She watched him waiting.

NINJA: You're supposed to ask why.

HEX: Am I?

NINJA: Most people do.

HEX: Most people are irritating.

She stared at him.

Then she nodded once.

NINJA: That's a surprisingly accurate assessment.

He didn't ask why.

And because he didn't, she found herself wondering whether she wanted to tell him.

Names

The bartender came to collect the empty glasses.

BARTENDER: Another round?

Ninja looked at Hex.

NINJA: Only if I'm paying for yours this time.

HEX: I didn't buy you a debt.

NINJA: Good. I didn't accept one.

He inclined his head.

HEX: Then I'll have whatever she's having.

The bartender went away looking faintly amused.

NINJA: You know, I don't even know your name.

The simplicity of the question struck him harder than it should have.

HEX: Hex.

She blinked.

NINJA: Hex?

HEX: That's usually how that conversation goes.

NINJA: Is it a warning or a name?

HEX: Depends on the day.

NINJA: Right.

She leaned a little closer, studying him with a solemnity that lasted approximately two seconds.

NINJA: Ninja.

He had been prepared for it.

He had practiced his expression a hundred times in circumstances that had nothing to do with this one.

None of that mattered.

Hearing her say her own name, watching her offer it freely, was different from reading it in a file.

HEX — SURVIVAL THOUGHT: Careful.

HEX: Ninja.

NINJA: Don't even start.

HEX: I wasn't going to.

NINJA: You were thinking something.

HEX: I was thinking that between us we've got the naming conventions of a particularly questionable video game.

She laughed.

And just like that, the name became something belonging to this evening.

Not to the people who had written it beneath a photograph.

He wished that distinction could undo what had happened.

The things that stay

The conversation turned without either of them consciously steering it.

Someone near the fireplace mentioned a song playing softly in the background. Ninja glanced over and Hex noticed the change in her expression.

HEX: Not your kind of music?

NINJA: It's not bad. Just reminds me of somebody.

He kept his face still.

HEX: Someone who liked it?

NINJA: Someone who used to sing along to things he definitely didn't know the words to.

A small smile appeared and faded.

NINJA: He was absolutely convinced enthusiasm was a substitute for talent.

HEX: A dangerous philosophy.

NINJA: You have no idea.

Her eyes went back to the glass.

He could feel the moment arriving before he knew what form it would take.

NINJA: My brother.

Hex looked at her.

For the fraction of a second in which he had to choose what to say, the room was gone.

There was only a dark hallway, a body that had stopped moving, and a report in which someone would decide how much of the truth was necessary.

HEX — SURVIVAL THOUGHT: Do not react.

He didn't.

Not visibly.

HEX: Older or younger?

NINJA: Older. Unfortunately for me, he took the position very seriously.

HEX: Protective?

NINJA: Infuriatingly so.

Her smile widened a little.

NINJA: If I did something stupid, he'd tell me. Then he'd help me fix it. Then he'd remind me of it for the next six years.

Hex thought of Roan. Of the borrowed tools, the groceries outside a door, a missing shoe and a flock of deeply unreasonable pigeons.

HEX: That sounds familiar.

NINJA: You have siblings?

HEX: No. A friend who used to behave like one.

NINJA: Used to?

He heard the question she had not asked.

HEX: We lost touch.

She didn't demand a reason.

He was grateful.

NINJA: Sometimes that's worse than having an argument.

HEX: Sometimes.

She took another sip.

NINJA: Cassian would have liked it here, actually.

There it was.

His name.

Not incidental. Not associated person. Not a line in an internal report.

Cassian.

HEX — HUMAN THOUGHT: I know. God, I know.

He couldn't tell her.

Not in a room full of strangers. Not like this. Not when she had no idea what she would be discovering.

And beneath those reasonable objections was a less reasonable truth.

He didn't want this conversation to end.

HEX: He sounds like a good man.

She tilted her head, watching him.

NINJA: He was.

The past tense sat between them.

NINJA: He's been gone five years.

Hex set his glass down.

HEX: I'm sorry.

It was the most inadequate sentence he had ever spoken.

She looked at the bartender, then at the wall behind him.

NINJA: I know people mean well when they say that. It's just that I've heard it a lot.

HEX: Then I won't say it again.

She turned back.

He expected irritation. Instead she looked almost relieved.

NINJA: Thank you.

For a while the only sound between them was the rain, which had diminished to a faint dripping from the roof.

NINJA: You know what's strange?

He shook his head.

NINJA: People eventually stop talking about him. Like there's some invisible agreement that his name is now a sad thing, and mentioning it will ruin my day.

She drew a breath.

NINJA: But I didn't only know him when he died. I knew him when he was alive. He was funny. He was annoying. He couldn't cook the same thing twice without changing the recipe. I want to be able to tell somebody that without watching their face do that little sympathy thing.

Hex looked at her steadily.

HEX: Tell me something funny he did, then.

She was quiet for a moment.

Then her mouth curved.

NINJA: There was this music player...

And she told him.

Not everything. Just a small story about Cassian being completely convinced that something could be fixed by pressing precisely the button she had told him not to press.

He listened.

When she reached the end, he laughed.

It was not a large laugh. It was the sound of a man genuinely amused by somebody he had never been given the opportunity to know.

She hadn't expected that.

Neither had he.

HEX — HUMAN THOUGHT: This is what they took.

He wondered whether he could ever sit in another room without remembering this one.

A little less alone

They started talking.

Not the easy, polished conversation of people trying to impress each other. Something more careful and more real. She gave him the version of herself that traveled light and preferred places where no one knew her name. He gave her the version of himself that had washed up in a quiet village and stayed because the alternative was still running. Neither of them offered the full truth. Both of them sensed the shape of the things being held back, and for reasons neither examined too closely, it didn't make them pull away.

It made the air tighter.

NINJA: Do you ever get bored here?

HEX: Frequently.

NINJA: And you stay anyway?

HEX: Bored is underrated.

She considered that.

NINJA: I think I understand what you mean.

HEX: The worst thing that happened to me last Tuesday was an argument with a toaster.

NINJA: Who won?

HEX: That's not relevant.

NINJA: The toaster.

HEX: It cheated.

She grinned.

He looked down, smiling despite himself.

The easy moments were beginning to accumulate between the difficult ones. He was starting to understand something of her rhythm: the dry observation that arrived when a conversation threatened to become too serious, the way her attention sharpened when she was interested, the slight turn of her head when she suspected an answer was incomplete.

She was learning things too.

He didn't interrupt. He thought about questions before he answered them. When he laughed, the carefully neutral expression disappeared in a way she was finding increasingly distracting.

When someone dropped a glass near the fireplace, his attention snapped toward the sound before anyone else had reacted.

She saw it.

He saw that she'd seen it.

Neither commented.

A moment later she said:

NINJA: You know, for someone who came here to be bored, you don't seem particularly relaxed.

HEX: Neither do you.

She looked at him with narrowed eyes.

NINJA: That's rude.

HEX: Is it wrong?

She opened her mouth.

Closed it.

NINJA: That's irrelevant.

He smiled.

She sighed and looked away, fighting her own smile.

NINJA: I hate that answer.

HEX: I thought you might.

There was a little silence.

Not uncomfortable.

That was what made her notice it.

She was used to the feeling that if she didn't speak, the person beside her would start trying to fill the space. Hex seemed content to let it exist. He watched the diminishing rain outside the window while she took a moment to finish her drink.

She could be quiet without having to explain why.

It felt unfamiliar.

Chemistry didn't arrive as softness. It arrived as recognition. Two people who had spent years building walls suddenly finding the exact frequency that made those walls feel less necessary. Every glance lasted a fraction too long. Every silence carried weight. When she made a dry observation about the bar's attempt at atmosphere, the corner of his mouth moved in something that wasn't quite a smile but still made her stomach dip. When he asked a quiet question about what she was really doing in a place this far from anywhere, she answered more honestly than she had planned to.

NINJA: I think I got tired of being somebody people already knew.

He looked at her.

NINJA: They'd see me coming and I'd know what they were going to ask. Sometimes they'd be right about how I felt. Sometimes they wouldn't. But it never really mattered, because they'd already decided.

HEX: And here?

NINJA: Here nobody knows what I'm supposed to be.

She looked at him, as if she had just realized who she was telling.

NINJA: Well. Almost nobody.

He held her gaze.

HEX: I don't know what you're supposed to be.

The terrible thing was that he meant it.

He knew her name. He knew a handful of details that had no business being in his possession. He knew something about her loss that she had never been allowed to know.

He did not know who she was.

She was telling him that herself.

And he was terrified of the difference.

NINJA: What about you? Did you come here to be someone else?

He took a long time to answer.

HEX: I think I came here because I couldn't keep being who I was.

She didn't look away.

NINJA: Did it work?

HEX: Some days.

For a moment he thought she would ask what he had been.

Instead she rested her elbows on the bar and said:

NINJA: Some days is better than none.

It was such an ordinary observation.

He had no defense against it.

Closing time

Hours blurred.

The bar thinned out around them. The bartender stopped pretending not to notice. At some point Hex realized he had been leaning closer without deciding to. At some point Ninja realized she had stopped calculating exits.

The last two people near the fire put on their coats.

The bartender began collecting chairs from empty tables.

Ninja checked the time.

NINJA: Well, that's unfortunate.

HEX: What is?

NINJA: Apparently I've been talking to you for hours.

HEX: I can apologize if that would help.

NINJA: It probably wouldn't.

She slipped her phone back into her pocket.

The night had acquired an ending she hadn't agreed to.

He felt it too.

He ought to be relieved. The opportunity to do the sensible thing had arrived without requiring him to invent an excuse. They could say goodnight. She could return to her room above the bakery. Tomorrow, if he was careful, he could make certain they never crossed paths again.

HEX — SURVIVAL THOUGHT: Let her go. You have already made this worse.

He looked at her.

She was looking at him.

Neither moved.

NINJA: You look like you're having a very complicated discussion with yourself.

He blinked.

HEX: The other participant is being unreasonable.

NINJA: I know the feeling.

She smiled, softer this time.

He could have left.

He knew he could have left.

HEX: I'm staying at the little hotel up the street for a couple of nights. My place has a plumbing problem. If you wanted to keep talking somewhere quieter...

He left the rest unfinished.

There was no attempt to make the choice for her. No assumption that because she had stayed here this long, she owed him another minute.

She studied him.

NINJA: You're a repairman with a plumbing problem?

HEX: I repair motors. The pipes have unionized against me.

She shook her head.

NINJA: That's genuinely embarrassing.

HEX: I know.

He waited.

The independent part of her raised a final, half-hearted objection.

She considered it.

She considered the fact that she could go home, and that he seemed perfectly prepared to let her.

Then she made her own decision.

NINJA: All right. But if your conversation deteriorates, I'm leaving.

HEX: Reasonable.

NINJA: And no more whiskey.

HEX: Also reasonable.

They stood.

The bartender gave them a small nod that suggested he had witnessed this sort of evening before and had learned the considerable wisdom of keeping his observations to himself.

Ninja put on her jacket.

Hex held the door.

The air outside was cool enough to make them both draw a deeper breath.

The room

They walked through the cool night air side by side, not touching, the space between them charged with everything they weren't saying. The pavements were still wet, and a light in the bakery window illuminated the empty street.

NINJA: It's actually quite pretty here when it's stopped trying to drown you.

HEX: Give it a minute.

She laughed quietly.

He wanted to remember this walk.

That frightened him almost more than anything that had happened in the bar.

At the hotel the hallway was narrow and dim.

They stopped outside his door.

He turned toward her.

HEX: You sure?

She looked at him for a moment.

NINJA: I know where the door is, Hex.

His expression softened.

HEX: I know.

She took his hand.

NINJA: Then stop trying to decide for me.

He opened the door.

Inside there was a lamp, a narrow window, two glasses beside a water jug and the impersonal neatness of a room that belonged to nobody in particular.

For a few minutes they actually did continue talking.

She stood beside the window, looking at the sleeping village. He poured them water.

NINJA: You really are terrible at this mysterious stranger thing.

HEX: I thought I was doing reasonably well.

NINJA: You've told me about kettles, plumbing and pigeons.

HEX: I have a rich inner life.

She turned around, smiling.

He put the glass down.

There was a moment in which the distance between them felt like something neither had agreed to maintain anymore.

She stepped closer.

He stopped making jokes.

NINJA: Hex.

She said it quietly.

He looked at her, and for once there was nothing prepared behind his eyes.

They kissed.

And the rest of the night belonged to them alone.

Later, tangled in sheets that smelled of someone else's detergent, sleep took them in the heavy, unguarded way of people who had temporarily set down everything they carried.

Dawn

Dawn was only a pale suggestion at the edges of the curtains when the knock came.

Three hard, deliberate blows against the door.

Hex was on his feet before the third one finished, body already between Ninja and the entrance, every protective instinct roaring back to full volume. The shield that had softened for a few stolen hours locked into place with the force of a vault door.

Ninja pushed herself up on one elbow, heart slamming, the real world crashing back in through the cheap wood of the hotel door.

Whoever stood on the other side knocked again.

Harder.

End of Episode 3.
