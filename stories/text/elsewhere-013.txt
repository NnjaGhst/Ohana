# Elsewhere — Episode 13: The Back of the Receipt

### Previously on Elsewhere

Martin Pike came to the village hall and asked to borrow a charger. He said he had a copy of a note Elise wrote to the Directorate. Ninja asked him to give it to the officer and went outside to eat soup with Hex.

Later the archive found three receipts under Elise’s reference number. The officer asked Ninja to come to the print shop. There was something on the back of one receipt that was easier to show.

### Chapter One — Three Pieces of Paper

The officer reached the print shop before it opened. Mara knew this because she came downstairs with her tea and saw a woman standing on the pavement holding a folder flat between her forearms, as though the wind might try to turn the pages for her.

Rose put out the stools. Ninja called Hex from the pavement and asked him to come along. He arrived with a spot of blue paint on his sleeve, because he had been mending the chair for the upstairs room and painting over the one place where it had begun to rust.

“You’ve got a bit on you.”

“I was going to become a painter. I think this has saved me some time.”

He wiped it with the side of his thumb. It became a larger spot.

The officer put three copies on the counter, leaving a space between them. The first was for a fare on the old ferry. The second was for a small locker at the station. The third was for three hours in a meeting room Elise had used on the far side of town. The entries were dated within a few minutes of one another, but the receipt for the meeting room had been returned with the wrong time cut off the bottom.

The officer turned it over. On the back Elise had written a brief note, in the unremarkable pencil of somebody writing against a wall.

If they ask where I went, tell them the last place you actually saw me.

There was a second sentence, much smaller.

Please leave the other chair where it is.

“Were these in the envelope before the fire?”

“We have a delivery date on the envelope,” the officer said. “That’s one thing I want to check against a second record. What I can tell you is that a station employee recognized the handwriting. She said Elise wrote messages on pieces of paper she thought people would keep.”

Ninja held the edge of the counter. Of course Elise did. Ninja still had a supermarket coupon somewhere that said take a left at the yellow door because Elise disliked explaining directions twice.

“Who was supposed to read it?”

“That’s what I’d like to know.”

For a moment Ninja had felt very near the answer. She had to get used to it still being somewhere she couldn’t reach.

Mara had been listening from the doorway of the back room. She came through to put three small weights on the corners of the pages where they had started to lift in the draft. They were cut from the same pale grey stone, and she had drawn a different dot on each one. She said Rose called them her three expensive paperweights. They had come from a broken slab in the yard and cost nothing at all.

When the officer moved the middle receipt, Ninja saw a much older shadow on it, the mark of a staple that had been removed. At some point it had been part of a group of papers that belonged together. She tried to see the pages as Elise would have seen them, lying across the counter while the office was still open and another person was waiting to buy a ticket. She thought Elise would have been impatient with herself for needing to write quickly. Her sister liked to finish a sentence, even if it happened to be an argument.

Hex had moved near the window, where there was more light. He could see the paper from there well enough to recognise the old stamp from the station. He remembered a clerk who used to turn the stamp over in the evening so that the date wouldn’t smear across the counter. There had been a little stack of paper beside it for testing which number came up. The clerk would put the test slips in the recycling before she left. If someone had seen a particular date stamped on that envelope, he thought there might be another place to look for it too.

He told the officer about the stamp. She asked for the clerk’s name. He hadn’t known it, but he could remember the particular evening when she covered for another employee because she told him to come back for the last train if the bus didn’t show up. He gave the officer the details he knew. Then he stopped, and let her decide what she might do with them.

Ninja turned the nearest little grey weight a quarter turn. She liked that it stayed the same shape when she did that. The pencil on the back of the receipt had meant something before she came into the room. It would mean something when she went out. There was no advantage in making up an answer before there was one to hear.

### Chapter Two — The Woman with the Bicycle

Rose brought out an old delivery book. She found a name by the date on the studio receipt. Margaret. No last name. Under it someone had crossed out a telephone number and written a time. Rose touched the line with one finger.

“Margaret was the woman who worked at the bicycle shop. She borrowed my stand for a window display. The picture you showed me, with the milk bottle. I thought she had taken that picture because of the place it stood when it was printed. Now I remember why.”

The officer asked where they might find Margaret. Rose said she was still at the bicycle shop on Friday mornings, doing repairs if she felt like it. This morning was Friday. They rang ahead. The woman on the phone told them to come just before twelve because she didn’t like to be late for lunch.

On the way Hex and Ninja stopped at the crossing. Some rain had come without either of them noticing when it began. A dog stood under the shop awning with its lead disappearing into a doorway. The dog regarded the rain as a matter somebody ought to deal with.

“Do you ever think she was trying to tell everybody little things so the big thing could be found later?”

Hex thought about it long enough for the light to change and change back.

“I think she wanted somebody to remember the parts nobody was going to write down.”

“I think I’m finally getting better at hearing those.”

At the bicycle shop a woman in a yellow apron was oiling a little wheel no bigger than a dinner plate. She put it down before she asked them in. She had heard the name from Rose, and when she saw Ninja she looked at her for a second too long.

“The hair isn’t the same,” the woman said. “The way you’re standing is.”

“What way is that?”

“Like you’re deciding how slowly you want me to tell you.”

Ninja nodded, grateful to be understood without having had to find a sentence for it.

The bicycle shop had a counter the height of a low wall. All the receipts were in trays within reach of the floor. Margaret said she had set it up after she caught herself spending half her day looking at the ceiling because she could never find the steps. If a tall customer wanted something from the top one, she asked them to get it down.

Hex looked around the room while she took off her apron. There was a blue child’s bicycle on a hook and a saddle balanced on a stool. Somewhere he couldn’t see, a small radio was playing a song with a bass line he remembered from an old bus journey. He couldn’t remember the name of the song. Ordinarily he might have asked Margaret. Today he let the song finish while she put the apron over the back of a chair.

She showed Ninja the spot where the old bike stood before it was collected. It was darker than the floor around it because the rain used to run from the wheel and sit there in the rubber mat. Ninja looked at the narrow space. She could picture Elise leaning the bicycle to the left, checking it twice with one finger on the handlebar, then leaving it without looking back again.

Margaret asked if Ninja wanted a cup of tea. She’d learned to ask that before telling people a long story. If they said no she could tell them the story in one go. If they said yes there was somewhere to stop while she went to fetch the kettle.

Ninja accepted. While Margaret filled the kettle behind the counter, an older man came in with a pump that wouldn’t close. Hex got up to let him past. The pump worked after Margaret took a small piece of paper out of the nozzle. Nobody knew how it had got in there. The man said he’d be more interested if he hadn’t spent four pounds on the pump yesterday.

When he left, Margaret brought the tea over in shop mugs with a slightly chipped glaze. She put Ninja’s down on the side of the counter where she could reach it without leaning across the officer’s folder. Then she sat and looked at Ninja directly. There was time now for what she wanted to say.

### Chapter Three — Where She Was Going

Margaret remembered Elise’s last afternoon because the bicycle had come in with a stiff brake and a bottle of milk still in the basket. Elise gave her the milk on the condition that she could leave the bike there overnight. She had another place to be and thought she ought to go on foot.

“She used to hold her elbows this way when she was cold,” Margaret said, showing Ninja. “That’s how I know she was cold. She claimed she felt fine.”

Elise had given her the third receipt to put in the stationery tray for the station courier. The two others were there already, sent in by a clerk earlier in the day. Margaret remembered Elise turning the paper over and writing on it at the front counter. She thought the person who might ask was Iona. It was possible she meant more than one person.

“Where did you see her last?”

“Right in front of the shop. She put her scarf up before she went. Then she crossed the road and stopped when she saw that somebody was trying to walk a bicycle down the steps. She went back to help them.”

Margaret had been thinking about her own lunch. She’d seen Elise leave down the street afterward and assumed she would come back the following morning. Nothing about the afternoon had told her to take another look.

Ninja could have asked what shoes Elise had been wearing. She could have asked whether she waved. For a few seconds she could have found enough questions to keep the afternoon there until it got dark. Instead she asked what happened to the bike.

“I kept it for a little while,” Margaret said. “Then I took it to the upstairs landing at the print shop, because Elise said I could leave it inside if I needed the room. I think Rose still has it in the back.”

The officer made a note. Margaret agreed to tell her the story again for the record. Ninja watched them go into the back room and hoped they would both remember it was all right to stop for lunch.

Hex stood beside her and waited.

“I think I’d like to see the bicycle, if you have time.”

“I have time.”

They left Margaret and the officer at the bicycle shop. Outside, Hex stopped to buy a bottle of water. Ninja watched him ask the person at the till for two cups and come back with one cup and no bottle because the person had a tap round the back and wanted to know what they needed to buy water for. Hex said he hadn’t been able to think of a reason either.

Ninja drank from the cup first. She found she could swallow better than she could talk. They walked the short stretch to the print shop without trying to fill it with conversation. At the end of the lane there was a delivery lorry with its hazards on. Hex waited until it moved, and they crossed in the patch of still road behind it.

Ninja thought about Elise walking back to help somebody down the steps. All this time she’d been trying to imagine her sister’s final movements as a straight line toward the warehouse, because that was where she wanted the trail to end. Instead there had been a milk bottle, a repair, a person holding a bicycle on the steps. Elise could have been much later if somebody had asked her to stay and talk. There would still have been a person waiting in the same room across town, expecting her not to be late on purpose.

They reached Rose’s gate. Hex opened it and waited while Ninja tried to remember if she had put the key somewhere she could find it. It was still in the front pocket, where she had put it when there was more room in her hands.

The rain had stopped. From inside the yard somebody had hung two cloths on the rail to dry. Ninja could picture Rose hanging them there in the morning when she thought it might clear up, as though the rest of the day was still something she was allowed to take a little chance on.

### Chapter Four — The Patient Chair

The bicycle was under a grey cover in Rose’s yard. It had a small wicker basket and a patch of rust where a bell had come loose. Ninja turned the handlebars gently and heard the rubber of one tyre peel from the wet pavement. There was nothing remarkable about the sound. She’d been waiting to hear it for a long time without knowing.

She and Hex carried it through to the back wall, where the roof overhung the yard a little. The mechanic in him looked at the brake. He didn’t touch it until she asked.

“Could you make it work?”

“Yes. Whenever you decide you want to ride it.”

It was the answer she needed. She went back upstairs with him and found that Rose had already left the repaired folding chair by the window. Hex told her that it folded almost flat now and had a tendency to turn its feet inward if someone dragged it across the floor.

“So it has character.”

“It has a warranty of less than an afternoon.”

She sat in it anyway. There was enough light for both of them. Hex moved the older chair closer, and the piece of cardboard fell out again. They left it where it was until the little wobble made one of them laugh.

The officer sent a message at the end of the afternoon. Margaret’s account set the time of Elise’s last walk much earlier than the time written on the copy Pike offered. The next step was to look for the original with the station archive. There was still plenty to do. Ninja read the message and sent back a thank you.

Then she showed Hex the last photograph from her phone. It was a picture of two chairs beside the same window. All you could see of the village through it was a little stretch of roof and a piece of the sky.

“I think she’d have liked the second chair.”

“The folding one?”

“She’d have said I could have it as long as I didn’t put it in the wrong place.”

They sat until the light moved past the feet of both chairs. Ninja left the window just the way she had found it. At the door, she hung the green key on a small hook beside the frame, and took the white one with her. If somebody arrived later, it would be possible to let them in.
