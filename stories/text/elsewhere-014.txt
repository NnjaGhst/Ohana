# Elsewhere — Episode 14: The Station Copy

### Previously on Elsewhere

Margaret remembered Elise bringing her bicycle in with a stiff brake and a bottle of milk in the basket. On the back of a receipt Elise left a request. If anyone asked where she went, they were to say the last place they had actually seen her. The officer found that Margaret’s account put Elise on the street much earlier than the time on Martin Pike’s copy. She asked the station archive to look for the original. Ninja and Hex found Elise’s old bicycle in the print-shop yard and left it somewhere out of the rain.

### Chapter One — A Brake That Lets Go

On Saturday the forecast said it would rain at eleven. At nine twenty, Hex put his coat on to walk over to the print shop and found water running down the inside of the hood. He couldn’t prove that anyone had taken the coat out of the building while he was asleep. He dried the hood with his sleeve and went out in it anyway.

In the yard the bicycle stood against the line of the wall, where someone had chalked a little mark by the tyre. Rose said it was how she remembered where she’d seen it last. If it went missing, she’d be able to offer the police an exact location, give or take a hundred feet.

Ninja came down carrying two mugs and a dry cloth. Her hair was high enough to clear the collar of her coat, except for one loose strand she kept blowing away from the milk in her tea. She set the mugs on the sill and told him the archive had called. The officer had arranged for them to meet the station clerk at ten. They had enough time to see what the brake was doing.

Hex asked her to lift the front wheel by the handlebars. He turned it a quarter turn and squeezed. The wheel stopped. He let go of the lever, pushed the wheel again, and it refused to move. Ninja hadn’t known there was a particular sound made by a greying piece of rubber being asked to do something it didn’t wish to do.

“So she could stop whenever she liked. She just couldn’t start.”

“That’s why I wish you wouldn’t let her write the advert for it.”

Ninja ran a fingertip along the top of the front mudguard. Margaret had said Elise stopped the bicycle by putting her boot down on the street. Ninja couldn’t pretend she didn’t feel like trying it herself. She also wanted to see it glide a little without somebody wrestling it down. She said they could be sensible first and badly behaved later.

Hex rested the front wheel on an empty box. When he took the old cable out, he left it lying along the wall so the bend in it would stay as it was. Ninja asked why he didn’t throw it on the floor with everything else. He said he wanted to know how long it was when he put a new one in. She watched him straighten his back before he stood up. He was going to need another minute, so she went to move the tea nearer.

From the first-floor window Rose asked if anyone was going to the station in the next half hour. A customer had left a scarf on the counter and left a telephone number that belonged to a takeaway restaurant. The woman working at the station knew who it was. Hex asked how she could possibly know who it was. Rose said she knew all the people who left things in town and rang from somewhere else to say they never had them.

Ninja tried the lever after Hex fed the new cable through. The catch released with a clean little click. She did it again to make sure. Hex put both hands in his pockets so she could turn the wheel by herself. It ran for long enough that she forgot she was waiting for it to stop.

“I want to do the first ride when there’s somewhere to go.”

Hex told her to pick somewhere with a good view from the bottom of a hill. He tightened a screw on the lever by a fraction and stood the bicycle up beside the white door. Ninja picked up the two mugs, one empty and one so cold a skin had formed over the last of the milk. She took them both inside. When she came back for her coat, Hex had left the bicycle uncovered.

### Chapter Two — The Side Entrance

The police officer was standing under the station canopy with a paper cup that had started to soften where she held it. The front entrance had two automatic doors. One opened in the rain and the other waited for somebody walking in the opposite direction. Ninja chose to go round the side, where she could look down the ramp and see the platform before she walked onto it.

Hex carried Rose’s scarf in the bag with the striped handles. He’d worked out that if he walked down the ramp slowly enough he could count the seams where the old paving joined the new. At the third seam there was still a small cut in the wall. He showed Ninja where the clerk used to wedge the stop from the side door when she went home.

Partway down the ramp, Ninja could see that the platform clock was a minute slow. She held up her phone to check. It was exactly a minute and seven seconds. She was trying to decide which one she’d trust if she was about to miss a train when a man behind her checked a different clock and ran by them without looking at either of theirs.

“I’d like to see what she’s found before I see what Pike thinks it means.”

The officer said she could arrange that. She told them the clerk’s name was Evelyn. Evelyn had recognized the receipt from a photograph. She remembered putting some station papers in a cardboard file by hand before a new system came in. The officer’s job today was to see what was inside the file and where the papers had been between then and now. Ninja could stay for exactly as much of that as she wished.

Hex turned at the bottom to look for the door he remembered. There was still a round mark in the paint where someone had swept the old bell pull aside. When he touched it, the door swung inward. The woman behind it wore a cream cardigan and had a pair of reading glasses on a red cord. She looked down at the bag and said the scarf belonged to one of the conductors. She had seen him buy it because the shopkeeper took the security tag off with a piece of string.

She set the scarf on a shelf with a green water bottle that somebody had drawn a face on. Ninja could hear music from somewhere behind the shutters. It was a children's song played on a radio quietly enough that nobody could tell if the tuning was off. Evelyn had borrowed a room from the station office for the morning. She made them walk round the counter so they wouldn’t have to reach across it.

There was a staff photograph on the counter in a plastic holder. Ninja saw that a piece of the picture in the corner had stuck to the previous holder and torn away. She thought it was the kind of damage that came from deciding things were going to last forever while they still looked new. Evelyn said the young man without a forehead in the photograph had retired with his forehead in good condition.

He would have been able to tell them which files were kept. They had always had two racks. A copy for the office went into the upper one. A copy for the station stayed lower down so that someone on the spot could find it. Evelyn demonstrated the order with a thick rule book and a time sheet. Ninja noticed a dent in the red tabletop where a thumb would land if somebody set the rack out of the way.

She could see Hex recognise the pattern as soon as Evelyn showed him. She asked if he’d ever looked something up in the lower tray. Once, he said, when a man waited by the closed door after the last train. A clerk found the man a seat and the number of a taxi. Hex had gone to take the man a cup of tea and found him asleep before he could ask his name. It was the first thing about the station Hex thought to tell her, and she was glad he did.

### Chapter Three — Evelyn’s Counter

Evelyn carried out the file with a piece of cardboard tucked behind it. She kept the cardboard underneath until she put it down. At the top of the cover there was a clean pink mark where tape had been peeled away. At one corner the box had been rounded by the weight of all the other boxes sitting on it.

The officer took one picture of it shut and one as Evelyn opened it. Ninja held her hands by her sides. She was glad to have something to do with them because for a second she was sure that if she put her fingers on the cardboard she would knock something out of order.

Evelyn took a booking book out first. She told them she could show them the meeting room entry and the time at which its key had been collected. If they looked only at the booking book, they might think that was when the meeting started. It was when somebody walked down from the office and asked for the brass key with the red tag. People booked hours in advance. After the railway started charging by the hour, they generally collected the key as late as they could.

She moved the book to the light. The time written in the margin was twenty past four. Under the amount there was the name of the person who picked up the key. M. Pike. Evelyn said she knew his handwriting. She wasn’t telling them which Pike had attended the meeting. She could tell them which man stood on the other side of her counter and signed himself in.

“Did you see him go to the room?”

“I saw him leave the counter with the key in his hand. I can tell you which way he turned when he went through the door.”

Hex looked up from the file. Evelyn said he’d turned left. The room was to the right if you came out through the old arch. There were two ways to get to it. The other way took you past the ferry announcement board. If a person wanted to find out when the next boat left, that was the way they often went. It was also the way to a better coffee machine. Evelyn had never known which of those things made Pike go left.

With the book open under the light, she slid out a folded carbon sheet. At first it looked as dark as the blue paper Rose used when she wanted to make two invoices at once. Evelyn said she couldn’t read all that corner anymore. She asked Ninja to stand beside the lamp and look down from where she was.

Ninja walked round the counter. The officer moved to give her room. What came through first was a line drawn by someone who had pressed down very hard. The paper was pale just on the far side of it. Beneath it were the marks of a time and a set of initials. Above it the usual printed statement said the key had been returned without an incident.

“Is that what you usually wrote when you got it back?”

“If the door worked and the tag was still on. It meant nobody needed to go out with a screwdriver before closing.”

There had been a fire later that night. The phrase had been quoted in a later summary which said the station attendant had reported no incident. Evelyn was hearing that for the first time from the officer, who had a copy of the summary in her folder. Her hand stayed on the book after she finished reading.

Hex was the first to move. He asked if she wanted the windows shut. She said no and then asked for one to be shut anyway because of the draft on her neck. He closed the higher one. There was still enough of an opening to hear a train slow down on the other side of the wall.

Evelyn said she wanted to look at the exact wording once more. She asked the officer to put the summary on the table, beside her book, where she could read both without having to remember which line came first.

### Chapter Four — Under the Clock

Ninja had seen what she came for. She also had a feeling she might need to see it again before she could tell anybody about it. She asked if it was all right to go outside. The officer said she would come and find her after she and Evelyn looked at the rest.

The air on the platform felt colder than the air farther down the street. Hex came after her with two tea cups tucked together by the sides. He gave her the one without a lid. She could have asked for a lid and gone back inside to fetch one. She kept it without a lid because the steam warmed the place where her hand met the cup.

A pigeon landed under the clock. It looked at a wet footprint which belonged to a much bigger creature, then set one foot straight into it. Ninja heard the sound of a carriage door from farther along the platform. She watched a woman set out a folding ramp to let a passenger off. The woman waited at the bottom of the ramp until the passenger had finished deciding where he wanted to go.

Her phone buzzed. Her aunt had found the letter she wanted and sent pictures of both sides of the envelope. There was a second envelope in the pictures, looking very ordinary. Her aunt said she’d stuck a photograph of Elise inside it because she was frightened that she would put the things from that month in the same bag and forget what any of them looked like. She asked Ninja whether she wanted the photograph too.

“I’m going to say yes. I’d like to see which one she chose.”

Hex said he could hold the tea while she typed. She offered him the tea and then the phone because she wanted to hear the message a second time. Her aunt had used a voice note. Ninja’s earphones were at the house. Hex held the phone at shoulder height as if he had always intended to stand in the middle of a station and listen to somebody find a photograph.

At the end of the message came a little creak of cupboard doors. Her aunt said there was a shop down Bridge Street selling the same long matches if she remembered right. She hadn’t found them at the supermarket. If Ninja had any use for a spare box of matches, she could put one in the bag with the photograph. It would give her something to remember to do with her hands before she posted it.

Ninja told Hex to answer yes to the matches as well. At the window across the tracks she saw a station attendant curl the corner of a paper notice in two fingers and put it straight again. He would be able to go home after this and tell his family there had been nothing worth mentioning at work. She hoped he got all the way through his shift saying that.

She told Hex she was glad Evelyn wanted to see both sheets before she said anything. A year ago, she would have wanted Evelyn to say the summary was wrong the moment she saw it. What she wanted now was for Evelyn to walk past this clock in a week’s time and remember exactly what she agreed to tell people. That might be a lot to ask of one afternoon.

“Do you think they’ll have to change what the report says?”

“They might have to explain where the line came from. That’s a good thing to have to explain out loud.”

Ninja kept the cup where it was until she felt some heat through the seam. Farther down the platform a young man tried to zip his case while he stood on one foot and would have lost both shoes if the porter hadn’t reached for the handle. Everybody around them pretended they hadn’t seen it. The clock went on losing its minute. Nobody moved to correct it before the next train arrived.

### Chapter Five — The Second Copy

Evelyn came looking for them without her cardigan. She had left it on the back of a chair because a button kept catching on the arm. Hex gave her his empty cup to throw away only after she offered twice.

“I was looking for the money tray after you went out,” Evelyn said. “I pulled out a whole drawer.”

She brought back a second envelope. It had adhesive in a curled strip where somebody had opened and shut it more than once. Evelyn put it in the same place as the booking book. The officer made a note of the drawer before anyone touched the flap. Hex could hear the printer next door make three copies and go quiet.

Inside was the office’s typed cover note, the station copy with two signatures, and a little slip with the late ferry’s change in operating time. The typed note listed Elise as the person responsible for returning the key before six. On the station copy the second person who signed out a key was Iona, at five past five. Her signature was followed by a cramped note that she had given her key straight back after the room was found locked.

Evelyn could remember hearing it now. Someone had knocked. The person who answered said Pike still had the key and the room wasn’t open. Iona came back with the spare key in her coat pocket and said she’d see if he was by the window. She returned less than five minutes later. Evelyn was careful to say she didn’t remember who was standing by the window.

Ninja saw the exact place on the cover note where somebody had added Elise's name in a different spacing. Evelyn pointed out that her paper showed a separate part of the transaction. She hadn’t signed anything that said Elise collected the key. There was space between the two signatures. It had been useful all that time just to leave the space alone.

“Can you send a copy of yours to Iona, with the officer’s number?”

The officer said she would ask Iona whether she wanted to see it first. She told Evelyn they might be able to find out who added the name from the office version, and that someone would need to keep the pages together until then. Evelyn found a clear sleeve under the counter. The booking book was too wide for it, so she used the sleeve to hold the single sheet and put the folder over the top.

Hex asked about the ferry slip. Evelyn said it was a temporary shift change. That afternoon the last crossing had left earlier than the station notice said. She was meant to tear down the old notice once the new one was on the board. There were three places she could have put the notice and only two had been checked.

“Did someone miss the boat?” Hex asked.

Evelyn turned the little ferry slip over and tried to remember. A woman had come to find a man called Sal later in the day. He worked the slipway. She carried a lunch bag for him and didn’t want to take it onto the boat in case he was working on the near side. Evelyn couldn’t remember if the woman made it across. She thought she was fairly sure Sal had finished his shift by the time the woman arrived.

Ninja heard the names and times go into the officer’s notebook. She gave herself a moment before she asked whether she could have a copy of the line about Elise. The officer made one for her with the numbers above and below it left visible. Ninja folded it in half without covering a word. The important part was small enough to fit in her hand with the photograph when it came.

### Chapter Six — A Room with Hours

On the way back into town they saw a work van stop so a mother and child could get past a broken paving stone. The driver waited through the whole of the child's decision about which side to go. Hex said the man would go far in life if he could stand there looking that patient while it was nearly lunch.

Ninja took the white key from her pocket at Rose’s door. She thought of Evelyn using both hands to bring out the old cardboard folder. It made her want to open her own room as if somebody had asked to see something she’d kept. As they went upstairs, she carried the station copy on the flat of one hand with her palm underneath.

Hex asked if he should put the kettle on. She said he should find three mugs. Rose had come up behind them with a little stack of unused display cards for the print shop. There was a hole at each end where the cards could be tied to a shelf. Ninja wanted Rose to come in long enough to see what the place looked like with the table moved nearer the light.

The photograph of Elise was still on the windowsill. Ninja had set it there after Iona left. For a second she thought somebody must have put it back exactly right, because the light rested on the pale button in the picture. Then she remembered how little the picture had moved. It was the rest of the afternoon that had changed.

“I want people to be able to come up. A few people, for tea or to look at something. Could we start with Thursday?”

Rose asked if she meant people from the case. Ninja thought she might, at first. There were other things people wanted to talk about, and there would be tea whenever there was somebody there to let a person in. She thought it mattered that people could come and see which sort of meeting it was going to be before they told her why they'd come.

Rose showed her how to put the notice on the inside of the glass so the people in the street wouldn’t be able to turn it round for fun. Ninja said that would be very useful if they had the kind of visitors who could climb to the glass from the street. Rose looked down the stairs toward the counter and said she was more worried about the ones who could reach it from halfway up the steps.

Hex found a blue pen in the drawer and gave it to her. She wrote Thursday, four to six, then opened the upstairs door and looked at the card from the landing. Her handwriting took up less room than she’d thought. Under the hours she put knock if the door is closed. The blue line wobbled and ended well short of the far side.

“If I change my mind at five, could you help me lock up?”

Hex said he could. He brought the first mug over and held it until Ninja had both hands around the card again. She tied the string through the two holes and hung it on the hook. From inside the room it looked as if the notice had been there for an hour already. She put the station copy in the drawer, where it would be safe if the room filled up.

They sat down to have the first of the tea. The third mug was Rose’s. It seemed a fair way to start, with a person who’d actually been asked and who still had to go downstairs before her customers began wondering where she was.

After Rose left, a bicycle bell sounded on the street. Ninja had the brief conviction it was her bicycle. She looked out and saw a man pedalling away in a bright red cap. The bell rang again when he reached the corner. She thought about how far a person could be heard after they’d gone out of sight.
