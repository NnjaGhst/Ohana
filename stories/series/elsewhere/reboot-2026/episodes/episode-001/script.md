# Elsewhere
## Episode 1 — A Place That Doesn't Remember
### Reboot manuscript · Draft 1
**Narration:** Wade. **Ninja's spoken dialogue:** Hallie, at 90% volume. **Status:** draft, not yet recorded or published.

### The Road to Bellwether

By the time Ninja reached Bellwether, the rain had settled into the kind of steady drizzle that didn't feel dramatic enough to deserve a weather warning.

It simply got into everything.

The hills around the village disappeared and reappeared behind the mist. Old stone walls followed the narrow road between fields. An elderly sheep watched her car pass with an expression of deep personal disappointment.

"Yeah," Ninja muttered at it. "You and me both."

She had been driving for hours, with two bags in the back, one paperback on the passenger seat, and a cardboard box she hadn't managed to put away. The box held a chipped blue mug, a packet of photographs, and a key that no longer opened anything she owned.

At the village entrance, a weathered wooden sign announced BELLWETHER — WELCOME.

She almost laughed.

It was such an innocent word. Welcome. As though arriving somewhere was the difficult part.

She found the building above the bakery on her second attempt. The owner, a woman called Ada, opened the door before Ninja had managed to ring the bell.

"You must be the new guest."

"That depends," Ninja said. "Does the room have a bed?"

"Usually."

"Then yes. Definitely me."

Ada gave her an assessing look, but it wasn't the look Ninja had grown accustomed to. No careful lowering of the eyes. No flicker of recognition at a surname. No searching her face for evidence of whether today was a good day to mention her brother.

Just a woman in a cardigan holding a door.

"Upstairs, then. You'll find the kettle by the window. Mind the third step. It makes a noise like a dying goose."

"I've lived with worse."

"Oh, haven't we all."

Ninja carried her bags up. The room was small, clean, and slightly crooked. A quilt covered the bed. The window looked over a wet street with a florist, a repair shop, and the dark-red door of a pub at the far corner.

She placed her things down.

She didn't unpack. Not her clothes, not the box, not even the phone charger.

Unpacking made a place feel like a decision. Right now, the best she could manage was an address.

She pulled on her black jacket, checked the room key twice, and went back out.

Unfamiliar silence had a habit of bringing familiar voices with it.

And hers already sounded like Cassian's.

### Before There Were Secrets

The first time Cassian taught her to throw a punch, Ninja was eight years old.

It happened behind the building where they lived, on a patch of cracked concrete that somebody optimistically called a courtyard.

She had come home with blood on her knee, dirt on her sleeve, and the fierce conviction that she had won an argument with a boy twice her size.

Cassian, thirteen and already wearing the tired expression of somebody responsible for too many things, sat her on the bottom step.

"Tell me what happened."

"He was being an idiot."

"That's a condition, not an explanation."

"He took my bag."

"And you?"

"I took it back."

Cassian glanced at the split skin on her knuckles.

"With your face?"

"Shut up."

He laughed, despite trying not to. Then he cleaned her knee with water from a bottle, tearing a strip from an old cloth when they ran out of proper bandages.

She hissed when the cold water touched the scrape.

"Hold still, menace."

"You're pulling my leg off."

"Yes. That's the treatment. All doctors do it."

When he was done, he crouched in front of her and held out his hands.

"Okay. Here's the important part. If somebody bigger than you starts trouble, you don't have to stand there and prove you're fearless."

"I wasn't scared."

"Then you should've been. Fear can be useful."

She hated that answer.

Cassian tilted his head.

"Look at me, Nin. Being brave isn't the same as being stupid."

"You're stupid."

"Frequently. That's how I've gathered all this wisdom."

He showed her how to stand with her feet steady and how to move away from someone trying to grab her. He made her practise until she stopped trying to win and started paying attention.

"Good," he said at last.

"Can I punch you now?"

"Absolutely not."

She punched his arm anyway.

He looked gravely offended.

That evening they ate toast for dinner because the cupboards were nearly empty. Cassian put the better-looking pieces on her plate and insisted he preferred the burnt ones.

Years later, she would remember that lie more clearly than almost anything he ever taught her.

Their home wasn't always dangerous. Sometimes somebody cooked and the radio played old songs. Other nights, voices came through the walls and doors slammed hard enough to rattle the glasses.

Ninja learned to listen for footsteps. Cassian learned to turn up the television and invent a game whenever the footsteps became too loud.

On one particular night, when she was eleven and he was sixteen, the house finally went quiet long after midnight.

She found him sitting on the floor outside her bedroom.

"Why are you out here?"

"Guard duty."

"From what?"

"Spiders. Very serious threat."

"There aren't any spiders."

"Exactly. You're welcome."

She sat beside him. They said nothing for a while.

Eventually she leaned against his shoulder, and he rested his cheek on her head.

"One day," he said quietly, "we're going to have a place with doors that only slam when we want them to."

"Why would we want them to slam?"

"Because you'll be annoyed with me."

"I'm already annoyed with you."

"See? We've got a promising future."

She slept easier that night.

Not because the world had become safe, but because someone had stayed outside the door.

### The Person Who Stayed

Cassian wasn't an angel.

He forgot appointments. He borrowed things and returned them in odd places. He could turn a five-minute errand into a two-hour adventure, usually involving somebody's broken car and an extremely unconvincing explanation.

When Ninja was fifteen, he forgot the afternoon they'd planned to see a film together. She waited at the cinema entrance for forty minutes before walking home alone.

He arrived later, wet from rain, carrying two paper bags of takeaway and a look that was halfway between guilt and fear of what she'd do to him.

"I know," he said immediately.

"Wonderful. Saves me explaining."

"The van at work broke down."

"Did the van also break your phone?"

"No. That was me being an idiot."

"Finally, a diagnosis."

He put the bags on the table.

"I was wrong. I'm sorry."

Ninja held on to her anger for another few seconds, mostly out of principle.

Then she opened the bag.

"Did you get extra chips?"

"Do I look suicidal?"

She tried not to smile.

He saw it anyway.

As they grew older, Ninja started taking care of him, too: saving him dinner, warning him when people took advantage, and occasionally dragging him out of trouble.

One evening he came home exhausted and collapsed on the sofa. She put on a dreadful action film they'd seen a dozen times.

Halfway through, he opened one eye.

"Didn't we agree this movie was terrible?"

"Yes."

"Why are we watching it?"

"Because you know how it ends."

He was quiet for a moment.

Then he reached down and squeezed her shoulder.

That was how they understood each other. Not through grand speeches or permanent agreement. Through the mundane evidence of being there.

A second mug made without asking.

A text when someone got home.

The comfortable insult that meant, I'm happy you're alive.

Ninja believed there would always be someone who could turn the worst day into something survivable.

She'd built her idea of home around that belief.

### The Money He Didn't Explain

The changes in Cassian began small enough to make excuses for.

A shift that ran long. An unfamiliar car outside the apartment. His phone lighting up at dinner and his smile disappearing before he answered.

He was in his twenties by then, and Ninja had her own life, her own work, her own habits. They didn't account for every hour of each other's days.

Still, she knew the difference between ordinary tiredness and the kind of tiredness that made somebody listen to the hallway before opening the door.

One evening he came over with groceries and left a thick envelope on her kitchen counter.

"What's that?"

"Rent. And a bit extra."

She looked at him.

"I pay my own rent."

"I know."

"Then why are you paying it?"

"Because you spent half your childhood eating the worst toast ever made, and I'd like to correct the historical record."

She didn't touch the envelope.

"Cass."

"Nin."

"Where did it come from?"

"Work."

"What work?"

"The boring kind."

"You'd complain about boring work. You complain when your socks are too boring."

He smiled, but it didn't reach his eyes.

"Seriously. It's fine."

She pushed the envelope back.

"I don't need you to buy me a life."

He looked hurt then, and she regretted the words almost immediately.

"That's not what I'm doing."

"Then tell me what you are doing."

He took a breath. For a second she thought he would.

Instead, he picked up the envelope.

"Trying to make something easier for once."

She let him leave without another argument. She told herself he was an adult. That he had a right to parts of his life that weren't hers. That she could love him without interrogating him.

And all of that was true.

It wasn't the whole truth.

The jobs had begun as justifiable favors. A delivery. An envelope held for a few hours. A passenger taken somewhere he wasn't supposed to ask about. The pay was good, and every request came with the promise that it would be the last.

By the time Cassian understood how much other people knew about him, refusing had begun to feel less like a decision and more like a threat.

He didn't tell Ninja.

He couldn't bear to watch the knowledge change the way she looked at him.

There were nights when his phone displayed a number and he stared until it stopped ringing. There were days when he made promises to himself about finishing one final obligation and disappearing from it all.

He told himself that a clean break still existed somewhere.

He only had to reach it.

### The Last Ordinary Evening

The last time Ninja had dinner with Cassian, he turned up carrying a badly wrapped parcel and an apology.

"Before you ask," he said, "yes, the wrapping was done by a human being."

"A drunk one?"

"An artist. Unappreciated in his time."

Inside was an old portable music player, the same model she'd once loved and broken years earlier. Cassian had repaired it, or persuaded somebody who actually knew what they were doing to repair it, and loaded it with songs they used to play too loudly in the kitchen.

She stared at it.

"Where did you even find this?"

"I have mysterious talents."

"You have mysterious debts, probably."

The joke landed strangely.

Cassian looked down, just for a second. Then he smiled again.

"Both can be true."

They ate pasta sitting on the sofa, argued about whether one of the songs was good or simply nostalgic, and laughed until she nearly spilled her drink when he tried to imitate a singer he had absolutely no business imitating.

Later, as he put on his coat, she noticed him checking his phone.

"You okay?"

"Yeah."

"That sounded rehearsed."

"Everything's all right, Nin."

She moved closer.

For a moment she considered making him stay, demanding an explanation, refusing to let him hide behind his usual jokes.

But he looked so tired.

"Call me tomorrow," she said.

He tucked the phone away.

"I will."

"And don't do anything stupid."

"You're taking all my hobbies away."

She hugged him at the door. He held on a fraction longer than usual.

Then he stepped back, touched her cheek lightly, and smiled in the familiar crooked way that had been making terrible things seem manageable since she was a child.

"Lock up behind me, menace."

She rolled her eyes.

"I always do."

He waited until he heard the lock turn.

It was such a small thing. The two of them had repeated it a hundred times.

She would spend years wishing she'd opened the door again.

### The Knock

Three evenings later, someone knocked.

Ninja was standing at the sink, rinsing a mug. She remembered that detail afterward because it was the sort of useless thing memory kept when the important parts became impossible.

The tap was running.

A small patch of sunlight fell across the counter.

Her phone was on the table, face up.

She turned off the water and went to the door.

Two people stood outside. Dark coats, careful expressions, the uneasy stillness of strangers who had rehearsed what they were about to say.

"Are you Cassian's sister?"

Everything in her body seemed to recognize the question before her mind did.

"Why?"

They asked to come inside.

She didn't move.

"What happened?"

One of them said her name. The other looked toward the stairs.

They said Cassian had been killed. They said there appeared to have been a robbery. They said an investigation was ongoing, though there were indications he had simply been in the wrong place at the wrong time.

The words passed through the doorway in neat, impossible pieces.

"Killed?"

"I'm very sorry."

"Where?"

They told her.

"Was he alone?"

A pause.

"We're still establishing exactly what occurred."

"Did he call anyone? Did he say anything?"

"We don't have that information."

She looked from one face to the other.

They were gentle. They sounded certain about some things and strangely evasive about others.

It made no sense.

Cassian would have called. Cassian would have argued. Cassian would have found a way to say something ridiculous and then come home with a bruise and an apology.

There was no world in which he simply stopped.

"When can I see him?" she asked.

The older one inhaled slowly.

She hated that, too.

The mug remained in the sink. She eventually let them in, answered questions, accepted a number to call.

She didn't cry while they were there.

She wasn't holding herself together. She hadn't yet understood there was something to fall apart.

After they left, she found the phone on the table and called Cassian.

His voicemail answered.

She listened to his voice all the way through.

Then she called again.

And again.

The fourth time, she put the phone down without ending the call and sat on the kitchen floor.

The voicemail continued speaking into an empty room.

### A House Full of Missing Things

There was a funeral.

People brought flowers. Some brought food. Others brought stories she hadn't heard before, most of them kind and almost all of them unbearable.

Someone called Cassian a good man.

Ninja believed that.

Someone else said that at least he hadn't suffered.

They couldn't possibly know that.

She looked down at her hands because she didn't trust what might happen if she answered.

Grief didn't look like the collapse people expected. It looked like reaching for her phone to tell him something funny. Buying too much food. Leaving his messages unread because, for one impossible second, the notification made her believe he might answer.

She slept badly. She became angry at ordinary conversations.

A friend asked whether she had considered speaking to someone about it.

"About what?" Ninja asked.

"About Cassian."

"What would you like me to say?"

"I just thought—"

"I know what you thought."

Her friend went quiet.

Ninja apologized later, but she didn't let the woman back inside the apartment.

It was easier that way. If nobody came close enough to ask, she didn't have to explain the enormous, shapeless absence in the middle of every day.

The official account never sat right.

A robbery. A bad place. Bad timing.

Cassian had sometimes gone out late and come back strange. She remembered the envelope, the unanswered questions, the look on his face when she'd jokingly mentioned debts.

She wondered if there had been something he was afraid to tell her.

Then she hated herself for wondering.

Dead people couldn't defend themselves against suspicion. They couldn't explain. They couldn't say, No, Nin, you've got it wrong.

She asked for information and received little. The questions grew heavier without becoming clearer.

Eventually she stopped asking out loud.

But she never stopped thinking about them.

Months passed. She saw him everywhere: outside the coffee shop, leaning against familiar railings, reflected in bus windows that belonged to strangers.

The city was full of places that remembered him.

And she was exhausted by being expected to remember him in the acceptable ways.

One evening she opened the old music player.

The first song started through a pair of headphones.

Cassian had named the playlist FOR THE SMALL MENACE.

She laughed, one startled, broken sound.

Then she cried so hard she had to put the player down.

It was the first time she'd allowed herself to do both in the same moment.

### Somewhere Else

The idea of leaving came quietly, while she scrolled through jobs, rooms, and places she might afford.

A village in the mountains caught her attention.

Bellwether.

A room over a bakery. A street full of shops. Forest trails nearby. Rain, apparently, as a permanent civic policy.

She studied the photographs. She didn't imagine a village could fix anything.

Cassian would still be dead when she woke up there. Whatever had happened to him would still have happened. She would still know what his voice sounded like and hate that she couldn't hear it again without pressing play.

But nobody there would stop speaking when she entered a room.

Nobody would lower their voice and ask how she was coping, as though grief were an illness with a set of expected symptoms.

Nobody would know to call her Cassian's little sister.

For once, being unknown sounded less like loneliness and more like the possibility of rest.

So she packed.

She kept the old player, the photographs, and the chipped blue mug. She took the jacket he'd once lent her and never asked to have back.

She left other things behind because she could not bear the weight of making decisions about them.

On the morning she drove away, she sat with the engine off for several minutes.

"Well, Cass," she said softly, looking at the empty passenger seat.

She tried to think of what he'd say.

Probably something about checking the tires.

Probably a joke about her sense of direction.

Definitely something annoying.

She smiled despite herself.

Then she started the car.

### The Crooked Lantern

Back in Bellwether, rain tapped against Ninja's jacket as she walked past shuttered shopfronts.

Through the bakery window, Ada was wiping down a counter. She raised one hand when she saw Ninja pass.

Ninja returned the gesture.

It was a small exchange, not quite a friendship, and she was grateful for how little it asked of her.

At the corner, a warm light fell across the wet pavement.

The Crooked Lantern.

Its door creaked when she pushed it open. The room smelled of wood polish, damp wool, and the comforting suggestion of something cooking behind the bar.

A few people sat at tables. Someone had left a coat over the back of a chair. A low conversation drifted from near the fire. The place seemed to have no interest in impressing anyone.

Perfect.

The bartender looked up.

"Evening."

"Whiskey, please."

"Any preference?"

"The one that doesn't ask personal questions."

One corner of his mouth lifted.

"That narrows it down surprisingly little."

"Then your favorite."

He poured a glass and set it in front of her.

She chose a stool with a good view of the door and her shoulder turned slightly away from the room. Old habits. Not fear, exactly. Just the relief of not offering herself to conversation.

For a few minutes, it almost worked.

The glass was cool beneath her fingertips. Rain slipped down the window. Nobody knew her name. Nobody asked about Cassian.

She let the quiet settle.

Then another glass appeared beside the first.

Ninja looked at it.

She hadn't ordered a second.

The bartender tipped his head toward a far corner.

"From the man across the room."

Of course.

Her first instinct was irritation, sharp and reassuringly familiar.

She hadn't driven all this way to be approached by a stranger who thought a drink was an invitation to claim part of her evening.

She could send it back.

She almost did.

But the refusal stalled somewhere between thought and speech.

She stared at her whiskey, annoyed by the hesitation. Once, she might have enjoyed the contest of figuring out a stranger before they figured out her.

That person felt far away.

She lifted her gaze anyway.

He sat alone in the corner, dark clothes against the low light, long hair, a beard, and the kind of stillness that made everyone else's movements seem slightly louder.

He was already looking at her.

Not with the easy confidence she'd expected. There was no smug little smile, no exaggerated charm.

For a fraction of a second, he looked almost startled.

As though he'd done something before he had the chance to talk himself out of it.

That caught her attention.

She studied him in return.

He sat where he could see the exits. His control looked practiced, maintained rather than effortless.

There were people like that. People who carried too much while insisting they were perfectly capable of carrying more.

Near the end, Cassian had sometimes sat that way.

The memory stung without warning.

Ninja's fingers tightened around her glass.

Don't start, she told herself.

You came here to be nobody. Not to collect somebody else's problems.

She could leave the second drink untouched, turn away, and make her wishes clear.

She was exceptionally good at that.

But she was curious about his voice. Whether he belonged here, or whether, like her, he needed somewhere nobody would ask questions.

The thought made her angry at herself.

She didn't know him. She had no reason to care.

Something in his expression suggested he, too, had once believed that moving far enough might finally outrun the past.

She held his gaze.

A second longer than she meant to.

The man in the corner didn't look away.

Neither did she.

**End of Episode 1.**
