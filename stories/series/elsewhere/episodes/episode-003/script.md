# Elsewhere — Episode 3: The Stranger at the Bar
## Full narration manuscript — draft

### Previously on Elsewhere

Five years ago, a Directorate operation killed Elise Varen, Ninja's older sister. Hex tried to stop it, then vanished into Bellwether under the name Elias Ward, carrying a photograph of the sister he had never met.

Ninja came to Bellwether for a few days of quiet. She met Ada, explored the village, and found an unexpected measure of peace. At The Crooked Lantern, she and Hex nearly crossed paths without seeing each other. Then Maeve mentioned a visitor with bright pink hair, and Hex realised that the woman from the photograph might be only a few streets away.

An anonymous message had already warned him: YOU SHOULD HAVE STAYED GONE.

### Chapter One — Breakfast and Other Negotiations

The radiator woke Ninja at seven with three sharp knocks.

She opened one eye.

"If you're a ghost," she told the ceiling, "you're going to have to wait until I've had coffee."

The radiator knocked again.

"Rude."

Outside, Bellwether was wrapped in pale morning mist. The bakery downstairs had already begun its daily campaign to make the entire building smell like butter and fresh bread. Ninja lay still for another minute, listening to the muffled clatter of trays and the distant sound of someone arguing with a delivery van.

She hadn't slept particularly well. Her dreams had been crowded with familiar places she could no longer visit. But there was something different about waking here. No immediate need to check a work message. No stack of letters on the kitchen counter. No pressure to explain herself to someone who had already decided what the explanation should be.

She dressed slowly, pulling her pink hair into a messy bun, and went downstairs.

Ada was arranging toast with the concentration of a military commander.

"Morning," she said. "You survived the radiator."

"Only because I negotiated a ceasefire."

"Good. It usually wins."

Ninja took a seat by the window.

Ada placed a mug of coffee in front of her, followed by a plate of eggs and toast.

"You're going to feed me until I can't leave, aren't you?"

"That's the business model."

"Diabolical. I respect it."

Ada smiled and sat opposite her for a moment, wiping her hands on her apron.

"Sleep all right?"

Ninja considered lying. Not because Ada had done anything wrong, but because the answer to that question had become a rehearsed performance over the years.

"Some of it," she said.

"That's enough for a holiday. You're not being graded."

Ninja looked at her.

Ada stood again before the moment could become too serious.

"Besides, Inspector's expecting you."

"Who's Inspector?"

"The florist's dog. Apparently you made a favourable impression."

"Finally. Recognition from someone important."

When she stepped outside half an hour later, the village was waking at its own unhurried pace. The florist was sweeping leaves from the doorway. Inspector was supervising from the middle of the pavement.

Ninja crouched.

"Good morning, sir. I've come for my performance review."

Inspector accepted an ear scratch and offered no criticism.

"Excellent. Promotion already."

The florist laughed from the doorway.

"He's never liked strangers this quickly."

"That's because I understand his management style. Minimal effort, maximum snacks."

Ninja stayed to chat about the weather, the roads, and the cat named Mayor who had apparently stolen half a sausage from the café the previous evening.

Then she headed toward the bookshop, carrying a small paper bag of biscuits that Ada had insisted she take.

She had no plan beyond wandering. For once, that was enough.

### Chapter Two — The Man Who Didn't Sleep

Hex had been awake since four.

The anonymous message had disappeared from his phone, but not from his thoughts.

He had checked the windows twice before sunrise. Checked the workshop door. Walked the perimeter of the yard as though a five-year-old habit could tell him whether danger was real.

At six, Tomas arrived carrying two coffees.

"You're early."

"So are you."

"I own the place."

"Convenient excuse."

Tomas handed him a cup and leaned against the workbench.

"Maeve said you left the pub looking like you'd seen a ghost."

"Maeve exaggerates."

"Not about ghosts. She's surprisingly restrained."

Hex said nothing.

Tomas watched him for a moment.

"You don't have to tell me what happened. But if something's wrong, don't make me guess whether you're about to disappear."

Hex looked at him.

The older man's face was calm, but there was a quiet hurt beneath the words. Five years of working together had taught Tomas which questions not to ask. It hadn't taught him not to care.

"I'm not leaving," Hex said.

Tomas nodded once.

"Good. Because the forestry truck still needs a clutch."

"Touching."

"I know. I'm sentimental."

They worked through the morning. Hex kept his hands busy and his attention divided. Every unfamiliar engine noise made him look up. Every car slowing outside the gate drew a glance.

At midday, Tomas finally put down his tools.

"Go get lunch."

"I'm fine."

"You're holding a spanner like it's personally insulted your family."

"It has."

"Then take it out to lunch. Maybe you can resolve your differences."

Hex sighed and reached for his coat.

Tomas added, more quietly, "Elias."

Hex paused.

"You don't have to be alone with every bad thought."

For a moment Hex almost answered honestly.

Instead he said, "I'll bring you something."

"Pie."

"Of course."

"And don't let Maeve charge you for fixing the extractor."

"She'll charge me for breathing near it."

"That's why she's a successful businesswoman."

Hex walked into town beneath a sky the colour of wet slate.

He told himself he was going for food.

He did not tell himself he was hoping to see pink hair.

### Chapter Three — A Very Bad Book

Ninja had returned to The Narrow Cup with the terrible paperback she'd bought the day before.

The bookseller had been right. The heroine had now fallen into a bog, escaped a mysterious stranger, and somehow found time to change into a fresh evening gown.

Ninja stared at the page.

"Where did you get the dress?" she muttered.

The café owner, passing with a tray, glanced at her.

"Sorry?"

"The woman in this book. She's been chased through a swamp for three chapters and she's wearing silk. I need answers."

The owner laughed.

"Maybe the swamp has a boutique."

"That would honestly explain more than the plot does."

She was still smiling when her phone lit up.

A routine email from home.

Nothing urgent. Nothing threatening. But for a second the familiar cold pressure returned to her stomach. She turned the phone over.

She could deal with it later.

The café owner set a fresh glass of water beside her.

"You're allowed to ignore the world for an afternoon," she said.

"Is that a village rule?"

"Strictly enforced."

Ninja raised her mug in salute.

By the time she finished the chapter, the rain had begun again. She wandered into a small shop selling wool scarves, postcards, and an assortment of objects that appeared to have been carved by people who had never seen the animals they were depicting.

One wooden creature might have been a goat. It might also have been a chair.

Ninja held it up.

"What is this supposed to be?"

The shopkeeper peered over her glasses.

"A mountain sheep."

"Of course. I was just testing you."

She bought a postcard of Bellwether in the snow, then spent five minutes deciding whom to send it to.

Her thumb moved toward Elise's name in her contacts before she caught herself.

There it was again: that little collision between habit and absence.

She put the postcard in her bag.

Maybe she would write it anyway.

Not everything needed a destination.

### Chapter Four — The Second Evening

By evening, the rain had softened to a fine mist, and Ninja had decided that The Crooked Lantern deserved another visit.

Partly because the stew had been excellent.

Partly because Maeve had the kind of dry humour that made sitting alone feel less lonely.

And partly because she had spent an entire day being responsible for nothing more complicated than choosing where to walk.

At the pub, Maeve looked up as Ninja entered.

"Back already?"

"Your stew has compromised my independence."

"Good. That's how we get repeat customers."

"Is there a loyalty card?"

"Yes. Every tenth meal, I insult your taste in books for free."

Ninja placed the paperback on the bar.

"You can start early. This one deserves it."

Maeve glanced at the cover and groaned.

"Oh, that disaster."

"She changed dresses in a swamp."

"She does it twice."

Ninja closed her eyes.

"Don't tell me. I need to experience the full tragedy."

Maeve laughed and pointed toward the small table by the fire.

"Sit. I'll bring you something."

Ninja settled in, feeling pleasantly anonymous.

At the far end of the bar, a man in a dark coat had been speaking to Tomas.

Hex heard the laugh before he saw her.

It was an ordinary laugh. Warm, slightly incredulous, the sound of someone enjoying an absurdity despite herself.

Then he turned.

Bright pink hair, gathered into a loose bun. Dark clothes. A small septum ring catching the firelight. A face he had known only from a photograph, changed subtly by five years and the expression of someone who had learned to carry sorrow without letting it erase her.

For a second, the room narrowed around him.

He recognised her.

Not vaguely. Not hopefully.

Her.

Ninja.

Elise's sister.

He looked away before she noticed.

Tomas followed his gaze.

"Know her?"

"No."

The answer came too quickly.

Tomas studied him, then let it pass.

"She's staying at Ada's. Seems nice."

Hex tightened his grip on his glass.

"How do you know?"

"Because I live in a village, Elias. Knowing things about strangers is our main industry."

Hex tried to smile.

Across the room, Ninja was examining the menu as though it had personally offended her.

He should leave.

He should get up, walk through the side door, and never return while she was in Bellwether.

That would be the safest choice.

He stayed seated.

### Chapter Five — The Stranger at the Bar

It happened because Maeve was busy, the pub was crowded, and Ninja decided to collect her own drink rather than wait.

She came to the bar holding the paperback in one hand.

"Maeve, I have a formal complaint."

Maeve was carrying three plates.

"Get in line."

"The heroine has now survived the swamp, a kidnapping, and an inexplicable costume change. Nobody has offered her a sandwich."

"Criminal."

"Exactly."

Maeve nodded toward the man standing beside the counter.

"Elias, can you hand her that glass? My hands are full."

Hex turned.

For the first time, he looked directly into Ninja's eyes.

He had imagined this moment once or twice over the years, always with dread. He had imagined recognition, accusation, a terrible question he couldn't answer.

Instead, she looked at him with amused curiosity.

"Are you the village's emergency glass distributor?"

He handed her the glass.

"Only on Thursdays."

"It's Friday."

"Then I'm operating without a licence."

She smiled.

It was devastatingly ordinary.

"I'm Ninja," she said.

He knew.

Of course he knew.

But hearing her say it aloud made something in his chest tighten.

"Elias."

"Nice to meet you, Elias."

He nearly told her his real name.

Instead he nodded.

"You too."

There was a pause. Not uncomfortable, exactly, but full of possibilities neither of them knew how to name.

Ninja lifted the paperback.

"Have you read this?"

Hex glanced at the cover.

"Should I?"

"Absolutely not. Unless you've ever wondered how a person can change clothes in the middle of a swamp."

"Can't say I have."

"Well, now you will."

A reluctant smile pulled at his mouth.

She noticed.

"There it is," she said.

"What?"

"A smile. I was beginning to think Bellwether had a strict ration."

"We're saving them for winter."

"Understandable. Difficult climate."

Maeve returned, set down a plate of food, and looked between them with open satisfaction.

"Good. You've met."

"Apparently," Hex said.

Maeve turned to Ninja.

"Elias fixes things."

"Does he?"

"Machines, mostly. Occasionally the pub's terrible decisions."

"Not all of them," Hex said.

Ninja looked around at the crooked beams and mismatched furniture.

"That sounds like a full-time job."

"It is," he replied.

Tomas appeared behind them.

"He's modest. He can also repair an engine while complaining about it."

"Complaining is part of the process," Hex said.

Ninja raised her glass.

"Finally, a professional I understand."

For the first time in days, Hex laughed without planning it.

The sound surprised him.

It surprised Tomas too.

Ninja took her drink back to her table, but not before glancing over her shoulder.

"Enjoy your illegal glass-distribution career, Elias."

"I'll try."

He watched her go.

Tomas leaned closer.

"Well."

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You said 'well.'"

"A very versatile word."

Hex stared into his glass.

Across the room, Ninja opened her book again and smiled to herself.

### Chapter Six — An Ordinary Conversation

Half an hour later, the pub had grown quieter.

Ninja's food had arrived, and the terrible paperback lay open beside her plate. Hex had meant to leave twice, but Maeve had found another small problem with a cupboard hinge, and Tomas had ordered another drink.

When Hex finally crossed the room to retrieve his coat from the peg, Ninja looked up.

"Quick question."

He stopped.

"Depends on the question."

"Is there actually a sensible way to get to the old bridge, or does the village require a sacrifice to the fountain?"

He glanced toward the window.

"Which bridge?"

"The one by the café. I managed to approach it from three directions and somehow ended up behind the florist."

"That's impressive."

"Thank you. I've been practising."

He described the route, using the bakery and the church as landmarks.

Ninja listened carefully, then frowned.

"So I turn left where the road looks like it should go right."

"Exactly."

"And ignore the sign."

"Usually."

"Are you sure you're not trying to get rid of me?"

"Not yet."

She grinned.

He had to look away.

It was easier to be Elias Ward when nobody asked him to be anything more than useful. Ninja, without knowing it, had asked him to be present.

"You're not from here," she said.

"No."

"Me neither. Although I think Inspector's considering adopting me."

"The florist's dog?"

"You know him?"

"Everyone knows Inspector."

"He's a good judge of character."

Hex considered that.

"He's also been known to eat gravel."

"Nobody's perfect."

She smiled at him again, then gestured toward the empty chair opposite her.

"You can sit for a minute, if you're not busy. I promise not to ask you to fix anything."

He should say no.

He had a thousand reasons to say no.

Instead, he sat.

They talked about Bellwether's roads, about the book, about Ada's suspiciously generous breakfasts. Ninja told him about the robin at the overlook that had judged her fitness level. Hex told her that the robin probably worked for the village council.

"That explains everything," she said.

He found himself asking questions.

Not about Elise. Never about Elise.

About the village. About whether she'd enjoyed the view. About what she thought of the café.

She answered with stories instead of simple facts, her humour flickering through even the smallest details.

Then she asked, "Have you lived here long?"

"Five years."

"Do you like it?"

He looked toward the window, where rain moved through the lamplight.

"More than I expected."

"That sounds suspiciously like a compliment."

"Don't tell anyone."

"Your secret's safe."

He almost laughed at the word secret.

She didn't notice.

A quiet settled between them, not awkward now.

Ninja stirred the last of her tea.

"I needed somewhere different," she said.

Hex kept his voice gentle.

"Did you find it?"

She considered.

"I think so. It's nice being somewhere nobody knows my whole story."

The words struck him harder than she could have guessed.

He knew a piece of her story she didn't know herself.

And he was sitting across from her pretending to be a stranger.

"Sometimes that's a good thing," he said.

She looked at him, studying his expression for a moment.

"Yeah," she said softly. "Sometimes it is."

### Chapter Seven — The Road Back

By the time Ninja stood to leave, the pub was nearly empty.

She pulled on her coat and gathered her book.

"Well, Elias, thank you for the directions and the excellent professional advice about illegally distributing glasses."

"Any time."

"Careful. I might take you seriously."

He stood too.

Outside, the rain had returned.

Ninja looked at it with theatrical disappointment.

"I specifically ordered better weather."

"Bellwether doesn't accept requests."

"I've noticed."

He opened the door for her.

The cold air smelled of wet stone and chimney smoke.

For a moment they stood beneath the narrow awning, watching rain scatter across the cobbles.

"Which way to Briar House?" he asked.

She pointed confidently in the wrong direction.

Hex looked at her.

She sighed.

"Don't say it."

"I wasn't going to."

"You absolutely were."

He pointed the other way.

"That street."

"Right. Obviously. I was testing you."

"Naturally."

She pulled up her hood.

"Goodnight, Elias."

"Goodnight, Ninja."

She walked away, then turned back once.

"Try smiling again sometime. It suits you."

He stood beneath the awning, unable to answer before she disappeared around the corner.

For several seconds, the world seemed strangely quiet.

Then his phone vibrated.

An unknown number.

He looked at the screen.

WE SEE YOU.

The warmth vanished from his face.

Across the street, a parked car started its engine.

Hex watched its headlights sweep across the wet pavement before it pulled away.

He had spent five years running from the Directorate.

Now Ninja was here, and someone had noticed.

He looked toward the street where she had gone.

He could leave Bellwether tonight. Disappear before dawn. Let her finish her holiday without ever knowing why the quiet mechanic had vanished.

But he had just sat across from her, heard her laugh, and watched her talk about a book she hated as though it were the most important thing in the world.

He could no longer pretend she was only a photograph.

Inside the pub, Tomas called his name.

Hex slipped the phone into his pocket.

"Coming."

He took one last look at the empty street.

Somewhere beyond the corner, Ninja was walking home, thinking about a quiet man with a dry smile.

Neither of them knew how little time the ordinary world had left to give them.
