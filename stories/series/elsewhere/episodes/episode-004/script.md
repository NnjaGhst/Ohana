# Elsewhere — Episode 4: The Walk Home

*Third-person narration. Contemporary Bellwether. Draft for Wade/Hallie audio production.*

### Previously on Elsewhere

Ninja had come to Bellwether for a holiday, hoping to spend a few days somewhere her past didn't follow her into every room. She found a village full of eccentric people, an elderly dog named Inspector, and a mechanic named Elias Ward who was considerably more interesting than he wanted to appear.

She didn't know Elias was Hex, a former Directorate operative who had been present the night her sister Elise died.

After their first proper conversation at The Crooked Lantern, Ninja headed back to Briar House in the rain. Hex stayed beneath the pub awning, smiling despite himself.

Then a message arrived on his phone.

WE SEE YOU.

And across the street, a parked car drove away.

### Chapter One — The Street with Two Names

Ninja made it exactly twenty-six steps before she stopped beneath a streetlamp and looked back.

Not because she'd forgotten anything.

She told herself that immediately.

She had her book tucked under her jacket, her phone in her pocket, and the key to Briar House in the small zipped compartment of her bag. She had checked the key twice before leaving the pub, because Ada had explained with the grave seriousness of someone discussing a national security procedure that the front door liked to stick after rain.

No. She stopped because she had walked past a narrow lane that looked suspiciously like the one Elias had pointed out.

There was a sign on the wall: OLD MILL LANE.

And below it, a smaller sign: MILL STREET.

“Brilliant,” she muttered. “Even the roads can't agree on their names.”

The village was quiet now. Most of the shop windows were dark. Rain fell in a fine silver mist, enough to turn the cobbles slick without making much sound.

She could still hear Elias's voice.

*That street.*

His expression when she'd pointed the wrong way had been almost unbearably composed. Like a man trying not to laugh in church.

She smiled despite herself.

It had been a long time since a stranger made her feel comfortable without trying too hard. Most people either asked too many questions or spoke to her as if she were made of something fragile. Elias had done neither. He'd talked about roads and terrible books and a robin with apparent municipal authority.

He had looked at her as though he were actually listening.

She pulled out her phone and opened the map.

The little blue dot insisted she was standing inside a bakery.

“I'm not,” she informed it.

The dot remained unconvinced.

A gust of wind pushed cold rain under her hood. She tucked the phone away and chose the lane that sloped uphill. Briar House was uphill. Probably.

Behind her, somewhere beyond the bend, a car engine started.

She barely noticed.

### Chapter Two — A Man Who Counts Exits

Hex watched the taillights disappear.

He did not move until the engine noise had faded beyond the square.

The phone remained in his hand, the message bright against the dark screen.

WE SEE YOU.

There were dozens of ways to send a threat without leaving an obvious trail. A disposable number, a relay service, a compromised device. The words alone told him very little.

But the timing told him plenty.

Five years of silence. Two messages in one evening. And Ninja was in Bellwether.

He stepped inside The Crooked Lantern.

The warmth hit him first, followed by the smell of spilled beer and woodsmoke. Tomas sat near the fireplace, studying the bottom of his glass with exaggerated concentration.

“You look like you've seen a ghost,” Tomas said.

“Maybe the extractor's haunted after all.”

“Don't joke about that. Maeve will put you on a retainer.”

Hex set his toolbox on the floor.

He wanted to ask Tomas whether he'd seen the car outside. He wanted to know who had been sitting in it, how long it had been there, whether its driver had watched the pub door.

Instead he looked at the windows.

Maeve came over with a cloth.

“You all right, Elias?”

“Did you notice a dark car parked opposite?”

She frowned. “There's always a dark car parked opposite. Half the village owns dark cars.”

“This one left just now.”

“Didn't see it. Why?”

“No reason.”

Maeve gave him the sort of look that made clear she knew there was a reason and was deciding whether to challenge him.

Then she glanced at Tomas.

“You two are both terrible liars.”

“I'm excellent,” Tomas said. “I just don't practise.”

Hex tried to smile. It didn't quite work.

He picked up his toolbox.

“I'm heading home.”

Tomas put down his glass.

“Walk with you.”

“It's out of your way.”

“I'm old. I need the exercise. Apparently.”

Hex could have refused. He didn't.

They stepped into the damp night together.

For the first few streets, Tomas talked about a generator that had developed a peculiar rattle and a customer who had blamed a flat battery on the moon.

Hex listened with only half his attention.

Every window reflected movement. Every parked vehicle had to be assessed and dismissed. He hated how quickly the old habits returned.

At the crossroads Tomas stopped.

“Elias.”

Hex looked at him.

“You're not going to tell me what's wrong.”

It wasn't a question.

“Not tonight.”

Tomas nodded slowly.

“Fine. But whatever it is, don't make the mistake of thinking you have to carry it without help.”

Hex looked toward the empty road.

“You don't know what you're offering.”

“No,” Tomas said. “That's why I didn't offer to fix it. I offered to help.”

The words landed harder than Hex expected.

Tomas clapped him once on the shoulder and went home.

Hex stood under the streetlight until the older man disappeared.

Then he took the longer route to his cottage, checking behind him twice.

### Chapter Three — The House That Remembered

Ninja found Briar House by accident.

The correct lane, it turned out, was the one she had almost rejected because it looked like a private driveway. Bellwether's commitment to misleading signage was impressive.

The stone house glowed warmly behind its ivy. Rain ticked against the window ledges, and a small lamp had been left on in the entrance hall.

She eased the key into the lock.

It stuck.

“Don't start,” she whispered.

The door opened on the second attempt.

Inside, the house smelled of polished wood, lavender and whatever Ada had baked that afternoon.

A floorboard creaked.

Ada appeared from the sitting room wearing a cardigan and carrying a mug.

“There you are. I was beginning to wonder if the fountain had claimed another victim.”

“Almost. I think the village changes its streets when tourists aren't looking.”

“Only on Thursdays.”

“That's reassuring.”

Ada's eyes flicked to Ninja's wet coat.

“Did you eat?”

“Stew. Tea. And I made several extremely responsible decisions about dessert.”

“Good.”

“I also met someone who knows where the roads go.”

“Useful sort of person.”

“He fixes things. Apparently including the pub's resident ghost.”

Ada's expression brightened.

“Elias?”

Ninja paused.

“Does everyone here know everyone?”

“Of course. That's what villages are for.”

“He seems nice.”

Ada smiled into her mug, but she didn't tease.

“He is. Quiet, though. Took him a long time to settle here.”

Something in the way she said it made Ninja curious.

“Where did he come from?”

“Somewhere he didn't much care to talk about.”

Ninja considered that.

“Fair enough.”

Ada looked at her a moment longer.

“You don't have to explain yourself to everyone who asks, you know.”

Ninja's smile softened.

“I know.”

But she appreciated hearing it.

Upstairs, her room was warm and dim. She hung her jacket over the chair, placed the paperback on the bedside table, and sat on the edge of the bed without turning on the main light.

For a few minutes she did nothing.

It had been a good day.

That simple fact felt unexpectedly difficult to hold.

She had laughed with strangers. Climbed to a viewpoint. Eaten something delicious. Met a man who looked as though smiling was a skill he had mislaid and then found by accident.

Her phone lit up with an old photograph in the memories widget.

Elise.

Two sisters in a kitchen, both laughing, a cake between them that had collapsed on one side. Elise had flour on her cheek. Ninja was holding a spatula like a weapon.

Ninja stared at it.

The ache arrived quietly, as it often did now. Not a wave that knocked her over, but a weight settling in a familiar place.

“Hey,” she whispered to the picture.

She told Elise about the village.

About the dog who had apparently secured a management position. About Ada's terrifying competence. About the mountains.

And then, after a pause, about Elias.

“Don't make that face,” she murmured. “I only met him tonight.”

She could almost hear Elise's answer.

*I didn't say anything.*

“You would have.”

She smiled, and the smile hurt a little.

Then she set the phone facedown.

For once, grief had not taken the entire day from her. She was allowed to be glad about that.

### Chapter Four — A Light Across the Square

At his cottage, Hex locked the door, checked the back window and placed his phone on the kitchen table.

He had lived there five years.

The furniture was secondhand. The shelves held repair manuals, a few novels Tomas had lent him, and a chipped blue mug he'd bought at the village market. The rooms were comfortable in a practical, temporary way.

He had never quite stopped living as though he might have to leave.

He pulled the curtains shut.

Then he opened the small tin from the cupboard.

Inside lay the photograph of Ninja and Elise's damaged phone.

He looked at the photograph.

He knew how dangerous it was to build a person out of an image. He had done it anyway, for years. The woman in the picture had become a symbol of what he had failed to prevent.

But the woman in the pub was not a symbol.

She had opinions about badly written books. She was suspicious of village geography. She talked to strangers without surrendering her boundaries. She could turn a conversation about a robin into something that made him forget, briefly, to be afraid.

He had heard her laugh.

That was the detail he couldn't stop thinking about.

His own phone vibrated.

He went still.

A message from Tomas.

*Generator's still rattling. If I die in an explosion, tell Maeve she owes me six pounds.*

Hex stared at the screen.

Then, against his better judgement, he laughed.

He typed back.

*If you die in an explosion, I'm billing your estate for the repairs.*

The reply came almost immediately.

*Heartless.*

Hex put the phone down.

For the first time since leaving the pub, he felt his shoulders loosen.

He took a notebook from the drawer and wrote down everything he remembered about the car: dark hatchback, wet rear window, no visible roof rack, engine started shortly after Ninja left, departure toward the east road. He couldn't identify a make or registration. He would not invent certainty where there was none.

The message itself was gone. He'd deleted it in a reflex he now regretted.

He wrote down its exact wording and the time.

Then he checked the locks again.

He could leave.

He knew how. He could pack in fifteen minutes and be out of the village before dawn. A new name, another town, another workshop.

He imagined Ada asking where Elias had gone. Tomas standing alone over an engine. Maeve discovering that the extractor had developed another complaint.

And Ninja, perhaps, asking why the quiet man from the pub had disappeared.

He closed the notebook.

Not tonight.

### Chapter Five — A Morning Without Rain

Morning arrived with pale sunlight.

Ninja woke to the unfamiliar luxury of not having an alarm.

She lay still, listening.

No rain.

No traffic.

A bird outside the window was delivering a speech of considerable length to an audience that had not requested one.

“Excellent,” she said sleepily. “The robin has called a meeting.”

Downstairs, Ada had prepared breakfast with the sort of efficiency that suggested she'd been awake since the invention of time.

“Sleep well?”

“Very. Although someone outside my window is campaigning for public office.”

“The robin?”

“He's persistent.”

“His father was the same.”

Ninja stopped pouring tea.

“You're joking.”

Ada's expression remained perfectly serious.

“Am I?”

Ninja laughed.

She ate slowly, reading a few pages of the dreadful paperback and making notes in the margin whenever the author committed a particularly offensive crime against common sense.

When she finished, Ada handed her a small folded map.

“I've marked the old bridge. And the bakery.”

“Are you implying I need supervision?”

“I'm implying I'd like you back before Christmas.”

Ninja examined the map.

It had handwritten arrows, a star over the bridge, and a warning beside the fountain: *DO NOT TRUST THIS ROUNDABOUT.*

She looked up.

Ada was smiling.

“Oh, you're all in on it.”

“On what?”

“The conspiracy.”

“Eat another cinnamon bun, dear.”

Ninja accepted the bun.

Some conspiracies were worth joining.

### Chapter Six — The Old Bridge

The old bridge crossed a narrow river on the edge of the village. Its stone parapets were dark with age, and moss grew in the joints. Beyond it, a modern road curved past a row of cottages toward the foothills.

Ninja arrived without getting lost.

She considered calling the local newspaper.

She leaned against the parapet and watched the water moving over smooth stones.

It was the sort of place Elise would have loved. Not because it was spectacular, but because she would have found something to be delighted by: a crooked tree, an oddly shaped cloud, a duck with an unreasonable attitude.

Ninja took a photograph.

She almost sent it to her sister.

The old habit was so quick that her thumb had already moved toward the messaging app before she remembered.

She stood quietly for a moment.

Then she put the phone away.

A delivery van crossed the bridge behind her. The driver lifted two fingers in greeting. She waved back.

Life went on in ordinary little motions, whether she was ready or not.

“Morning.”

She turned.

Elias stood a few steps away with a paper bag in one hand and a small metal part in the other.

He looked less exhausted in daylight, though there were still shadows under his eyes. His dark hair was tied back loosely. A black jacket covered most of the tattoos she'd glimpsed the night before.

Ninja smiled.

“Well, look at that. The bridge actually exists.”

“I told you it did.”

“I assumed it was local folklore.”

“Most of the village is.”

He held up the metal part.

“I'm collecting a replacement bearing.”

“Of course you are. Do you ever go anywhere without something that needs fixing?”

“Sometimes I go places where things break after I arrive.”

“Very efficient. You bring the atmosphere with you.”

He almost smiled.

Ninja noticed.

“Ah. There it is.”

“What?”

“That expression. The one you were practising last night.”

“I wasn't practising.”

“Then you're a natural. Terrifying.”

He looked away toward the river.

For a moment, neither spoke.

The silence was comfortable, but it held something else too. Hex was measuring the street behind her, the parked cars, the windows across the road.

Ninja followed his glance.

“Are you waiting for someone?”

“No.”

“You looked like you were.”

“Just checking the road.”

She raised an eyebrow.

“Does it move too?”

He laughed once, quietly.

“No. Not usually.”

She watched him.

“Rough night?”

He considered lying.

“Didn't sleep much.”

“I'm sorry.”

There was no demand for an explanation in her voice.

That made it harder to dismiss.

“Thanks.”

Ninja rested her elbows on the stone parapet.

“I had a good night, actually.”

Hex looked at her.

“At the pub?”

“Yeah. And afterwards. Ada left the light on, and I managed to find the house without being rescued by a search party.”

“Impressive.”

“I know. Personal growth.”

He smiled again.

She studied him for a second.

“You know, you don't have to look surprised every time something makes you laugh.”

“I don't.”

“You absolutely do.”

He shook his head.

Then a dark car rolled slowly past the end of the bridge.

Hex's expression changed so quickly she almost missed it.

He shifted a step, not toward the car, but slightly between it and the parapet where she stood.

The vehicle continued around the bend.

Ninja looked at him.

“Elias?”

He blinked.

“Sorry. Thought I recognised someone.”

“Did you?”

“No.”

The answer came too fast.

She didn't press.

Instead she nodded at the paper bag.

“Is that breakfast or an important mechanical component?”

“Both, technically. The bakery gave me a pastry.”

“Did they give you the bearing too?”

“Not this time.”

“Disappointing service.”

He glanced at his watch.

“I should get back.”

“Of course. The village can't repair itself.”

“Apparently not.”

She stepped away from the parapet.

“Thanks for the directions. Again.”

“You found it without me.”

“Don't ruin my gratitude with facts.”

He laughed, properly this time.

And for a few seconds, standing in the weak morning sunlight beside an old stone bridge, they were simply two people enjoying a conversation.

“See you around, Elias,” Ninja said.

“See you, Ninja.”

She walked toward the bakery, turning once to wave.

Hex waved back.

He waited until she had disappeared beyond the corner.

Then he took out his phone and wrote down the time the car had passed.

### Chapter Seven — The Price of Staying

At the workshop, Tomas was already arguing with the generator.

“You're late,” he announced.

“By four minutes.”

“Civilisation collapses in four minutes.”

Hex put the replacement bearing on the bench.

“Then it's a miracle we're still here.”

Tomas looked up.

“You saw her again.”

Hex stopped.

“Who?”

“The woman with the pink hair.”

“How would you know?”

“Because you've got the face.”

“What face?”

“The one you get when you remember you're alive.”

Hex reached for a spanner.

“That's not a face.”

“It's a rare one. I'd recognise it anywhere.”

Hex said nothing.

Tomas watched him for a moment, then returned to the generator.

“You don't have to tell me about her. But if there's trouble coming, I'd rather know before it knocks the workshop down.”

Hex stared at the workbench.

He wanted to tell him everything. The Directorate. Elise. The photographs. The people who had died. The message on the phone.

Instead he said, “Someone might be looking for me.”

Tomas's hands went still.

“Someone dangerous?”

“Possibly.”

“Does the woman know?”

“No.”

“Is she part of it?”

Hex swallowed.

“She doesn't know she is.”

Tomas set down the spanner.

“Then you need to be careful what you do next.”

“I know.”

“No,” Tomas said gently. “I mean careful with *her*. Not just careful with yourself.”

Hex looked at him.

Tomas continued, “People aren't problems you can solve by deciding things for them. Even when you're trying to protect them.”

The words stayed with Hex long after Tomas returned to work.

By late afternoon the generator was running smoothly. The workshop smelled of warm oil and metal. Outside, the sky darkened toward another shower.

Hex washed his hands at the sink.

His phone buzzed.

Unknown number.

He didn't touch it for several seconds.

Then he opened the message.

*She took the bridge this morning.*

Below the words was a photograph.

Ninja, standing at the old bridge, looking out over the river.

The picture had been taken from behind her.

Hex felt the blood drain from his face.

A second message appeared.

*You can't watch every road.*

He looked through the workshop window at the village beyond.

He had told himself that staying in Bellwether was a choice.

Now he understood someone else intended to make that choice for him.

He saved the messages this time.

Then he went to Tomas.

“We need to talk.”
