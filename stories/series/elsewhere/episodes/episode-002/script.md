# Elsewhere — Episode 2: The Crooked Lantern
## Full narration manuscript — draft

### Previously on Elsewhere

Five years ago, a Directorate operation ended in a warehouse fire. Six people died, including Elise Varen, Ninja's older sister. Hex, an operative sent to recover a stolen archive, disobeyed orders and tried to save the people trapped inside. He escaped with Elise's damaged phone and a photograph of Ninja, then vanished under the name Elias Ward.

Ninja was told the fire was an accident. She spent years grieving Elise and fighting strange accusations attached to her employment records. Hex hid in the mountain village of Bellwether, repairing machinery for an older man named Tomas. Only recently did he discover that the woman in his photograph was Elise's sister.

And now, without knowing any of that, Ninja has arrived in Bellwether for a few days away.

### Chapter One — The Room Above the Bakery

The first thing Ninja learned about Bellwether was that the village had a personal disagreement with straightforward roads.

The second was that nobody appeared particularly interested in resolving it.

She passed the same stone fountain twice before deciding the road signs were either decorative or malicious.

“Excellent,” she told the dashboard. “I've been here twelve minutes and the village is already bullying me.”

Rain stippled the windscreen. Beyond the narrow lane, cottages leaned together beneath slate roofs. Window boxes spilled stubborn late-season flowers over old stone walls, and somewhere a church bell rang five times with the weary confidence of something that had never once been late.

Her accommodation was called Briar House. The booking photograph had shown a charming doorway beneath ivy.

The actual doorway was charming, certainly. It was also hidden down an alley barely wider than her luggage.

She parked where a handwritten sign instructed VISITORS, PLEASE DON'T BLOCK THE MILK VAN, and dragged her bag across the wet cobbles.

At the door, a small brass bell hung beneath a sign: RING ONCE. WE HEARD YOU.

Ninja rang once.

A moment later she heard footsteps, the scrape of a bolt, and a woman calling, “Coming! Unless you're selling windows, in which case I've died!”

The door opened on a woman in her sixties wearing a wool cardigan and the expression of someone who had already formed an opinion.

“You're the booking.”

“I am,” Ninja said. “Although I did consider becoming a permanent resident of the roundabout.”

“Ah. The fountain?”

“Twice.”

“Only twice? You're practically local.”

The woman introduced herself as Ada, owner of Briar House, occasional baker, and apparently unofficial keeper of the village's entire social history.

She led Ninja upstairs past framed landscape photographs and a staircase that complained beneath every step.

“Breakfast is downstairs at eight,” Ada said. “Unless you sleep in. Then it's at nine. Unless I sleep in, in which case we negotiate.”

“Sounds fair.”

“There's hot water, mostly. The radiator knocks when the wind changes. That's normal.”

“Is there anything that isn't normal?”

Ada considered.

“The mayor's brother.”

Ninja laughed before she could stop herself.

Her room was small and warm, with a brass bed, a thick quilt, a writing desk, and a window looking across rooftops toward the mountains. A kettle sat beside two mismatched cups. Someone had left a sprig of rosemary in a narrow vase.

For a moment, standing there with her suitcase still in her hand, Ninja felt the peculiar loneliness of arriving somewhere nobody expected anything from her.

No work calls. No letters demanding explanations. No one asking whether she'd heard anything more about the investigation.

She could be a stranger for a few days.

Ada put the room key on the desk.

“There's a map downstairs. Don't trust it too much. They changed the bridge footpath three years ago and nobody's told the printer.”

“Of course they haven't.”

“And if you get hungry, The Crooked Lantern serves supper. Tell Maeve I sent you.”

“Will that get me a discount?”

“Absolutely not. But she'll know you're not a menace.”

Ninja raised an eyebrow.

Ada looked at her pink hair and dark coat, then smiled.

“Well. Not a dangerous one, anyway.”

After the door closed, Ninja sat on the edge of the bed and listened to the rain. She took out her phone, opened her messages, and stopped at Elise's name.

She hadn't meant to.

Her thumb hovered over the conversation. For a second she imagined sending a picture of the room, followed by a complaint about the staircase.

Elise would have replied that the house was haunted, and that Ninja should befriend the ghost because it probably had better directions.

Ninja smiled at the thought.

Then she placed the phone face down.

“Okay,” she whispered to the quiet room. “I'm here.”

Outside, the mountains disappeared behind a low band of cloud.

### Chapter Two — A Village with Opinions

An hour later, the rain had softened into mist.

Ninja pulled on her coat and went exploring with a vague plan involving coffee, a walk, and not accidentally joining a local committee.

Bellwether's main street curved around the fountain, then divided into three lanes that seemed to have been designed by people who disliked one another.

There was a grocery shop with crates of apples outside, a tiny bookshop whose window displayed more handwritten notes than books, and a florist with a sleepy dog sprawled across the entrance.

Ninja crouched beside the dog.

“Hello, gorgeous. Are you employed here?”

The dog opened one eye, decided she was acceptable, and returned to sleep.

A voice from inside the shop said, “That's Inspector.”

“Inspector?”

“He supervises everyone. Doesn't do any work.”

“Management material,” Ninja said.

The florist laughed.

At the bookshop, she bought a second-hand paperback because the cover featured a dramatic woman running across a moor in a dress wholly unsuitable for running across a moor.

The bookseller, a narrow man with round spectacles, studied her purchase.

“That one's terrible.”

“Excellent recommendation.”

“The ending makes no sense.”

“Now I absolutely have to read it.”

He wrapped it in brown paper and pointed her toward a café beside the old bridge.

The café was called The Narrow Cup. Its owner had filled the windows with plants and apparently surrendered the remaining space to mismatched furniture.

Ninja ordered coffee and a cinnamon bun large enough to constitute a legal threat.

She took a table beside the window.

Across the street, two older men argued over whether the village clock was seven minutes fast or eight minutes slow. Neither appeared to consider the possibility that both were wrong.

She watched a woman cross the square carrying a violin case and a bag of potatoes. A delivery driver reversed three times to avoid a cat that was clearly enjoying the power.

It was all gloriously ordinary.

And ordinary, Ninja discovered, could feel unfamiliar when you'd spent years waiting for the next terrible letter.

She opened her paperback and read three pages before realising she had been staring at the same sentence.

Her phone buzzed with a work-related notification.

Her stomach tightened on instinct.

She checked it. Nothing urgent. A routine reminder she could deal with after the trip.

Still, the familiar pressure remained behind her ribs, as though the world had reached across the mountains to remind her that peace came with conditions.

Ninja set the phone down.

The café owner appeared beside her table with a small glass of water.

“First day here?” the woman asked.

“Is it that obvious?”

“You're looking at the square like it's a television programme.”

“It's a pretty good one. The cat's my favourite character.”

“That's Mayor. Not officially. He just believes it.”

Ninja laughed.

The woman nodded toward the window.

“Walk up to the old overlook before sunset. Best view in the village. Unless the clouds are in a mood.”

“Are they usually?”

“Constantly.”

Ninja thanked her, finished her coffee, and tucked the paperback under her arm.

As she left, she realised she hadn't thought about the warehouse for nearly twenty minutes.

The realisation hurt a little.

Then she let herself be pleased anyway.

### Chapter Three — The Workshop at the Edge of Town

At the forestry workshop, Hex was trying to convince a stubborn engine that their disagreement did not need to become personal.

The engine disagreed.

He leaned over the open bonnet, sleeves rolled to his elbows, dark hair tied back loosely enough that strands kept escaping around his face.

“Come on,” he muttered.

Behind him, Tomas made a thoughtful noise.

“Have you tried being polite?”

“I've tried everything else.”

“Could threaten to replace it.”

“That's your management philosophy?”

“Worked on you.”

Hex turned his head.

Tomas grinned over the rim of his mug.

They'd spent the morning repairing a vehicle belonging to a forestry crew. The owner had described the problem as a little rattle, which had turned out to mean a fractured bracket, two loose belts, and a noise resembling a cupboard full of angry cutlery.

Hex tightened the final fitting and stepped back.

“Try it.”

Tomas turned the key.

The engine started cleanly.

“See?” Tomas said. “Politeness.”

Hex wiped his hands on a rag. “You should write a manual.”

“People would buy it.”

“Only to use as firewood.”

The workshop telephone rang.

Tomas answered, listened, and made the weary expression he reserved for requests that began with just a quick favour.

“Yes, Maeve. Yes, I remember. No, I haven't forgotten.”

Hex looked up.

Tomas covered the receiver.

“The Lantern's kitchen extractor is making that noise again.”

“Which noise?”

“The one that apparently sounds like a ghost swallowing cutlery.”

“Helpful diagnosis.”

Tomas returned to the call. “He'll take a look after closing.”

Hex stared at him.

Tomas smiled innocently.

When the call ended, Hex said, “I don't recall volunteering.”

“You were about to.”

“Was I?”

“Eventually. Once you finished pretending you weren't.”

Hex shook his head, but he didn't argue.

The Crooked Lantern was one of the few places in Bellwether he visited without automatically planning an exit. Maeve, its owner, had long ago decided that Elias Ward was quiet rather than unfriendly. She kept his preferred table near the side wall and never asked questions about his past.

That suited him.

Still, the message from the encrypted channel sat folded in his coat pocket.

N-17.

Relationship to deceased courier: sibling.

He had read it so many times the words seemed engraved on the back of his eyes.

He thought about the photograph beneath his floorboard. The woman smiling outside a café. He had kept it because throwing it away felt wrong, and looking at it felt worse.

Tomas set down his mug.

“You're miles away again.”

“Thinking.”

“Dangerous habit.”

Hex tried a smile.

Tomas watched him for a moment, then said, more quietly, “Whatever's going on, don't spend the whole evening alone with it.”

Hex looked at the open workshop door, where the mist drifted through the yard.

“I have to fix Maeve's extractor, remember?”

“That's a start.”

### Chapter Four — The Overlook

The path to the old overlook began behind the church and climbed through a stand of beech trees.

Ninja followed the signs until the signs stopped, which felt entirely consistent with everything else she'd learned about Bellwether.

The path was muddy but manageable. Wet leaves shone beneath her boots, and the air smelled of earth, rain, and woodsmoke carried up from the village.

Halfway up, she stopped to catch her breath.

Not because the hill was especially steep, she told herself, but because the trees were very interesting and deserved her full attention.

A robin landed on a fence post a few feet away.

“Don't judge me,” she told it.

The robin offered no reassurance.

At the top, a low stone wall marked the edge of the overlook. Beyond it, Bellwether spread across the valley like a handful of houses carefully placed between the hills. Smoke curled from chimneys. The narrow bridge crossed a stream that glittered through the trees. Farther away, the mountains rose in overlapping shades of blue-grey.

Ninja leaned against the wall.

For the first time since arriving, she was completely still.

She took a photograph, then another, trying to capture the way the clouds opened briefly over the distant ridge.

She wished Elise could see it.

The thought came without warning, but not with the sharpness it once had. More like a hand resting gently on an old bruise.

“Elise,” she said under her breath, “you would have complained about the walk all the way up.”

And Elise would have done it theatrically, stopping every fifty steps to announce that her younger sister was trying to murder her for the sake of a photograph.

Ninja laughed softly.

She imagined the two of them standing there, Elise pretending not to be impressed.

The empty space beside her remained empty.

But the view was still beautiful.

After a while, Ninja took her phone out and opened the saved voice message.

Hello, menace. I'm outside your building. I have food.

She listened only once.

Then she slipped the phone into her pocket, pulled her coat tighter, and watched the last sunlight move across the rooftops.

A small part of her had been afraid that enjoying the trip would mean leaving Elise behind.

Standing there, she understood something different.

Elise had loved her. She would have wanted her to go places, eat enormous cinnamon buns, get lost in ridiculous villages, and have stories to tell.

Ninja brushed moisture from her cheek before deciding whether it was rain.

“Fine,” she murmured. “You win. I'm having a holiday.”

Below her, the lights of The Crooked Lantern flickered on.

### Chapter Five — Supper at the Lantern

The Crooked Lantern looked as though someone had built a pub, discovered halfway through that straight lines were optional, and committed to the decision.

Its sign swung above a narrow doorway. Warm light spilled onto the cobbles. Through the windows Ninja could see a low ceiling crossed by dark beams and people sitting close around wooden tables.

She stood outside for a moment.

Going into an unfamiliar pub alone was not a tragedy. She knew that.

Her brain, however, had prepared a brief but passionate argument against it.

She ignored the argument and opened the door.

Warmth met her first. Then the smell of roasted potatoes, old wood, and something simmering with enough garlic to make its presence known from across the room.

Conversation dipped briefly as she entered, not out of hostility but because Bellwether appeared to notice everything.

A woman behind the bar looked up.

“Evening. You must be staying with Ada.”

Ninja stopped.

“Is there a village-wide announcement system?”

“Only Ada.”

“Terrifying.”

“I'm Maeve.” The woman smiled. “Find a seat. Kitchen's still open.”

Ninja chose a small table beside a bookshelf filled with abandoned paperbacks and board games missing their instructions.

A chalkboard listed the evening's food in handwriting that became progressively less patient toward the bottom.

She ordered stew and bread.

While she waited, she watched the room.

Two men played cards beneath a crooked photograph of the village in winter. A young couple shared chips near the fireplace. At the bar, a man in a green jacket told a story so elaborate that even the people who'd clearly heard it before leaned in for the ending.

Ninja found herself smiling.

Maeve brought the stew in a heavy ceramic bowl.

“First time in Bellwether?”

“Yes.”

“Good choice?”

“Ask me after I've survived the roads.”

“Ah. The fountain.”

“Does everyone know about the fountain?”

“Everyone who's ever arrived here.”

The stew was excellent. Ninja ate slowly, listening to the crackle of the fire and the soft, overlapping conversations.

At one point, a little girl at a nearby table dropped a wooden toy beneath Ninja's chair.

Ninja retrieved it and handed it back.

“Your dragon escaped.”

“He does that,” the girl said gravely.

“Sounds like a troublemaker.”

“He's a very good dragon.”

“Of course. My mistake.”

The girl's father thanked her, and the child spent the next five minutes explaining, with extraordinary seriousness, the dragon's entire family history.

Ninja listened as though receiving a classified briefing.

It felt good to be useful in a way that didn't demand anything from her.

When the family left, she ordered tea.

The pub had grown busier. Rain tapped the windows again, gentle at first and then harder.

Maeve glanced toward the door.

“Elias is late.”

“Who's Elias?” asked one of the card players.

“The only person in this village who can persuade that extractor to behave.”

“Have you tried asking it nicely?” someone called.

Maeve rolled her eyes.

Ninja looked up from her cup only because the name was unfamiliar. It meant nothing to her.

Not yet.

### Chapter Six — The Man Who Almost Stayed Home

Across the village, Hex stood in his rented kitchen staring at the tin box on the table.

He had taken it from beneath the floorboard without entirely deciding why.

Inside lay the photograph, its corners softened from years of handling, and Elise's damaged phone, long dead.

He hadn't looked at either for weeks.

Tonight he picked up the photograph.

Ninja was laughing at something outside the frame. Whoever had taken the picture had caught her in an unguarded moment, head turned slightly, expression alive with mischief.

He wondered what she sounded like.

The question startled him.

For five years he had thought of her as a responsibility, a consequence, a person he had failed without ever meeting. It was easier to think in those terms than to imagine the life she actually lived.

He set the photograph down.

Mara's voice came back to him from the motel years ago.

Walk away from this. You can still have a life.

He had walked away, but the life he'd built was made of careful absences. No photographs on the walls. No old friends. No questions he couldn't answer.

Tomas was the closest thing he had to family now, though neither of them had ever said so.

Hex folded the report and returned it to the tin.

Then he put on his coat.

The extractor at The Crooked Lantern needed fixing.

That was a normal problem. A solvable problem. Something that would not require him to decide whether he owed a stranger the truth about her sister's death.

He locked the door and headed into the rain.

Halfway down the street, his phone vibrated.

An unknown number.

He stopped beneath the awning of the closed grocer's shop.

The screen displayed a single message.

YOU SHOULD HAVE STAYED GONE.

Hex read it twice.

No sender. No explanation.

The rain sounded suddenly louder.

He checked the street behind him. Empty, except for the reflected glow of the lamps in puddles.

It might have been a cruel joke. A wrong number. Someone who had found an old contact.

He knew better than to believe that without evidence.

He deleted the message, then regretted deleting it.

For a moment he considered going home.

Instead he looked toward the pub, where warm light showed through the rain.

He had spent five years letting the Directorate decide how much of his life he was allowed to have.

“Not tonight,” he said quietly.

And he kept walking.

### Chapter Seven — The Crooked Door

Inside the pub, Ninja had reached the point where she was contemplating a second dessert despite being completely full.

The little girl with the toy dragon had left behind a paper napkin covered in drawings. Maeve picked it up and showed Ninja.

“Apparently you've been appointed guardian of the dragon.”

Ninja studied the scribbles.

“That's a serious responsibility.”

“I'll have to warn Ada.”

“Please don't. She already thinks I'm a menace.”

Maeve laughed and returned to the bar.

A gust of cold air pushed through the room as the door opened.

Ninja didn't turn immediately. She was busy trying to work out whether the dessert menu's promise of a modest portion was legally enforceable.

Across the threshold stepped a tall man in a dark coat, rain glistening in his long hair. He paused just inside to shake water from his sleeves.

Maeve looked up.

“Elias! I was about to send a search party.”

“Would you have paid them?”

“Absolutely not.”

“Then I'm surprised anyone volunteers.”

He spoke quietly, with the dry amusement of someone who preferred to let other people do most of the talking.

Ninja glanced toward the bar.

She saw his profile first: the dark beard, the tired eyes, the line of an eyebrow piercing catching the lamplight. Tattoos disappeared beneath his collar.

He was speaking to Maeve, not looking around the room.

There was nothing dramatic about it.

No thunderclap. No sudden silence. No sense that the universe had rearranged itself.

Just a stranger arriving at a pub on a rainy evening.

Ninja looked back at her menu.

Across the room, Hex accepted a toolbox from Maeve and headed toward the narrow passage beside the kitchen.

He never saw Ninja's face.

Not yet.

The extractor gave a mournful metallic screech.

From the passage, Hex called, “Is that the ghost?”

Maeve shouted back, “That's the ghost.”

Ninja laughed into her tea.

A few minutes later, the noise stopped.

The room applauded.

Hex reappeared, wiping his hands with a cloth, looking faintly embarrassed by the attention.

Tomas, who had arrived while he was working, raised his glass.

“Our village hero.”

“It's a fan belt, Tomas.”

“A modest hero.”

Hex shook his head and took the seat Maeve indicated at the far end of the bar.

He was tired. The strange message still sat in the back of his mind. He told himself he would stay for one drink and leave.

Ninja finished her tea and stood to pay.

At the bar, Maeve was helping another customer. Ninja waited, looking at the photographs behind the bottles.

One showed Bellwether beneath deep snow. Another showed a younger Tomas standing beside a tractor with a triumphant expression.

She heard a man beside her say, “You should charge more for the extractor repairs.”

Maeve answered, “And ruin my reputation for taking advantage of you?”

Ninja smiled at the exchange.

Hex was standing a few steps away, reaching for his coat.

For one small moment they occupied the same patch of warm light, separated by a stool, a stack of clean glasses, and the fact that neither had looked directly at the other.

Maeve took Ninja's payment.

“Did you enjoy the stew?”

“Very much. I might come back tomorrow.”

“Do. We need someone who appreciates the food without giving me an engineering report.”

From behind her, Hex said, “The engineering reports are complimentary.”

Ninja laughed and turned halfway toward the voice.

But at that exact moment, the front door opened and a cluster of wet hikers came in, blocking the space between them.

Someone called for Maeve. Tomas asked Hex a question. Ninja stepped aside to let the hikers through.

The moment passed.

She pulled up her hood and left.

Outside, the rain had become a fine silver mist.

Ninja walked back toward Briar House with the smell of woodsmoke in her coat and the pleasant ache of a day spent doing things she hadn't needed to do.

She was thinking about the cinnamon bun, the ridiculous book, and whether the dog named Inspector might permit another visit tomorrow.

Inside the pub, Hex sat down again.

Maeve set a glass in front of him.

“New guest at Ada's,” she said casually. “Seems nice.”

“Good for Ada.”

“Pink hair. Funny. Got lost at the fountain twice.”

Hex's hand stopped against the glass.

He looked toward the door.

“Pink hair?”

Maeve was already moving away.

“Bright pink. Can't miss her.”

Hex stood.

Tomas looked up. “Where are you going?”

Hex didn't answer immediately.

Outside the window, a hooded figure turned the corner and disappeared into the mist.

He hadn't seen her face. He had no reason to believe that every woman with pink hair was the one in the photograph.

But his heart was beating too fast.

He sat back down, slowly.

Tomas watched him with a troubled expression.

“You all right?”

Hex stared at the doorway.

“No,” he said.

And this time, he didn't pretend otherwise.
